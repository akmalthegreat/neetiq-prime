9.1 How to analyse chemical composition

All living organisms, from bacteria to humans, are built from the same kinds of chemicals. To find out what they are, NCERT describes a simple analysis.

Organic compounds: the grinding method

  • Living tissue (a vegetable or a piece of liver) is ground in trichloroacetic acid (Cl₃CCOOH) using a mortar and pestle to get a thick slurry.
  • The slurry is strained through cheesecloth or cotton, giving two fractions:
Tissue + Cl₃CCOOH→Grind → slurry→Strain (cheesecloth)→Filtrate = acid-soluble pool+Retentate = acid-insoluble fraction

Filtrate

  • Acid-soluble pool
  • Thousands of small organic compounds
  • MW 18 to about 800 Da

Retentate

  • Acid-insoluble fraction
  • Proteins, nucleic acids, polysaccharides, lipids
  • Mostly macromolecules

Each separated compound is then isolated and purified, and analytical techniques give its molecular formula and probable structure. All carbon compounds obtained from living tissues are called biomolecules.

Inorganic elements and compounds: the ash

  • A small amount of tissue is weighed (wet weight) and dried; all water evaporates and the rest is the dry weight. Fully burning the tissue oxidises all carbon compounds to gases (CO₂, water vapour), leaving the ash.
  • The ash contains inorganic elements such as calcium, magnesium etc., and inorganic compounds such as sulphate, phosphate etc.
  • Elemental analysis gives composition in terms of H, O, Cl, C etc.; compound analysis tells what kind of organic and inorganic constituents are present.

Amino acids

Definition Amino acids are organic compounds that carry an amino group and an acidic (carboxyl) group as substituents on the same carbon, the α-carbon — hence called α-amino acids.
  • They are substituted methanes: the α-carbon carries four substituents — hydrogen, carboxyl group, amino group and a variable R group.
  • Many amino acids exist depending on the R group, but those occurring in proteins are only of twenty types.
  • Chemical and physical properties depend on the amino, carboxyl and R functional groups.
R groupAmino acid
Hydrogen (–H)Glycine

Because –NH₂ and –COOH are ionizable, the structure of an amino acid changes with the pH of the solution. The form carrying both a positive and a negative charge is the zwitterionic form.

Lipids

Lipids are generally water insoluble. They may be simple fatty acids or more complex molecules.

  • Fatty acid = a carboxyl group attached to an R group. R may be a methyl (–CH₃), ethyl (–C₂H₅) or a higher number of –CH₂ groups (1 to 19 carbons).
  • Palmitic acid has 16 carbons including the carboxyl carbon; arachidonic acid has 20 carbons including the carboxyl carbon.
  • Saturated fatty acids have no double bond; unsaturated ones have one or more C=C double bonds.
  • Glycerol is a simple lipid = trihydroxy propane.
  • Fatty acids esterified with glycerol give monoglycerides, diglycerides and triglycerides.

Fats

  • Higher melting point
  • Solid at lower temperatures

Oils

  • Lower melting point (e.g. gingelly oil), so they stay oil (liquid) even in winter
  • Lipids containing phosphorus and a phosphorylated organic compound are phospholipids; they occur in the cell membrane. Lecithin is one example.
  • Some tissues, especially neural tissues, have lipids with more complex structures.
Trap Carbon count of a fatty acid in NCERT includes the carboxyl carbon: palmitic = 16 C, arachidonic = 20 C. Glycerol is itself counted as a lipid.

Nitrogen bases, nucleosides and nucleotides

  • Living organisms contain carbon compounds with heterocyclic rings: the nitrogen bases adenine, guanine, cytosine, uracil and thymine.
  • Base + sugar = nucleoside. Base + sugar + phosphate (esterified to the sugar) = nucleotide.
  • Nucleic acids such as DNA and RNA consist of nucleotides only; DNA and RNA function as genetic material.

9.2 Primary and secondary metabolites

The most abundant chemical in living organisms is water. Beyond it lie thousands of small and large compounds, called metabolites in biochemistry.

Primary metabolites

  • Found in animal tissues (and all organisms)
  • Have identifiable functions and known roles in normal physiology
  • e.g. amino acids, sugars

Secondary metabolites

  • Seen in plant, fungal and microbial cells
  • Roles or functions in the host are not all understood at present
  • e.g. alkaloids, flavonoids, rubber, essential oils, antibiotics, coloured pigments, scents, gums, spices

9.3 Biomacromolecules

  • Acid-soluble pool compounds have molecular weights from 18 to around 800 daltons (Da).
  • The acid-insoluble fraction has only four types of organic compounds: proteins, nucleic acids, polysaccharides and lipids.
  • Except lipids, these have molecular weights of ten thousand daltons and above.

Micromolecules

  • MW less than 1000 Da
  • Found in acid-soluble pool
  • Amino acids, sugars, nucleotides etc.

Macromolecules (biomacromolecules)

  • Found in acid-insoluble fraction
  • Polymeric; MW ≥ 10,000 Da
  • Proteins, nucleic acids, polysaccharides
Exception: lipids Lipids have molecular weights that do not exceed 800 Da, yet they come in the acid-insoluble fraction. Reason: lipids are arranged into cell membrane and other membranes. On grinding, membranes break into pieces and form water-insoluble vesicles, which separate with the acid-insoluble pool. So lipids are not strictly macromolecules.
Key idea The acid-soluble pool roughly represents the cytoplasmic composition. Macromolecules from the cytoplasm and organelles form the acid-insoluble fraction. Together, both fractions represent the entire chemical composition of living tissue.

9.4 Proteins

Definition Proteins are polypeptides — linear chains of amino acids linked by peptide bonds.
  • Each protein is a polymer of amino acids. As there are 20 types of amino acids (e.g. alanine, cysteine, proline, tryptophan, lysine), a protein is a heteropolymer, not a homopolymer.
  • A homopolymer has only one type of monomer repeating "n" times.

Essential amino acids

  • Cannot be made by the body
  • Obtained through diet/food
  • Dietary proteins are their source

Non-essential amino acids

  • Our body can make them

Functions of proteins

Some transport nutrients across the cell membrane, some fight infectious organisms, some are hormones, some are enzymes, and so on.

InsulinHormone
AntibodyFights infectious agents

  • Ribulose bisphosphate Carboxylase-Oxygenase (RuBisCO) is the most abundant protein in the whole of the biosphere.
Trap "Most abundant protein" — check the scope: animal world → collagen; biosphere → RuBisCO. GLUT-4 is a transporter, not a hormone.

9.7 Structure of proteins

Proteins are heteropolymers of amino acids. Their structure is described at four levels.

LevelWhat it describes
PrimaryThe sequence of amino acids — positional information: which amino acid is first, second and so on.
SecondaryThe thread is not an extended rigid rod; some portions are folded as a helix, other regions into other forms.
TertiaryThe long chain folds upon itself like a hollow woollen ball, giving a 3-dimensional view.
QuaternaryArrangement of two or more folded polypeptides (subunits) with respect to each other — e.g. a linear string of spheres, spheres stacked as a cube or plate.
  • Imagine the protein as a line: the left end carries the first amino acid = N-terminal amino acid; the right end carries the last amino acid = C-terminal amino acid.
  • In proteins, only right-handed helices are observed.
  • Tertiary structure is absolutely necessary for many biological activities of proteins.
NEET Highlight Adult human haemoglobin has 4 subunits: two α and two β (α₂β₂) — an example of quaternary structure.
Trap
  • Only some portions of a protein are helical — not the whole chain.
  • Quaternary structure exists only in proteins with more than one polypeptide.
  • Left = N-terminal, right = C-terminal (not the other way).

9.8 Enzymes

  • Almost all enzymes are proteins. Some nucleic acids behave like enzymes — these are called ribozymes.
  • Like any protein, an enzyme has primary (amino acid sequence), secondary and tertiary structure.
  • In the tertiary structure, the backbone folds upon itself and criss-crosses, creating crevices or pockets. One such pocket is the active site — a crevice or pocket into which the substrate fits.
  • Through the active site, enzymes catalyse reactions at a high rate.

Inorganic catalysts

  • Work efficiently at high temperature and high pressure

Enzymes

  • Get damaged at high temperatures (say above 40°C)
  • Protein catalysts, specific active site
Exception: thermostable enzymes Enzymes isolated from organisms living at extremely high temperatures (e.g. hot vents and sulphur springs) are stable and keep their catalytic power even at high temperatures (up to 80°–90°C). Thermal stability is thus an important quality of such enzymes.

9.8.1 Chemical reactions

Physical change

  • Change in shape without breaking bonds
  • Change in state of matter: ice → water, water → vapour

Chemical reaction

  • Bonds are broken and new bonds formed
  • Inorganic: Ba(OH)₂ + H₂SO₄ → BaSO₄ + 2H₂O
  • Organic: hydrolysis of starch into glucose

Rate of reaction

  • Rate = amount of product formed per unit time: rate = δP/δt. Rate is called velocity if direction is specified.
  • Rate doubles or decreases by half for every 10°C change in either direction.
  • Catalysed reactions proceed at rates vastly higher than uncatalysed ones; enzyme-catalysed ones are very high.

Carbonic anhydrase — the NCERT example

CO₂ + H₂O⇌H₂CO₃(carbonic anhydrase)

With carbonic anhydrase (in cytoplasm)about 600,000 molecules every second
NEET Highlight Carbonic anhydrase accelerates the reaction rate by about 10 million times. The power of enzymes is incredible.

Metabolic pathways

Thousands of enzymes each catalyse a unique reaction. A metabolic pathway is a multistep chemical reaction series where each step is catalysed by the same enzyme complex or different enzymes.

Glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆)→10 enzyme-catalysed steps→2 Pyruvic acid (C₃H₄O₃)

Overall: C₆H₁₂O₆ + O₂ → 2C₃H₄O₃ + 2H₂O. The conversion of glucose to pyruvic acid occurs through ten different enzyme-catalysed reactions.

ConditionFate
Anaerobic, in skeletal musclePyruvic acid → lactic acid
Normal aerobic conditionsGlucose → pyruvic acid (end product)
Yeast, during fermentationGlucose → ethanol (alcohol)
Key idea The same starting molecule gives different products under different conditions, because different enzymes act.

9.8.2 How do enzymes bring about such high rates?

  • The chemical that is converted is the substrate (S); enzymes (E) convert it into product (P): S → P.
  • The substrate diffuses towards and binds the enzyme at its active site (a cleft or pocket).
  • There is an obligatory formation of an ES complex. It is short-lived and dissociates into product(s) and the unchanged enzyme, via an intermediate enzyme-product (EP) complex.
E + S⇌ES→EP→E + P
Transition state While the substrate is bound to the active site, a new structure of the substrate called the transition state structure is formed. Once bond breaking/making is complete, the product is released. All intermediate structural states between S and P are unstable; stability relates to energy status.
  • If P is at a lower energy level than S, the reaction is exothermic; no energy (heating) need be supplied to form the product.
  • Whether exothermic/spontaneous or endothermic/energy-requiring, S must still pass through a much higher energy state — the transition state.
Activation energy The difference in average energy content of S from that of the transition state is called activation energy. Enzymes bring down this energy barrier, making the transition of S to P easier.
Trap Enzymes lower the activation energy only; they do not change the energy levels of S or P (so they do not make an endothermic reaction exothermic). The enzyme comes out unchanged.
NCERT diagrams
  • Concept of activation energy — labels to know: substrate (S), product (P), transition state, activation energy with and without enzyme, potential energy (y-axis), progress of structural transformation (x-axis); asked as: what the enzyme lowers / exothermic or not.

9.8.3 Nature of enzyme action

Each enzyme has a substrate-binding site, so that a highly reactive ES complex is produced; it is short-lived and dissociates into P and unchanged E. The catalytic cycle runs in four steps:

  1. The substrate binds to the active site, fitting into it.
  2. Binding of substrate induces the enzyme to alter its shape, fitting more tightly around the substrate.
  3. The active site, now in close proximity to the substrate, breaks chemical bonds of the substrate and a new enzyme-product complex forms.
  4. The enzyme releases the products; the free enzyme is ready to bind another substrate molecule and run the cycle again.
Key idea Step 2 is the induced fit: the enzyme is not a rigid lock — substrate binding changes the enzyme's shape so it grips the substrate more tightly. Because the enzyme comes out unchanged, a small amount of enzyme can convert a very large number of substrate molecules.
Trap The ES complex is short-lived and its formation is obligatory (essential) for catalysis. The sequence is ES → EP → E + P, not ES → E + P directly.
Memory trick "Bind – Bend – Break – Bye": substrate binds, enzyme bends (induced fit), bonds break, products say bye.

9.8.4 Factors affecting enzyme activity

Activity changes with any condition that alters the tertiary structure of the protein: temperature, pH, substrate concentration, or binding of specific chemicals that regulate activity.

Temperature and pH

  • Enzymes work in a narrow range of temperature and pH. Each shows highest activity at a particular optimum temperature and optimum pH; activity declines both below and above the optimum.
  • Low temperature preserves the enzyme in a temporarily inactive state; high temperature destroys activity because proteins are denatured by heat.
Trap Cold = reversible inactivation (enzyme preserved); heat = irreversible loss (denaturation). That is why the temperature curve falls sharply after the optimum.

Substrate concentration

  • With rising substrate concentration, velocity first rises.
  • It finally reaches a maximum velocity (Vmax), which is not exceeded by any further rise in substrate concentration.
  • Reason: enzyme molecules are fewer than substrate molecules; after they are saturated, no free enzyme is left to bind additional substrate.

Inhibition

Definition When binding of a chemical shuts off enzyme activity, the process is inhibition and the chemical is an inhibitor. An inhibitor that closely resembles the substrate in molecular structure is a competitive inhibitor.
  • Due to structural similarity it competes with the substrate for the substrate-binding site; the substrate cannot bind, so enzyme action declines.
  • Example: malonate inhibits succinic dehydrogenase because it closely resembles the substrate succinate.
  • Competitive inhibitors are often used in the control of bacterial pathogens.
EnzymeSubstrateCompetitive inhibitor
Succinic dehydrogenaseSuccinateMalonate
Memory trick Malonate = "mal" (bad) look-alike of Succinate — it sits in succinic dehydrogenase's seat.

9.8.5 Classification and nomenclature of enzymes

Enzymes are divided into 6 classes, each with 4–13 subclasses, and named by a four-digit number, based on the type of reaction they catalyse.

ClassReaction catalysedGeneral form
1. Oxidoreductases / dehydrogenasesOxidoreduction between two substrates S and S′S reduced + S′ oxidised → S oxidised + S′ reduced
2. TransferasesTransfer of a group G (other than hydrogen) between a pair of substratesS – G + S′ → S + S′ – G
3. HydrolasesHydrolysis of ester, ether, peptide, glycosidic, C–C, C–halide or P–N bonds—
4. LyasesRemoval of groups by mechanisms other than hydrolysis, leaving double bondsX–C–C–Y → X–Y + C=C
5. IsomerasesInter-conversion of optical, geometric or positional isomers—
6. LigasesLinking together of 2 compounds, e.g. joining C–O, C–S, C–N, P–O bonds—
Memory trick NCERT order O-T-H-L-I-L: "Oh The Hungry Lion Is Lazy" — Oxidoreductases, Transferases, Hydrolases, Lyases, Isomerases, Ligases.
Trap
  • Transfer of hydrogen is not a transferase job — that is oxidoreduction (dehydrogenases).
  • Lyase removes groups without water and leaves a double bond; ligase joins two molecules. Similar names, opposite jobs.
  • Breaking a peptide or glycosidic bond with water = hydrolase.

9.8.6 Co-factors

Enzymes are made of one or several polypeptide chains. In many cases a non-protein constituent, the co-factor, is bound to the enzyme to make it catalytically active. The protein portion is then called the apoenzyme.

Apoenzyme (protein)+Co-factor (non-protein)→Catalytically active enzyme

Prosthetic groupOrganic; tightly bound to the apoenzymeHaem in peroxidase and catalase (break H₂O₂ into water and oxygen); haem is part of the active site
