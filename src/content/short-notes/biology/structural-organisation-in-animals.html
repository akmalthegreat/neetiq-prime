7.1 From cells to organ systems

  • In unicellular organisms, all functions like digestion, respiration and reproduction are performed by a single cell.
  • In the complex body of multicellular animals, the same basic functions are carried out by different groups of cells in a well-organised manner.
  • Example: the body of a simple organism like Hydra is made of different types of cells, and the number of cells in each type can be in thousands.
  • The human body is composed of billions of cells performing various functions.
DefinitionTissue — a group of similar cells along with intercellular substances that performs a specific function.
Cells→Tissues→Organs→Organ systems
  • Organs (stomach, lung, heart, kidney) are formed when tissues are organised in specific proportion and pattern.
  • When two or more organs perform a common function by physical and/or chemical interaction, they form an organ system (e.g. digestive system, respiratory system).
  • Cells, tissues, organs and organ systems split up the work so as to show division of labour and contribute to the survival of the body as a whole.
Key ideaAnimal tissues are broadly classified into four types: epithelial, connective, muscular and neural. Cell structure varies according to function, so tissues differ.
TissueCore jobOne-line identity
EpithelialCovering / liningCompact cells, very little matrix, free surface
ConnectiveLinking & supportingMost abundant; cells scattered in matrix with fibres
MuscularMovementLong fibres of myofibrils that contract and relax
NeuralControl & coordinationExcitable neurons + supporting neuroglia
Memory trick“Every Cell Must Network” — Epithelial, Connective, Muscular, Neural.

7.2 Epithelial tissue — simple epithelium

Commonly called epithelium (plural: epithelia). Epithelial tissue has a free surface, which faces either a body fluid or the outside environment, and thus provides a covering or a lining for some part of the body.

  • Cells are compactly packed with little intercellular matrix.
  • They rest on a non-cellular basement membrane (seen as the base line in NCERT figures).
  • Two types: simple and compound epithelium.

Simple epithelium

  • Single layer of cells
  • Functions as a lining for body cavities, ducts and tubes

Compound epithelium

  • Two or more cell layers
  • Has a protective function, as it does in our skin

Three types of simple epithelium (by structural modification of cells)

TypeCell shapeFound inFunction
SquamousSingle thin layer of flattened cells with irregular boundariesWalls of blood vessels and air sacs of lungsForming a diffusion boundary
CuboidalSingle layer of cube-like cellsDucts of glands and tubular parts of nephrons in kidneysSecretion and absorption
ColumnarSingle layer of tall and slender cells; nuclei at the base; free surface may have microvilliLining of stomach and intestineSecretion and absorption
  • The epithelium of the proximal convoluted tubule (PCT) of the nephron in the kidney has microvilli. (PCT epithelium is cuboidal — microvilli are not limited to columnar cells.)
  • If the columnar or cuboidal cells bear cilia on their free surface, they are called ciliated epithelium.
  • Function of ciliated epithelium: to move particles or mucus in a specific direction over the epithelium.
  • Found mainly in the inner surface of hollow organs like bronchioles and fallopian tubes.
TrapAir sacs (alveoli) and blood vessel walls → squamous, not cuboidal. Nephron tubules and gland ducts → cuboidal. Stomach & intestine lining → columnar. Bronchioles & fallopian tubes → ciliated. NEET loves “match the epithelium to its location”.
ExceptionCiliated epithelium is not a separate cell shape: it is columnar or cuboidal epithelium that carries cilia.
Memory trick“Squash Air, Cube Kidney, Column Gut, Cilia Tube” — squamous–air sacs, cuboidal–nephron, columnar–gut lining, ciliated–fallopian tube/bronchioles.

7.3 Glandular epithelium, compound epithelium and cell junctions

Glandular epithelium

Some of the columnar or cuboidal cells get specialised for secretion and are called glandular epithelium.

Unicellular gland

  • Isolated glandular cells
  • e.g. goblet cells of the alimentary canal

Multicellular gland

  • Cluster of cells
  • e.g. salivary gland

On the basis of the mode of pouring of their secretions, glands are of two types:

FeatureExocrine glandsEndocrine glands
DuctsPresent — products released through ducts or tubesAbsent (ductless)
SecretionsMucus, saliva, earwax, oil, milk, digestive enzymes and other cell productsHormones
Where releasedThrough ducts to a surface/cavityDirectly into the fluid bathing the gland
Memory trickExocrine products — “My Sister Eats Oily Mangoes Daily”: Mucus, Saliva, Earwax, Oil, Milk, Digestive enzymes.

Compound epithelium

  • Made of more than one layer (multi-layered) of cells.
  • It has a limited role in secretion and absorption. Its main function is to provide protection against chemical and mechanical stresses.
  • Covers the dry surface of the skin, the moist surface of the buccal cavity and pharynx, the inner lining of ducts of salivary glands and of pancreatic ducts.
TrapSalivary and pancreatic ducts are lined by compound epithelium (NCERT), whereas “ducts of glands” in general are listed under cuboidal. If the question names salivary/pancreatic ducts specifically, answer compound.

Cell junctions

All cells in epithelium are held together with little intercellular material. In nearly all animal tissues, specialised junctions provide both structural and functional links between its individual cells.

JunctionFunction
Tight junctionsHelp to stop substances from leaking across a tissue
Adhering junctionsPerform cementing to keep neighbouring cells together
Gap junctionsHelp cells communicate by connecting the cytoplasm of adjoining cells, for rapid transfer of ions, small molecules and sometimes big molecules
Memory trickTight = sTop leak · Adhering = Adhesive cement · Gap = Gossip (communication).

7.4 Connective tissue — general features, loose and dense

  • Connective tissues are most abundant and widely distributed in the body of complex animals.
  • Named for their special function of linking and supporting other tissues/organs of the body.
  • They range from soft connective tissues to specialised types that include cartilage, bone, adipose and blood.
  • In all connective tissues except blood, the cells secrete fibres of structural proteins called collagen or elastin. The fibres provide strength, elasticity and flexibility to the tissue.
  • The cells also secrete modified polysaccharides, which accumulate between cells and fibres and act as matrix (ground substance).
ExceptionBlood is the only connective tissue whose cells do not secrete fibres. It is still a connective tissue (fluid connective tissue).

(i) Loose connective tissue

Cells and fibres are loosely arranged in a semi-fluid ground substance.

Areolar tissue

  • Present beneath the skin
  • Often serves as a support framework for epithelium
  • Contains fibroblasts (cells that produce and secrete fibres), macrophages and mast cells

Adipose tissue

  • Loose connective tissue located mainly beneath the skin
  • Cells are specialised to store fats
  • Excess nutrients not used immediately are converted into fats and stored here

(ii) Dense connective tissue

Fibres and fibroblasts are compactly packed. The orientation of fibres gives a regular or irregular pattern.

FeatureDense regularDense irregular
Fibre arrangementCollagen fibres in rows between many parallel bundles of fibresFibroblasts and many fibres (mostly collagen) oriented differently
ExamplesTendons and ligamentsPresent in the skin
  • Tendons attach skeletal muscles to bones; ligaments attach one bone to another.
Memory trickTendon = Muscle-to-bone (“TM”, trademark); Ligament = bone-to-bone (“Link two bones”).
TrapSkin is linked to three connective tissues: areolar and adipose lie beneath the skin, while dense irregular tissue is in the skin. Read “beneath” vs “in” carefully.

  • Dense connective tissue — (a) dense regular (parallel collagen fibres), (b) dense irregular (fibres in different directions).

7.5 Specialised connective tissue — cartilage, bone, blood

Cartilage

  • Intercellular material of cartilage is solid and pliable and resists compression.
  • Cells — chondrocytes — are enclosed in small cavities within the matrix secreted by them.
  • Most of the cartilages in vertebrate embryos are replaced by bones in adults.
  • In adults, cartilage is present in the tip of the nose, outer ear joints, between adjacent bones of the vertebral column, limbs and hands.

Bone

  • Has a hard and non-pliable ground substance rich in calcium salts and collagen fibres, which give bone its strength.
  • It is the main tissue that provides structural frame to the body.
  • Bones support and protect softer tissues and organs.
  • The bone cells (osteocytes) are present in the spaces called lacunae.
  • Limb bones, such as the long bones of the legs, serve weight-bearing functions.
  • They interact with skeletal muscles attached to them to bring about movements.
  • The bone marrow in some bones is the site of production of blood cells.

Blood

  • Blood is a fluid connective tissue containing plasma, red blood cells (RBC), white blood cells (WBC) and platelets.
  • It is the main circulating fluid that helps in the transport of various substances (details in Body Fluids and Circulation).
FeatureCartilageBoneBlood
MatrixSolid, pliable; resists compressionHard, non-pliable; calcium salts + collagenFluid — plasma
CellsChondrocytes in small cavitiesOsteocytes in lacunaeRBC, WBC, platelets
Key roleFlexible support (nose tip, outer ear, between vertebrae)Structural frame, protection, weight-bearing, movement, blood-cell production (marrow)Transport
TrapChondrocytes ↔ cartilage; osteocytes ↔ bone (lacunae). Swapping these is a classic distractor. Also: adipose is a loose connective tissue, not a specialised one, in NCERT’s three-way classification.

7.6 Muscle tissue

  • Each muscle is made of many long, cylindrical fibres arranged in parallel arrays.
  • These fibres are composed of numerous fine fibrils, called myofibrils.
  • Muscle fibres contract (shorten) and relax (lengthen) in a coordinated fashion in response to stimulation by electrical changes.
  • In general, muscles play an active role in all the movements of the body.
  • Three types: skeletal, smooth and cardiac.

Skeletal muscle

  • Closely attached to skeletal bones.
  • In a typical muscle such as the biceps, striated (striped) skeletal muscle fibres are bundled together in a parallel fashion.
  • A sheath of tough connective tissue encloses several bundles of muscle fibres.

Smooth muscle

  • Fibres taper at both ends (fusiform) and do not show striations.
  • Cell junctions hold them together; they are bundled together in a connective tissue sheath.
  • Found in the wall of internal organs such as blood vessels, stomach and intestine.
  • Smooth muscles are “involuntary” as their functioning cannot be directly controlled. We cannot make them contract merely by thinking about it, as we can with skeletal muscles.

Cardiac muscle

  • Cardiac muscle tissue is a contractile tissue present only in the heart.
  • Cell junctions fuse the plasma membranes of cardiac muscle cells and make them stick together.
  • Communication junctions (intercalated discs) at some fusion points allow the cells to contract as a unit — when one cell receives a signal to contract, its neighbours are also stimulated to contract.
FeatureSkeletalSmoothCardiac
StriationsPresent (striated)AbsentPresent
ShapeLong, cylindrical, parallel bundlesFusiform (tapering ends)Branched
ControlVoluntaryInvoluntaryInvoluntary
LocationAttached to bones (e.g. biceps)Walls of blood vessels, stomach, intestineHeart only
Special featureBundles in tough connective-tissue sheathHeld by cell junctionsIntercalated discs → contract as a unit
TrapCardiac muscle is striated but involuntary — it shares striations with skeletal muscle and involuntary action with smooth muscle. “Striated = voluntary” is false.
NEET HighlightIntercalated discs are communication junctions — they are why the heart beats as one coordinated unit. Pair this with the gap-junction function from 7.3.

7.7 Neural tissue

  • Neural tissue exerts the greatest control over the body’s responsiveness to changing conditions.
  • Neurons, the unit of neural system, are excitable cells.
  • The neuroglial cells, which constitute the rest of the neural system, protect and support the neurons.
  • Neuroglia make up more than one-half the volume of neural tissue in our body.

How a neuron works

Suitable stimulus→Electrical disturbance→Travels swiftly along plasma membrane→Arrives at endings (output zone)→Stimulates or inhibits adjacent neurons / other cells
TrapBy volume, neuroglia (not neurons) form more than half of neural tissue. Neurons are the excitable, functional units; neuroglia only protect and support.

7.8 Frog — introduction and habit

  • Frogs can live both on land and in freshwater; they belong to class Amphibia of phylum Chordata.
  • The most common species of frog found in India is Rana tigrina.
  • They do not have constant body temperature, i.e., their body temperature varies with the temperature of the environment. Such animals are called cold-blooded or poikilotherms.
  • Frogs can change colour to hide from enemies (camouflage). This protective coloration is called mimicry.
  • Frogs are not seen during peak summer and winter: they take shelter in deep burrows to protect themselves from extreme heat and cold.

Aestivation

  • Summer sleep
  • Protection from extreme heat

Hibernation

  • Winter sleep
  • Protection from extreme cold
TrapNCERT calls the frog’s protective colour change “mimicry” (in the sense of camouflage). Go with NCERT’s word in NEET. Also, the species name in NCERT is Rana tigrina — answer with this name.
Memory trickAestivation = Against heat (summer sleep); Hibernation = Hide from cold (winter sleep).

7.9 Morphology of frog

DefinitionMorphology — study of form or externally visible features (shape, size, colour, appendages, externally visible organs, etc.).

Skin

  • The skin is smooth and slippery due to the presence of mucus. It is always maintained in a moist condition.
  • Colour: dorsal side olive green with dark irregular spots; ventral side uniformly pale yellow.
  • The frog never drinks water but absorbs it through the skin.

Body plan

  • Body divisible into head and trunk. A neck and tail are absent.
  • Above the mouth: a pair of nostrils.
  • Eyes are bulged and covered by a nictitating membrane that protects the eyes while in water.
  • On either side of the eyes: a membranous tympanum (ear) that receives sound signals.
  • Forelimbs and hind limbs help in swimming, walking, leaping and burrowing.
FeatureForelimbsHind limbs
DigitsFourFive
SizeSmallerLarger and more muscular
SpecialCopulatory pad on first digit (male only)Webbed digits help in swimming

Sexual dimorphism

Frogs exhibit sexual dimorphism. Male frogs can be distinguished by the presence of sound-producing vocal sacs and also a copulatory pad on the first digit of the fore limbs. These are absent in female frogs.

Memory trick“Fore-Four, Hind-Five” — 4 digits in forelimb, 5 in hind limb. Male = “Voice + Pad” (vocal sacs + copulatory pad).
TrapWebbing is on the feet (hind limbs) for swimming; the copulatory pad is on the first digit of the forelimb, not the hind limb.

7.10 Anatomy of frog — digestive system

The body cavity of the frog accommodates the different organ systems: digestive, circulatory, respiratory, nervous, excretory and reproductive — each with well-developed structures and functions.

  • Digestive system = alimentary canal + digestive glands.
  • The alimentary canal is short because frogs are carnivores and hence the length of intestine is reduced.
Mouth→Buccal cavity→Pharynx→Oesophagus (short tube)→Stomach→Intestine→Rectum→Cloaca

Digestive glands

  • Liver secretes bile, which is stored in the gall bladder.
  • Pancreas, a digestive gland, produces pancreatic juice containing digestive enzymes.

Steps of digestion

  1. Food is captured by the bilobed tongue.
  2. In the stomach, digestion takes place by the action of HCl and gastric juices secreted from the stomach walls.
  3. Partially digested food, called chyme, passes from the stomach to the first part of the intestine, the duodenum.
  4. The duodenum receives bile from the gall bladder and pancreatic juices from the pancreas through a common bile duct.
  5. Bile emulsifies fat; pancreatic juices digest carbohydrates and proteins.
  6. Final digestion takes place in the intestine.
  7. Digested food is absorbed by the numerous finger-like folds in the inner wall of the intestine called villi and microvilli.
  8. Undigested solid waste moves into the rectum and passes out through the cloaca.
TrapBile has no enzyme role here — it only emulsifies fats. Carbohydrate and protein digestion in the duodenum is by pancreatic juice. And it is a common bile duct that brings both bile and pancreatic juice.
NEET HighlightShort gut ↔ carnivorous diet. Bilobed tongue, chyme, duodenum, common bile duct and cloaca are the five most-asked words from frog digestion.

7.11 Respiration and circulation in frog

Respiration

Frogs respire on land and in the water by two different methods.

SituationRespiratory organ(s)Term
In waterSkin acts as the aquatic respiratory organ; dissolved O₂ is exchanged through the skin by diffusionCutaneous respiration
On landBuccal cavity, skin and lungsRespiration by lungs = pulmonary respiration
Aestivation & hibernationSkin onlyCutaneous
  • Lungs: a pair of elongated, pink-coloured sac-like structures in the upper part of the trunk region (thorax).
  • Air enters through the nostrils → buccal cavity → lungs.
TrapDuring aestivation and hibernation, gaseous exchange is through the skin — not the lungs. On land, three surfaces work: buccal cavity, skin and lungs.

Vascular system

  • The vascular system of frog is well-developed closed type. Frogs have a lymphatic system also.
  • Blood vascular system = heart + blood vessels + blood. Lymphatic system = lymph + lymph channels + lymph nodes.
  • Heart: a muscular structure in the upper part of the body cavity.
  • It has three chambers, two atria and one ventricle, and is covered by a membrane called pericardium.
  • A triangular structure called sinus venosus joins the right atrium. It receives blood through the major veins called vena cava.
  • The ventricle opens into a sac-like conus arteriosus on the ventral side of the heart.
  • Blood from the heart is carried to all parts of the body by arteries (arterial system); veins collect blood from different parts and return it to the heart (venous system).

Hepatic portal system

  • Special venous connection between liver and intestine

Renal portal system

  • Special venous connection between kidney and lower parts of the body

Blood and lymph

  • Blood = plasma + cells; cells are RBC (erythrocytes), WBC (leucocytes) and platelets.
  • RBCs are nucleated and contain red coloured pigment, namely haemoglobin.
  • The lymph is different from blood. It lacks few proteins and RBCs.
  • Blood carries nutrients, gases and water to the respective sites during circulation, which is achieved by the pumping action of the muscular heart.
ExceptionFrog RBCs are nucleated — unlike the enucleate RBCs of mammals. A favourite assertion–reason item.
Memory trickBlood route: “Very Sleepy Rabbits Visit Cones” — Vena cava → Sinus venosus → Right atrium → Ventricle → Conus arteriosus.

7.12 Excretory system of frog

Elimination of nitrogenous wastes is carried out by a well-developed excretory system: a pair of kidneys, ureters, cloaca and urinary bladder.

  • Kidneys: compact, dark red, bean-like structures situated a little posteriorly in the body cavity on both sides of the vertebral column.
  • Each kidney is composed of several structural and functional units called uriniferous tubules or nephrons.
  • Two ureters emerge from the kidneys.
  • A thin-walled urinary bladder is present ventral to the rectum and also opens into the cloaca.
  • The frog excretes urea and thus is a ureotelic animal.
  • Excretory wastes are carried by the blood into the kidney, where they are separated and excreted.
FeatureMale frogFemale frog
UreterActs as urinogenital duct (carries urine + sperm)Carries urine only
Opening into cloacaSingle common urinogenital duct on each sideUreters and oviducts open separately in the cloaca
Trap“Urinogenital duct” applies to the male frog. In the female, ovaries have no functional connection with the kidneys, so urinary and genital paths stay separate.
NEET HighlightFrog = ureotelic (urea). Its kidney units = uriniferous tubules / nephrons.

7.13 Control and coordination — nervous system, endocrine glands, sense organs

The system for control and coordination is highly evolved in the frog. It includes both neural system and endocrine glands.

Endocrine glands

Chemical coordination of organs is achieved by hormones secreted by endocrine glands. Prominent ones in frog:

Pituitary, thyroid, parathyroid, thymus, pineal body, pancreatic islets, adrenals and gonads.

7.14 Reproductive system and economic importance of frog

Frogs have well-organised male and female reproductive systems.

Male reproductive system

  • A pair of yellowish ovoid testes, adhered to the upper part of the kidneys by a double fold of peritoneum called mesorchium.
  • Vasa efferentia are 10–12 in number; they arise from the testes, enter the kidneys on their side and open into Bidder’s canal.
  • Bidder’s canal finally communicates with the urinogenital duct, which comes out of the kidneys and opens into the cloaca.
  • The cloaca is a small, median chamber that is used to pass faecal matter, urine and sperms to the exterior.
Testis→Vasa efferentia (10–12)→Kidney→Bidder’s canal→Urinogenital duct→Cloaca

Female reproductive system

  • A pair of ovaries situated near the kidneys; there is no functional connection of ovaries with kidneys.
  • A pair of oviducts arising from the ovaries opens into the cloaca separately.
  • A mature female can lay 2500 to 3000 ova at a time.

Fertilisation and development

  • Fertilisation is external and takes place in water.
  • Development involves a larval stage called tadpole, which undergoes metamorphosis to form the adult.
FeatureMaleFemale
GonadsYellowish ovoid testes on upper kidney (mesorchium)Ovaries near kidneys, no functional link
DuctsVasa efferentia → Bidder’s canal → urinogenital ductOviducts open separately into cloaca

TrapThe numbers: 10–12 vasa efferentia; 2500–3000 ova at a time; 10 pairs of cranial nerves; 3 heart chambers. Don’t swap them.

Economic importance

  • Frogs are beneficial for mankind because they eat insects and protect the crop.
  • They maintain ecological balance as an important link of the food chain and food web in the ecosystem.
  • In some countries the muscular legs of frog are used as food by man.
