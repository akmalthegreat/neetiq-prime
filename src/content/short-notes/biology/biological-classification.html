2.1 How classification began

Humans have always tried to sort living things, first for practical uses (food, shelter, clothing) and later on a scientific basis.

Aristotle — the first scientific attempt

  • Plants were grouped by simple morphology into trees, shrubs and herbs.
  • Animals were split into two groups: those with red blood and those without red blood.

Linnaeus — two-kingdom system

Linnaeus placed all organisms in just two kingdoms: Plantae and Animalia.

Why it failed The two-kingdom system did not distinguish between:
  • prokaryotes and eukaryotes,
  • unicellular and multicellular organisms,
  • photosynthetic (green algae) and non-photosynthetic (fungi) organisms.
A very large number of organisms did not fit into either kingdom.

In older schemes, bacteria, blue-green algae, fungi, mosses, ferns, gymnosperms and angiosperms were all lumped as "plants". The one character uniting them was the presence of a cell wall. This forced heterotrophic fungi into the same group as photosynthetic green plants, even though fungi also differ in cell-wall composition.

Key idea Classification moved from simple morphology towards criteria such as cell structure, nature of wall, mode of nutrition, habitat, reproduction and evolutionary (phylogenetic) relationships.

NEET Highlight
  • Only Monera is prokaryotic; nuclear membrane absent only here.
  • Cell wall: chitin in Fungi, cellulose in Plantae, absent in Animalia.
  • Heterotrophic, chitin-walled fungi, once kept with plants, now form their own kingdom.
  • Unicellular eukaryotes (e.g. Chlamydomonas, Chlorella, earlier with algae; Paramoecium, Amoeba, earlier with animals) were brought together in Protista.
Remember A three-domain system has also been proposed: Monera is split into two domains and all eukaryotic kingdoms form the third — effectively a six-kingdom classification. Classification keeps changing as our understanding of evolution improves.

2.3 Kingdom Monera

Bacteria are the sole members of Kingdom Monera. They are the most abundant microorganisms — hundreds live in a handful of soil — and occur even in extreme habitats such as hot springs, deserts, snow and deep oceans. Many are parasites.

Shapes of bacteria

Bacteria show the most extensive metabolic diversity of any group: some are autotrophs (photosynthetic or chemosynthetic), but the vast majority are heterotrophs.

Archaebacteria

Special because they live in some of the harshest habitats. They differ from other bacteria in having a different cell-wall structure, which helps them survive extreme conditions.

2.4 Eubacteria — the "true" bacteria

Eubacteria have a rigid cell wall and, if motile, a flagellum.

1. Cyanobacteria (blue-green algae)

  • Contain chlorophyll a like green plants; photosynthetic autotrophs.
  • Unicellular, colonial or filamentous; freshwater, marine or terrestrial.
  • Colonies usually surrounded by a gelatinous sheath.
  • Often form blooms in polluted water bodies.
  • Some fix atmospheric nitrogen in specialised cells called heterocysts, e.g. Nostoc and Anabaena.

2. Chemosynthetic autotrophic bacteria

They oxidise inorganic substances such as nitrates, nitrites and ammonia and use the released energy to make ATP. They play a major role in recycling nitrogen, phosphorus, iron and sulphur.

3. Heterotrophic bacteria

The most abundant bacteria in nature; most are important decomposers.

Useful

  • Curd from milk
  • Production of antibiotics
  • N₂ fixation in legume roots

Pathogenic

  • Cholera, typhoid, tetanus (humans)
  • Citrus canker (plants)

Reproduction

  • Mainly by fission; under unfavourable conditions some form spores.
  • A primitive sexual process occurs by transfer of DNA from one bacterium to another.

Mycoplasma

NEET Highlight Mycoplasma completely lack a cell wall, are the smallest living cells known and can survive without oxygen. Many are pathogenic in plants and animals.

2.5 Kingdom Protista — photosynthetic groups

  • All single-celled eukaryotes are placed in Protista; its boundaries are not well defined.
  • Mostly aquatic; may bear cilia or flagella.
  • Being eukaryotes, they have a well-defined nucleus and membrane-bound organelles.
  • Reproduce asexually and sexually (cell fusion + zygote formation).
  • Form a link with plants, animals and fungi.

Chrysophytes — diatoms & golden algae (desmids)

  • Fresh water and marine; microscopic; float passively with currents (plankton). Most are photosynthetic.
  • Diatom walls form two thin overlapping shells that fit together like a soap box, and are embedded with silica — hence indestructible.
  • Their deposits over billions of years form diatomaceous earth — gritty soil used in polishing and filtration of oils and syrups.
  • Diatoms are the chief 'producers' in the oceans.

Dinoflagellates

  • Mostly marine and photosynthetic; appear yellow, green, brown, blue or red depending on main pigment.
  • Cell wall has stiff cellulose plates on its outer surface.
  • Usually two flagella — one longitudinal, one transverse in a furrow between the plates.
  • Red dinoflagellates (Gonyaulax) multiply rapidly and make the sea look red — red tides. Their toxins may kill fishes and other marine animals.

Euglenoids

  • Mostly freshwater, in stagnant water. Example: Euglena.
  • No cell wall; a protein-rich pellicle keeps the body flexible.
  • Two flagella — one short, one long.
  • Photosynthetic in sunlight; in its absence they behave as heterotrophs by preying on smaller organisms.
  • Pigments are identical to those of higher plants.
Trap Silica wall → diatoms; cellulose plates → dinoflagellates; pellicle (no wall) → euglenoids. Don't mix them up.

2.6 Slime moulds and protozoans

Slime moulds

  • Saprophytic protists; the body creeps over decaying twigs and leaves, engulfing organic matter.
  • Under suitable conditions they aggregate into a plasmodium that may spread over several feet.
  • In unfavourable conditions the plasmodium forms fruiting bodies bearing spores at their tips.
  • Spores have true walls, are extremely resistant, survive for many years and are dispersed by air currents.

Protozoans

All are heterotrophs — predators or parasites — and are believed to be primitive relatives of animals. Four major groups:

2.7 Kingdom Fungi — structure and reproduction

A unique kingdom of heterotrophs, diverse in form and habitat. Familiar fungi: bread mould, orange rot, mushrooms and toadstools; white spots on mustard leaves are due to the parasitic fungus Albugo.

Useful

  • Yeast (unicellular) — bread and beer
  • Penicillium — antibiotics

Harmful

  • Puccinia — wheat rust
  • Diseases in plants and animals

Fungi are cosmopolitan, preferring warm and humid places (so food is refrigerated to prevent fungal spoilage).

Body

  • Except unicellular yeasts, fungi are filamentous: thread-like hyphae forming a network called mycelium.
  • Coenocytic hyphae — continuous tubes with multinucleate cytoplasm; others have septa (cross walls).
  • Cell wall made of chitin and polysaccharides.

Nutrition

  • Saprophytes — absorb soluble organic matter from dead substrates.
  • Parasites — depend on living plants and animals.
  • Symbionts — with algae as lichens; with roots of higher plants as mycorrhiza.

Reproduction

  • Vegetative: fragmentation, fission, budding.
  • Asexual spores: conidia, sporangiospores, zoospores.
  • Sexual spores: oospores, ascospores, basidiospores — formed in fruiting bodies.
Plasmogamy (cytoplasm fusion)→Karyogamy (nuclear fusion)→Meiosis in zygote→Haploid spores
Dikaryon In ascomycetes and basidiomycetes, an intervening dikaryotic stage (n + n) — two nuclei per cell — occurs between plasmogamy and karyogamy; this phase is the dikaryophase. In other fungi, fusion of two haploid cells gives a diploid cell immediately.

Two haploid hyphae of compatible mating types fuse in sexual reproduction. Classes are based on morphology of mycelium, mode of spore formation and fruiting bodies.

2.8 The four classes of fungi

Phycomycetes

  • Aquatic, on decaying wood in moist places, or obligate parasites on plants.
  • Mycelium aseptate and coenocytic.
  • Asexual: zoospores (motile) or aplanospores (non-motile), produced endogenously in sporangium.
  • Sexual: zygospore by fusion of two gametes — isogamous, anisogamous or oogamous.
  • Examples: Mucor, Rhizopus (bread mould), Albugo (parasite on mustard).

Ascomycetes — sac fungi

  • Mostly multicellular (Penicillium), rarely unicellular (yeast, Saccharomyces).
  • Saprophytic, decomposers, parasitic or coprophilous (growing on dung). Mycelium branched, septate.
  • Asexual: conidia, produced exogenously on conidiophores.
  • Sexual: ascospores, produced endogenously in sac-like asci, which are arranged in fruiting bodies called ascocarps.
  • Examples: Aspergillus, Claviceps, Neurospora (used widely in biochemical and genetic work). Morels and truffles are edible delicacies.

Basidiomycetes

  • Mushrooms, bracket fungi, puffballs; in soil, on logs and stumps, or as parasites (rusts and smuts). Mycelium branched, septate.
  • Asexual spores generally absent; fragmentation is common. Sex organs absent.
  • Plasmogamy by fusion of two vegetative/somatic cells of different strains → dikaryotic structure → basidium.
  • Karyogamy and meiosis in the basidium give four basidiospores, produced exogenously; basidia are borne in basidiocarps.
  • Examples: Agaricus (mushroom), Ustilago (smut), Puccinia (rust).

Deuteromycetes — imperfect fungi

  • Called imperfect because only asexual or vegetative phases are known. When the sexual stage is discovered, the fungus is shifted to its proper class.
  • Reproduce only by conidia; mycelium septate and branched.
  • Saprophytes or parasites; many are decomposers of litter and help in mineral cycling.
  • Examples: Alternaria, Colletotrichum, Trichoderma.

2.9 Kingdoms Plantae and Animalia

Kingdom Plantae

  • All eukaryotic, chlorophyll-containing organisms; prominent chloroplasts; cell wall mainly cellulose.
  • Includes algae, bryophytes, pteridophytes, gymnosperms and angiosperms.
Exception A few plants are partially heterotrophic: insectivorous plants such as bladderwort and Venus fly trap, and the parasite Cuscuta.
Alternation of generation The plant life cycle has a diploid sporophytic and a haploid gametophytic phase that alternate with each other. Their relative lengths, and whether each is free-living or dependent, vary between groups.

Kingdom Animalia

  • Heterotrophic, eukaryotic, multicellular; cells lack cell walls.
  • Depend directly or indirectly on plants for food; digest food in an internal cavity.
  • Store reserve food as glycogen or fat; nutrition is holozoic (by ingestion).
  • Definite growth pattern, reaching adults of definite shape and size.
  • Higher forms show elaborate sensory and neuromotor mechanisms; most can move.
  • Sexual reproduction by copulation of male and female, followed by embryological development.
Key idea Both kingdoms are treated in detail in later chapters (Plant Kingdom; Animal Kingdom). Here, just know their defining features.

2.10 Viruses

Viruses, viroids, prions and lichens find no place in the five-kingdom system.

Definition Viruses are non-cellular organisms with an inert crystalline structure outside the living cell. Inside a host cell they take over its machinery to replicate, killing the host. "Virus" means venom or poisonous fluid. They are obligate parasites.

Discovery

ScientistContribution
D.J. Ivanowsky (1892)Found microbes causing mosaic disease of tobacco; smaller than bacteria as they passed through bacteria-proof filters
M.W. Beijerinek (1898)Extract of infected tobacco infected healthy plants; called the fluid Contagium vivum fluidum (infectious living fluid)
W.M. Stanley (1935)Showed viruses can be crystallised; crystals are mostly proteins

Structure

  • A virus is a nucleoprotein: protein + genetic material (RNA or DNA — never both); the genetic material is infectious.
  • Plant viruses: usually ssRNA. Animal viruses: ss or dsRNA, or dsDNA. Bacteriophages: usually dsDNA.
  • Protein coat = capsid, made of subunits called capsomeres, arranged in helical or polyhedral forms; it protects the nucleic acid.

Diseases

In humans

  • Mumps, small pox, herpes, influenza
  • AIDS

Symptoms in plants

  • Mosaic formation; leaf rolling and curling
  • Yellowing and vein clearing
  • Dwarfing and stunted growth

  • (a) TMV (b) Bacteriophage — labels to know: RNA, capsid (TMV); head, collar, sheath, tail fibres (phage); asked as: identify part / nature of genetic material.

2.11 Viroids, prions and lichens

Viroids

  • Discovered by T.O. Diener (1971) as the cause of potato spindle tuber disease.
  • Smaller than viruses; consist of free RNA without a protein coat.
  • The RNA is of low molecular weight.

Prions

  • Infectious agents made of abnormally folded protein; similar in size to viruses; transmit certain neurological diseases.
  • Diseases: bovine spongiform encephalopathy (BSE) or mad cow disease in cattle, and its analogous variant Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease (CJD) in humans.

Virus

  • Protein coat + RNA or DNA
  • e.g. TMV, phage

Viroid

  • Free RNA only
  • Potato spindle tuber

Prion

  • Misfolded protein only
  • BSE, CJD

Lichens

Definition Lichens are symbiotic (mutually useful) associations between algae and fungi. Algal partner = phycobiont (autotrophic); fungal partner = mycobiont (heterotrophic).
  • Alga prepares food for the fungus; the fungus provides shelter and absorbs water and mineral nutrients for its partner.
  • The association is so close that a lichen looks like a single organism.
NEET Highlight Lichens are very good pollution indicators — they do not grow in polluted areas.
Memory trick Phyco = alga (think phycology, study of algae) → cooks the food; Myco = fungus (think mycology) → provides the house.
