12.1 Ecosystem — structure and function

DefinitionAn ecosystem is a functional unit of nature where living organisms interact among themselves and with their surrounding physical environment. It varies from a small pond to a large forest or sea; many ecologists regard the entire biosphere as a global ecosystem.
CategoryExamples
TerrestrialForest, grassland, desert
AquaticPond, lake, wetland, river, estuary
Man-madeCrop fields, aquarium
  • Identification and enumeration of species = species composition. Vertical distribution of species at different levels = stratification (trees top, shrubs second, herbs and grasses bottom).
  • Ecosystem functions: productivity, decomposition, energy flow and nutrient cycling.

Pond — a simple, self-sustainable ecosystem

ComponentIn a pond (NCERT)
AbioticWater with dissolved inorganic and organic substances; rich soil deposit at the bottom; solar input, temperature, day-length regulate the rate of function
AutotrophsPhytoplankton, some algae, floating, submerged and marginal plants
ConsumersZooplankton, free-swimming and bottom-dwelling forms
DecomposersFungi, bacteria and flagellates, abundant at the bottom
Key idea(i) Autotrophs convert inorganic to organic material using solar energy; (ii) heterotrophs consume autotrophs; (iii) decomposition and mineralisation release materials for reuse. Energy moves unidirectionally to higher trophic levels and is lost as heat.

12.2 Productivity

  • Primary production: amount of biomass or organic matter produced per unit area over a time period by plants during photosynthesis; expressed as weight (g m–2) or energy (kcal m–2).
  • The rate of biomass production = productivity, expressed in g m–2 yr–1 or (kcal m–2) yr–1.
TermMeaning
GPP (gross primary productivity)Rate of production of organic matter during photosynthesis
NPP (net primary productivity)GPP – R = NPP (R = respiration losses); available biomass for heterotrophs (herbivores and decomposers)
Secondary productivityRate of formation of new organic matter by consumers
  • Primary productivity depends on the plant species, environmental factors, nutrient availability and photosynthetic capacity of plants.
NEET HighlightAnnual NPP of the whole biosphere ≈ 170 billion tons (dry weight). Oceans, despite covering about 70% of the surface, produce only 55 billion tons; the rest is on land.
TrapNPP = GPP minus respiration (not plus). Secondary productivity belongs to consumers. Ocean productivity is low relative to its area.

12.3 Decomposition

  • Earthworm = ‘farmer’s friend’: breaks down complex organic matter and loosens soil.
  • Decomposers break down complex organic matter into CO2, water and nutrients = decomposition.
  • Dead plant remains (leaves, bark, flowers) and dead animal remains including fecal matter = detritus, the raw material for decomposition.
Fragmentation→Leaching→Catabolism→Humification→Mineralisation
StepWhat happens
FragmentationDetritivores (e.g. earthworm) break detritus into smaller particles
LeachingWater-soluble inorganic nutrients go down into the soil horizon and precipitate as unavailable salts
CatabolismBacterial and fungal enzymes degrade detritus into simpler inorganic substances
HumificationForms dark, amorphous humus: highly resistant to microbial action, decomposes extremely slowly, colloidal, a reservoir of nutrients
MineralisationHumus degraded by microbes, releasing inorganic nutrients
  • All steps operate simultaneously; humification and mineralisation occur in soil. Decomposition is largely oxygen-requiring.

Slower decomposition

  • Detritus rich in lignin and chitin
  • Low temperature and anaerobiosis → build-up of organic matter

Faster decomposition

  • Detritus rich in nitrogen and water-soluble substances like sugars
  • Warm and moist environment
NEET HighlightTemperature and soil moisture are the most important climatic factors regulating decomposition (through soil microbes).
TrapLeaching makes nutrients unavailable (precipitated salts). Humus decomposes very slowly. Fragmentation is by detritivores; catabolism by bacterial and fungal enzymes.

12.4 Energy flow

  • Except for the deep-sea hydro-thermal ecosystem, the sun is the only energy source for all ecosystems.
  • Of incident solar radiation, less than 50% is photosynthetically active radiation (PAR). Plants capture only 2–10% of PAR, and this sustains the entire living world.
  • Energy flow is unidirectional: sun → producers → consumers. Ecosystems obey the first and second laws of thermodynamics; they need a constant energy supply to counter increasing disorderliness.
RoleExamples
ProducersTerrestrial: herbaceous and woody plants; aquatic: phytoplankton, algae, higher plants
Primary consumers (herbivores)Insects, birds, mammals (terrestrial); molluscs (aquatic)
Secondary consumersPrimary carnivores (feed on herbivores)
Tertiary consumersSecondary carnivores

Grazing food chain (GFC)

  • Begins with producers: Grass → Goat → Man
  • Major conduit of energy in aquatic ecosystems

Detritus food chain (DFC)

  • Begins with dead organic matter; decomposers (mainly fungi and bacteria) = saprotrophs (sapro: to decompose); secrete digestive enzymes and absorb products
  • In terrestrial ecosystems a much larger fraction of energy flows through DFC than GFC
  • DFC and GFC are connected (some DFC organisms are prey to GFC animals; omnivores like cockroaches and crows) → food web.
  • Trophic levels: producers = first; herbivores = second; carnivores = third. Energy decreases at successive levels.
  • Standing crop: mass of living material at a trophic level at a particular time, measured as biomass or number per unit area; dry weight is more accurate.
10 per cent lawOnly about 10% of energy is transferred to each trophic level from the lower one; this restricts the number of trophic levels in a grazing food chain.
TrapPlants capture 2–10% of PAR, and PAR is < 50% of incident radiation. GFC dominates in water; DFC dominates on land.

12.5 Ecological pyramids

Base = producers (first trophic level); apex = top consumer. Three types: number, biomass and energy. Calculations must include all organisms at a trophic level.

PyramidUsuallyException (NCERT)
NumberUpright (grassland: PP 5,842,000 → PC 708,000 → SC 354,000 → TC 3)Inverted: insects feeding on a big tree (+ small birds on insects, larger birds on small birds)
BiomassUpright (grassland: PP 809 → PC 37 → SC 11 → TC 1.5)Inverted in the sea: biomass of fishes/zooplankton exceeds phytoplankton (PP 4 → PC 21)
EnergyAlways upright; can never be inverted — energy is lost as heat at each stepNone
Trophic level is functionalA trophic level represents a functional level, not a species. A sparrow is a primary consumer when it eats seeds, fruits, peas, and a secondary consumer when it eats insects and worms.
Limitations of pyramids(i) Do not account for the same species at two or more trophic levels; (ii) assume a simple food chain, not a food web; (iii) saprophytes are given no place, though vital.
