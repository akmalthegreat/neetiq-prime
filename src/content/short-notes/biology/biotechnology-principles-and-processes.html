9.1 Principles of biotechnology

EFB definitionThe European Federation of Biotechnology (EFB): ‘The integration of natural science and organisms, cells, parts thereof, and molecular analogues for products and services’.

Genetic engineering

  • Alter the chemistry of genetic material (DNA, RNA), introduce it into a host and change its phenotype

Bioprocess engineering

  • Maintain a sterile (contamination-free) ambience so only the desired microbe/cell grows in large quantities for antibiotics, vaccines, enzymes etc.
  • Traditional hybridisation often carries undesirable genes along with desired ones; genetic engineering (recombinant DNA, gene cloning, gene transfer) introduces only the desired gene(s).
  • An alien DNA piece multiplies only if linked to an origin of replication (ori). Making multiple identical copies of template DNA = cloning.
First recombinant DNA (1972)Stanley Cohen and Herbert Boyer cut an antibiotic resistance gene from a plasmid of Salmonella typhimurium using restriction enzymes (‘molecular scissors’), joined it to a plasmid vector with DNA ligase, and transferred it into Escherichia coli, where it replicated using the host’s DNA polymerase = cloning of the antibiotic resistance gene in E. coli. A plasmid = autonomously replicating circular extra-chromosomal DNA.
Identify DNA with desirable gene→Introduce it into the host→Maintain it in host and pass it to progeny

9.2.1 Restriction enzymes

  • In 1963, two enzymes restricting growth of bacteriophage in E. coli were isolated: one added methyl groups to DNA, the other cut DNA (= restriction endonuclease).
  • First restriction endonuclease: Hind II (isolated five years later); always cuts at a particular point by recognising a specific sequence of six base pairs = recognition sequence.
  • Today: more than 900 restriction enzymes from over 230 strains of bacteria.
Letter(s) in EcoRIComes from
EGenus — Escherichia
coSpecies — coli
RStrain — RY 13
IRoman numeral: order of isolation from that strain

Exonucleases

  • Remove nucleotides from the ends of DNA

Endonucleases

  • Cut at specific positions within DNA (restriction enzymes)
  • Each restriction endonuclease recognises a specific palindromic sequence: base pairs reading the same on both strands when read in the same orientation (e.g. 5′-GAATTC-3′ / 3′-CTTAAG-5′).
  • They cut a little away from the centre of the palindrome but between the same two bases on opposite strands → single-stranded overhangs = sticky ends (form H-bonds with complementary ends), which help DNA ligase.
  • DNA from different sources cut with the same enzyme has the same sticky ends and can be joined → recombinant DNA.
TrapRestriction enzymes cut the sugar-phosphate backbone (phosphodiester bond). First isolated = Hind II. In EcoRI, R = strain, not “restriction”.

9.2.1 Separation and isolation of DNA fragments

  • Gel electrophoresis: DNA is negatively charged, so it moves towards the anode through a matrix.
  • Matrix: agarose, a natural polymer extracted from sea weeds; fragments separate by size (sieving effect) — smaller fragments move farther.
  • Visualised only after staining with ethidium bromide and exposure to UV → bright orange bands.
  • Bands are cut out and DNA extracted from the gel piece = elution.
TrapDNA moves to the anode (+). Smaller = farther. Bands are orange under UV, invisible in visible light.

9.2.2 Cloning vectors

  • Plasmids and bacteriophages replicate independently of chromosomal DNA. Bacteriophages have very high copy numbers; plasmids may have 1–2 or 15–100 copies per cell.
Feature of a vectorWhy needed
Origin of replication (ori)Replication starts here; also controls copy number of linked DNA (high copy number → many copies)
Selectable markerIdentifies and eliminates non-transformants, allows growth of transformants. For E. coli: resistance to ampicillin, chloramphenicol, tetracycline, kanamycin (normal E. coli has none)
Cloning sitesVery few, preferably single, recognition sites for common restriction enzymes (more sites → many fragments, complicating cloning)
TransformationThe procedure through which a piece of DNA is introduced into a host bacterium.

Selecting recombinants

MethodPrinciple (NCERT)
Antibiotic inactivation (pBR322)Foreign DNA ligated at the BamH I site of tetR → recombinants lose tetracycline resistance. Plate on ampicillin (selects transformants), then on tetracycline: recombinants grow on ampicillin but not on tetracycline; non-recombinants grow on both. Cumbersome (two plates).
Blue-white selectionInsert placed within the coding sequence of β-galactosidase → insertional inactivation. With a chromogenic substrate: no insert → blue colonies; insert (recombinant) → white colonies.
TrapWhite = recombinant; blue = non-recombinant. In pBR322 with insert in tetR, recombinants are amp-resistant but tet-sensitive.

Vectors for plants and animals

  • Agrobacterium tumefaciens, a pathogen of several dicot plants, delivers ‘T-DNA’ that turns plant cells into tumours. Its Ti (tumour inducing) plasmid is modified into a non-pathogenic cloning vector.
  • Retroviruses (transform animal cells into cancerous cells) are disarmed and used to deliver genes into animal cells.

9.2.3 Competent host (for transformation with rDNA)

  • DNA is hydrophilic and cannot pass through membranes; bacteria are made ‘competent’ by treating with a divalent cation such as calcium.
  • Cells incubated with rDNA on ice → heat shock at 42 °C briefly → back on ice → bacteria take up rDNA.

9.3 Processes of recombinant DNA technology

Isolate DNA→Cut with restriction enzyme→Isolate desired fragment→Ligate into vector→Transfer into host→Culture at large scale→Extract product

Purified DNA precipitates on adding chilled ethanol, seen as fine threads in suspension.

9.3.2–9.3.3 Cutting and joining

  • Purified DNA is incubated with a restriction enzyme at optimal conditions; agarose gel electrophoresis checks digestion. Vector DNA is cut with the same enzyme; gene of interest + cut vector + ligase → recombinant DNA.

9.3.4 Amplification of gene of interest using PCR

Polymerase Chain Reaction: multiple copies of the gene synthesised in vitro using two sets of primers (small chemically synthesised oligonucleotides complementary to DNA regions) and DNA polymerase.

  • Steps: denaturation → annealing → extension. Repeated cycles amplify the segment about a billion times.
  • Uses a thermostable DNA polymerase from Thermus aquaticus (Taq), active during high-temperature denaturation.
TrapPrimers are DNA oligonucleotides (chemically synthesised), two sets. Taq comes from a bacterium, Thermus aquaticus.

9.3.5 Insertion of rDNA into host

If rDNA carries an ampicillin resistance gene, transformed E. coli grow on ampicillin plates while untransformed cells die; the ampicillin resistance gene is the selectable marker.

9.3.6 Obtaining the foreign gene product

  • A protein-encoding gene expressed in a heterologous host gives a recombinant protein.
  • Continuous culture: used medium drained out from one side, fresh medium added from the other → cells stay in the log/exponential phase → more biomass and yield.
  • Bioreactors process large volumes (100–1000 litres), providing optimum temperature, pH, substrate, salts, vitamins and oxygen.
Stirred-tank bioreactor
Most commonly used; cylindrical or with curved base for mixing; stirrer gives even mixing and oxygen (or air is bubbled = sparged)
Has: agitator system, oxygen delivery system, foam control system, temperature control, pH control and sampling ports

9.3.7 Downstream processing

After biosynthesis, the product undergoes separation and purification = downstream processing; it is formulated with preservatives, undergoes clinical trials (for drugs) and strict quality control; processes vary from product to product.
