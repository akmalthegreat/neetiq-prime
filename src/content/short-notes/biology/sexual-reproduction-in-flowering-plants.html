1.2 Pre-fertilisation: structures and events

Much before the flower is seen on the plant, the decision that the plant is going to flower has taken place. Hormonal and structural changes lead to differentiation of the floral primordium; inflorescences form, bearing floral buds and then flowers.

1.2.1 Stamen, microsporangium and pollen grain

  • A stamen has a long slender stalk, the filament, and a terminal, generally bilobed structure, the anther. The proximal end of the filament is attached to the thalamus or petal.
  • A typical angiosperm anther is bilobed, each lobe having two theca → dithecous. A longitudinal groove often separates the theca.
  • The anther is a tetragonal structure with four microsporangia at the corners, two in each lobe. Microsporangia develop into pollen sacs, packed with pollen grains.
Wall layer (outer → inner)Function
Epidermis, endothecium, middle layersProtection and help in dehiscence of anther to release pollen
Tapetum (innermost)Nourishes the developing pollen grains; cells have dense cytoplasm and generally more than one nucleus

In a young anther, a group of compactly arranged homogeneous cells called sporogenous tissue occupies the centre of each microsporangium.

Microsporogenesis

  • Each sporogenous cell is a potential pollen mother cell (PMC) / microspore mother cell; it undergoes meiosis to form a microspore tetrad.
  • Formation of microspores from a PMC through meiosis = microsporogenesis.
  • As anthers mature and dehydrate, microspores dissociate from each other and develop into pollen grains; several thousands form in each microsporangium.

1.2.1 Pollen grain

  • Pollen grains represent the male gametophytes; generally spherical, about 25–50 micrometres in diameter.

Exine (outer, hard)

  • Made of sporopollenin, one of the most resistant organic materials known; withstands high temperature, strong acids and alkali; no enzyme that degrades it is known
  • Germ pores = apertures where sporopollenin is absent
  • Pollen are well preserved as fossils because of sporopollenin

Intine (inner)

  • Thin, continuous layer of cellulose and pectin
Cell of mature pollenFeatures
Vegetative cellBigger, abundant food reserve, large irregularly shaped nucleus
Generative cellSmall, floats in the cytoplasm of the vegetative cell; spindle shaped, dense cytoplasm and a nucleus
NEET HighlightIn over 60% of angiosperms, pollen grains are shed at the 2-celled stage. In the rest, the generative cell divides mitotically into two male gametes before shedding (3-celled stage).

Megasporogenesis and the female gametophyte

  1. A single megaspore mother cell (MMC) differentiates in the micropylar region of the nucellus (large cell, dense cytoplasm, prominent nucleus).
  2. MMC undergoes meiosis → four megaspores (megasporogenesis).
  3. In most flowering plants one megaspore is functional, the other three degenerate → monosporic development.
  4. Functional megaspore nucleus divides mitotically: 2-nucleate → 4-nucleate → 8-nucleate. These divisions are free nuclear (no immediate wall formation).
  5. After the 8-nucleate stage, cell walls are laid down → organised embryo sac.

1.2.3 Pollination

Transfer of pollen grains (shed from the anther) to the stigma of a pistil.

KindPollen goes toNote
AutogamyStigma of the same flowerComplete autogamy is rare in flowers that open
GeitonogamyStigma of another flower of the same plantFunctionally cross-pollination (needs an agent) but genetically similar to autogamy

Chasmogamous flowers

  • Flowers that open, exposing anthers and stigma

Cleistogamous flowers

  • Never open; anthers and stigma lie close; anthers dehisce in the bud
  • Invariably autogamous; assured seed-set even without pollinators
  • Viola (common pansy), Oxalis, Commelina produce both types

1.2.3 Agents of pollination

Abiotic (wind, water) and biotic (animals). Majority of plants use biotic agents; among abiotic, wind is more common.

Wind pollination (anemophily)Water pollination (hydrophily)
Pollen light and non-sticky; well-exposed stamens; large, often feathery stigma; often a single ovule per ovary; numerous flowers in an inflorescence. Corn cob: the tassels are stigma and style. Common in grasses.Quite rare, about 30 genera, mostly monocots. Examples: Vallisneria, Hydrilla (fresh water), marine sea-grasses like Zostera. Pollen protected from wetting by a mucilaginous covering.
  • Vallisneria: female flower reaches the water surface by a long stalk; male flowers/pollen are released on the surface and carried passively.
  • Sea-grasses: female flowers remain submerged; long, ribbon-like pollen released inside water.
  • Not all aquatic plants are water-pollinated: in water hyacinth and water lily, flowers emerge above water and are pollinated by insects or wind.
  • Wind- and water-pollinated flowers are not very colourful and do not produce nectar.

Biotic pollination

  • Bees, butterflies, flies, beetles, wasps, ants, moths, birds (sunbirds, humming birds) and bats; also lemurs, arboreal rodents, reptiles (gecko lizard, garden lizard). Bees are the dominant biotic pollinators.
  • Insect-pollinated flowers: large, colourful, fragrant, rich in nectar; small flowers clustered into inflorescences. Fly/beetle-pollinated flowers secrete foul odours. Pollen is generally sticky.
  • Floral rewards: nectar and pollen; sometimes a safe place to lay eggs.

1.2.4 Pollen-pistil interaction

  • The pistil recognises compatible pollen (accepts it) or incompatible pollen (rejects it by preventing germination or pollen tube growth). This is a continuous chemical dialogue between pollen and pistil.
  • Compatible pollen germinates to form a pollen tube through one of the germ pores; it grows through stigma and style to the ovary.
  • 2-celled pollen: the generative cell divides into two male gametes during pollen tube growth. 3-celled pollen: tube carries two gametes from the start.
  • Pollen tube enters the ovule through the micropyle, then one synergid through the filiform apparatus.
  • All events from pollen deposition on the stigma until pollen tubes enter the ovule = pollen-pistil interaction.
Artificial hybridisationEmasculation: removal of anthers from the flower bud before dehiscence using forceps. Bagging: covering with a bag (generally butter paper) to prevent contamination. When the stigma is receptive, pollen from the male parent is dusted on it and the flower is rebagged. If the female parent has unisexual flowers, no emasculation is needed.

1.3 Double fertilisation

  1. Pollen tube releases two male gametes into the cytoplasm of a synergid.
  2. One male gamete fuses with the egg nucleus → syngamy → diploid zygote.
  3. The other fuses with the two polar nuclei → triploid primary endosperm nucleus (PEN). Fusion of three haploid nuclei = triple fusion.
NEET HighlightSyngamy + triple fusion = double fertilisation, an event unique to flowering plants. The central cell becomes the primary endosperm cell (PEC) → endosperm; zygote → embryo.
TrapTriple fusion involves three haploid nuclei (1 male gamete + 2 polar nuclei), giving a 3n PEN. Gametes are released into a synergid, not directly into the egg.

1.4 Post-fertilisation: endosperm and embryo

1.4.1 Endosperm

  • Endosperm development precedes embryo development. PEC divides repeatedly → triploid endosperm filled with reserve food for the embryo.
  • Most common type: PEN undergoes successive nuclear divisions → free-nuclear endosperm; later cell walls form → cellular endosperm.
  • Coconut water = free-nuclear endosperm (thousands of nuclei); surrounding white kernel = cellular endosperm.
  • Endosperm may be fully consumed before seed maturation (pea, groundnut, beans) or persist in the mature seed (castor, coconut).

1.4.2 Embryo

  • Develops at the micropylar end. Most zygotes divide only after some endosperm has formed (assured nutrition).
  • Early stages (embryogeny) are similar in monocots and dicots: zygote → proembryo → globular → heart-shaped → mature embryo.

Dicot embryo

  • Embryonal axis + two cotyledons
  • Epicotyl (above cotyledons) ends in plumule
  • Hypocotyl (below) ends in radicle, covered by root cap

Monocot (grass) embryo

  • One cotyledon = scutellum, lateral to the axis
  • Radicle and root cap enclosed in coleorrhiza
  • Shoot apex and leaf primordia enclosed in coleoptile
Memory trick“Coleo-RHIZA = Root, Coleo-PTILE = Plumule (shoot)”.

1.4.3 Seed

  • Seed = final product of sexual reproduction, a fertilised ovule; seed coat(s) + cotyledon(s) + embryo axis.
  • Integuments harden into seed coats; the micropyle remains as a pore for entry of oxygen and water during germination.
  • Mature seeds are dry: 10–15% moisture by mass; embryo may enter dormancy, or germinate with adequate moisture, oxygen and suitable temperature.
Seed typeMeaningExamples
Non-albuminous (ex-albuminous)No residual endospermPea, groundnut
AlbuminousRetains part of endospermWheat, maize, barley, castor
PerispermResidual persistent nucellusBlack pepper, beet

Fruit

  • Ovules → seeds and ovary → fruit proceed simultaneously; ovary wall → pericarp. Fruits may be fleshy (guava, orange, mango) or dry (groundnut, mustard).
  • False fruits: thalamus also contributes, e.g. apple, strawberry, cashew. True fruits: develop only from the ovary.
  • Parthenocarpic fruits develop without fertilisation, e.g. banana; can be induced by growth hormones; such fruits are seedless.

Advantages and viability of seeds

  • Pollination and fertilisation are independent of water → dependable; better dispersal; food reserves nourish seedlings; hard seed coat protects; new genetic combinations → variation; basis of agriculture (dehydration and dormancy allow storage).

1.5 Apomixis and polyembryony

  • Apomixis: production of seeds without fertilisation (some species of Asteraceae and grasses); a form of asexual reproduction that mimics sexual reproduction.
  • Ways: a diploid egg formed without reduction division develops into an embryo without fertilisation; or, as in many Citrus and Mango varieties, nucellar cells surrounding the embryo sac divide, protrude into it and develop into embryos.
  • More than one embryo in a seed = polyembryony.
Why apomixis mattersSeeds collected from hybrids segregate and lose hybrid characters, so farmers must buy costly hybrid seed every year. If hybrids are made apomictic, there is no segregation and farmers can reuse hybrid seed year after year.
