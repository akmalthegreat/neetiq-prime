4.1 Basis of classification: levels of organisation

More than a million animal species have been described, so a classification system is essential. Animals differ in structure and form, yet some fundamental features are shared by many groups. These features form the basis of classification:

Levels of organisation·Symmetry·Germ layers·Coelom·Segmentation·Notochord

Levels of organisation

LevelWhat it meansSeen in
CellularCells arranged as loose cell aggregates; some division of labour among cellsSponges (Porifera)
TissueCells performing the same function are arranged into tissuesCoelenterates, ctenophores
OrganTissues grouped to form organs, each for a specific functionPlatyhelminthes
Organ systemOrgans associate to form functional systems, each for a specific physiological functionAnnelids, arthropods, molluscs, echinoderms, chordates
RememberOrgan systems of different groups show varied patterns of complexity; the digestive and circulatory systems are the classic examples.

Digestive system

Incomplete

  • Only one external opening, which acts as both mouth and anus
  • e.g., Platyhelminthes

Complete

  • Two openings: mouth and anus
  • e.g., Aschelminthes onwards

Circulatory system

Open type

  • Blood is pumped out of the heart; cells and tissues are directly bathed in it
  • e.g., arthropods, molluscs, hemichordates

Closed type

  • Blood circulates through a series of vessels of varying diameters (arteries, veins, capillaries)
  • e.g., annelids, chordates

4.2 Symmetry and germ layers

Symmetry

TypeDefinitionExamples
AsymmetricalNo plane through the centre gives equal halvesMost sponges
RadialAny plane passing through the central axis divides the body into two identical halvesCoelenterates, ctenophores, echinoderms (adult)
BilateralIdentical left and right halves are obtained in only one planeAnnelids, arthropods etc.

Germ layers (diploblastic vs triploblastic)

Diploblastic

  • Two embryonic layers: outer ectoderm and inner endoderm
  • An undifferentiated layer, mesoglea, lies between them
  • e.g., coelenterates (and ctenophores)

Triploblastic

  • A third layer, mesoderm, develops between ectoderm and endoderm
  • Platyhelminthes to chordates

4.3 Coelom, segmentation and notochord

DefinitionCoelom is the body cavity between the body wall and the gut wall that is lined by mesoderm.
TypeBody cavityExamples
Coelo­matesTrue coelom, fully lined by mesodermAnnelids, molluscs, arthropods, echinoderms, hemichordates, chordates
Pseudo­coelomatesCavity not lined by mesoderm; mesoderm present as scattered pouches between ectoderm and endodermAschelminthes
Acoelo­matesNo body cavityPlatyhelminthes
TrapIn a pseudocoelomate the cavity exists, but the gut wall has no mesodermal lining; mesoderm is present only as scattered pouches. Do not call Aschelminthes acoelomate.

Segmentation

In some animals the body is divided externally and internally into segments, with serial repetition of at least some organs. This is metameric segmentation and the phenomenon is metamerism, e.g., earthworm.

Notochord

DefinitionA mesodermally derived rod-like structure formed on the dorsal side during embryonic development in some animals. Animals with a notochord are chordates; those without one are non-chordates (Porifera to Echinodermata, plus Hemichordata).

4.5 Phylum Porifera (sponges)

Members have pores in the body (pore bearing). Generally marine, mostly asymmetrical, and primitive multicellular animals at the cellular level of organisation.

Water canal system

Ostia (minute pores)→Spongocoel (central cavity)→Osculum
  • Water transport (canal) system helps in food gathering, respiratory exchange and removal of wastes.
  • Choanocytes (collar cells) line the spongocoel and the canals.
  • Digestion is intracellular.
  • Skeleton: spicules or spongin fibres.

Reproduction

  • Sexes not separate: hermaphrodite (eggs and sperms produced by the same individual).
  • Asexual: fragmentation. Sexual: formation of gametes.
  • Fertilisation internal; development indirect (a larval stage morphologically distinct from the adult).
ExampleCommon name
SyconScypha
SpongillaFreshwater sponge
EuspongiaBath sponge
NEET HighlightWater enters through ostia and leaves through the osculum, never the other way round. Collar cells (choanocytes) are the hallmark cells of sponges.
TrapMost sponges are marine, but Spongilla is a freshwater sponge.

4.6 Coelenterata (Cnidaria) and Ctenophora

Phylum Coelenterata (Cnidaria)

  • Aquatic, mostly marine; sessile or free-swimming; radially symmetrical.
  • Name Cnidaria from cnidoblasts or cnidocytes, which contain stinging capsules (nematocysts), present on tentacles and body. Used for anchorage, defence and capture of prey.
  • Tissue level, diploblastic.
  • Central gastro-vascular cavity with a single opening, the mouth, on the hypostome.
  • Digestion is extracellular and intracellular.
  • Some (e.g., corals) have a skeleton of calcium carbonate.

Polyp

  • Sessile, cylindrical
  • Hydra, Adamsia

Medusa

  • Free-swimming, umbrella-shaped
  • Aurelia (jelly fish)
Key ideaCnidarians that show both forms show alternation of generation (metagenesis): polyps produce medusae asexually and medusae form polyps sexually, e.g., Obelia.
ExampleCommon name

Phylum Ctenophora

  • Commonly called sea walnuts or comb jellies.
  • Exclusively marine, radially symmetrical, diploblastic, tissue level.
  • Body bears eight external rows of ciliated comb plates, which help in locomotion.
  • Digestion both extracellular and intracellular.
  • Bioluminescence (property of a living organism to emit light) is well-marked.
  • Sexes not separate; reproduction only sexual; fertilisation external, development indirect.
  • Examples: Pleurobrachia, Ctenoplana.
TrapJelly fish (Aurelia) is a cnidarian medusa; comb jelly (Pleurobrachia) is a ctenophore. Comb plates = Ctenophora; cnidoblasts = Cnidaria.

4.7 Platyhelminthes and Aschelminthes

Phylum Platyhelminthes (flatworms)

  • Body dorso-ventrally flattened.
  • Mostly endoparasites found in animals including human beings.
  • Bilaterally symmetrical, triploblastic, acoelomate, organ level of organisation.
  • Hooks and suckers in parasitic forms; some absorb nutrients from the host directly through the body surface.
  • Specialised flame cells help in osmoregulation and excretion.
  • Sexes not separate. Fertilisation internal; development through many larval stages.
  • Some members like Planaria have high regeneration capacity.
ExampleCommon name
TaeniaTapeworm
FasciolaLiver fluke

Phylum Aschelminthes (roundworms)

  • Body circular in cross-section, hence roundworms.
  • Free-living, aquatic and terrestrial, or parasitic in plants and animals.
  • Organ-system level, bilateral, triploblastic, pseudocoelomate.
  • Alimentary canal complete with a well-developed muscular pharynx.
  • An excretory tube removes body wastes through the excretory pore.
  • Sexes separate (dioecious); often females are longer than males.
  • Fertilisation internal; development direct (young resemble adult) or indirect.

4.8 Phylum Annelida

  • Aquatic (marine and freshwater) or terrestrial; free-living, sometimes parasitic.
  • Organ-system level, bilateral, triploblastic, coelomate, metamerically segmented.
  • Name from Latin annulus = little ring (body surface distinctly marked into segments or metameres).
  • Longitudinal and circular muscles help in locomotion.
  • Aquatic annelids like Nereis have lateral appendages, parapodia, which help in swimming.
  • Closed circulatory system.
  • Nephridia (sing. nephridium) help in osmoregulation and excretion.
  • Neural system: paired ganglia connected by lateral nerves to a double ventral nerve cord.
  • Reproduction sexual.

4.9 Phylum Arthropoda

Key ideaThe largest phylum of Animalia, including insects. Over two-thirds of all named species on earth are arthropods.
  • Organ-system level, bilateral, triploblastic, segmented, coelomate.
  • Body covered by a chitinous exoskeleton.
  • Body consists of head, thorax and abdomen.
  • Jointed appendages (arthros = joint, poda = appendages).
  • Open circulatory system.
  • Excretion through Malpighian tubules.
  • Mostly dioecious; fertilisation usually internal; mostly oviparous; development direct or indirect.

4.10 Mollusca and Echinodermata

Phylum Mollusca

Second largest animal phylum. Terrestrial or aquatic (marine or freshwater); organ-system level, bilateral, triploblastic, coelomate.

  • Body covered by a calcareous shell; unsegmented, with distinct head, muscular foot and visceral hump.
  • A soft spongy layer of skin, the mantle, forms over the visceral hump.
  • Mantle cavity (space between hump and mantle) contains feather-like gills (ctenidia), which have respiratory and excretory functions.
  • Anterior head region has sensory tentacles.
  • Mouth contains a file-like rasping organ for feeding, the radula.
  • Usually dioecious, oviparous, development indirect.

Phylum Echinodermata

  • Endoskeleton of calcareous ossicles, hence the name (spiny-bodied).
  • All are marine; organ-system level; triploblastic, coelomate.
  • Adults radially symmetrical; larvae bilaterally symmetrical.
  • Digestive system complete: mouth on the lower (ventral) side, anus on the upper (dorsal) side.
  • Most distinctive feature: water vascular system, which helps in locomotion, capture and transport of food, and respiration.
  • Excretory system is absent.
  • Sexes separate; reproduction sexual; fertilisation usually external; development indirect with free-swimming larva.

4.11 Hemichordata and Chordata

Phylum Hemichordata

  • Earlier a sub-phylum under Chordata; now placed as a separate phylum under non-chordata.
  • Small group of worm-like marine animals; organ-system level; bilateral, triploblastic, coelomate.
  • Body cylindrical: anterior proboscis, a collar and a long trunk.
  • Circulatory system open; respiration through gills.
  • Excretory organ: proboscis gland.
  • Sexes separate; fertilisation external; development indirect.
  • Examples: Balanoglossus, Saccoglossus.
Read onceOlder editions described a rudimentary notochord-like structure, the stomochord, in the collar region of hemichordates. It is not a true notochord, which is why the group is non-chordate.

Phylum Chordata: three fundamental features

Notochord+Dorsal hollow nerve cord+Paired pharyngeal gill slits

Chordates are bilaterally symmetrical, triploblastic, coelomate, organ-system level. They have a post-anal tail and a closed circulatory system.

4.13 Cyclostomata, Chondrichthyes and Osteichthyes

Class Cyclostomata

  • All living members are ectoparasites on some fishes.
  • Elongated body with 6–15 pairs of gill slits for respiration.
  • Sucking and circular mouth without jaws.
  • Body devoid of scales and paired fins.
  • Cranium and vertebral column cartilaginous; circulation closed.
  • Marine, but migrate to fresh water for spawning; they die within a few days after spawning. Larvae return to the ocean after metamorphosis.
  • Examples: Petromyzon (lamprey), Myxine (hagfish).

★ Quick revision

  1. Cellular level: Porifera; tissue level: Coelenterata and Ctenophora; organ level: Platyhelminthes; organ-system: the rest.
  2. Incomplete gut (single opening) in Platyhelminthes; open circulation in arthropods and molluscs; closed in annelids and chordates.
  3. Radial symmetry: coelenterates, ctenophores, adult echinoderms; sponges are mostly asymmetrical.
  4. Diploblastic animals have undifferentiated mesoglea between ectoderm and endoderm; triploblasty runs from Platyhelminthes to Chordata.
  5. Acoelomate: Platyhelminthes; pseudocoelomate: Aschelminthes; coelomates: Annelida onwards.
  6. Sponge water path: ostia → spongocoel → osculum; choanocytes line the canals; digestion intracellular.
  7. Cnidoblasts (nematocysts) for anchorage, defence and prey capture; metagenesis in Obelia.
  8. Ctenophores: eight rows of ciliated comb plates, well-marked bioluminescence, only sexual reproduction.
  9. Flame cells: Platyhelminthes; Planaria shows high regeneration.
  10. Aschelminthes are dioecious; often females longer than males; muscular pharynx.
  11. Parapodia in Nereis; nephridia in annelids; double ventral nerve cord.
  12. Arthropoda: largest phylum (over two-thirds of named species); Malpighian tubules; Limulus is a living fossil.
  13. Mollusca: second largest phylum; radula for feeding; gills in mantle cavity.
  14. Echinoderms: all marine, water vascular system, no excretory system, radial adult and bilateral larva.
  15. Hemichordata: proboscis, collar, trunk; proboscis gland for excretion; Balanoglossus.
  16. Notochord only in larval tail in urochordates; lifelong head-to-tail in cephalochordates (Branchiostoma).
  17. Cyclostomes: jawless, 6–15 pairs of gill slits, ectoparasites on fishes; die after spawning in fresh water.
  18. Cartilaginous fishes lack an air bladder and operculum; bony fishes have both. Crocodile heart is four-chambered; platypus is an oviparous mammal.
