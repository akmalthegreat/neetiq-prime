3.1 How plants are classified

Under the five-kingdom scheme, Kingdom Plantae is now much narrower than older systems. Fungi and members of Monera and Protista that have cell walls have been taken out of Plantae. Cyanobacteria, once called blue-green algae, are no longer treated as algae.

This chapter covers five groups of Plantae: Algae, Bryophytes, Pteridophytes, Gymnosperms and Angiosperms.

Three broad systems

SystemBasisKey point
ArtificialA few superficial morphological characters such as habit, colour, number and shape of leaves; Linnaeus used mainly the androecium structureGave equal weightage to vegetative and sexual characters. Rejected because vegetative characters are easily changed by the environment
NaturalNatural affinities among organisms, using external and internal features: ultrastructure, anatomy, embryology, phytochemistryBentham and Hooker gave such a system for flowering plants
PhylogeneticEvolutionary relationships among organismsAssumes members of the same taxon share a common ancestor; the system accepted today

Other sources of taxonomic evidence

Numerical taxonomy

  • Done using computers
  • Based on all observable characters
  • Each character gets a number/code and equal importance
  • Hundreds of characters handled together

Cytotaxonomy

  • Uses cytological data
  • Chromosome number, structure and behaviour

Chemotaxonomy

  • Uses the chemical constituents of the plant to settle confusions
Trap"Equal weightage to vegetative and sexual characters" = artificial system (Linnaeus). "Equal importance to every character, computer-based" = numerical taxonomy. Do not mix the two phrases.

3.2 Algae: general features

DefinitionAlgae are chlorophyll-bearing, simple, thalloid, autotrophic and largely aquatic (freshwater and marine) organisms.

Where they occur

  • Fresh and sea water; also on moist stones, soil and wood.
  • Some live in association with fungi (as lichens) and with animals (for example, on the sloth bear).

Range of form and size

  • Colonial: Volvox
  • Filamentous: Ulothrix, Spirogyra
  • Massive plant bodies: a few marine forms such as kelps
NEET HighlightAlgae carry out at least half of the total CO2 fixation on Earth by photosynthesis. Being photosynthetic, they raise dissolved oxygen in their surroundings and are primary producers, the base of aquatic food chains.

3.3 Reproduction in algae

Vegetative

  • By fragmentation
  • Each fragment grows into a thallus

Asexual

  • By different kinds of spores
  • Most common: zoospores (flagellated, motile); they germinate into new plants

Sexual

  • Fusion of two gametes
  • Three types, based on the size and motility of gametes

Types of sexual reproduction

3.4 The three classes of algae

Chlorophyceae: green algae

  • Unicellular, colonial or filamentous; grass-green because of chlorophyll a and b, located in chloroplasts.
  • Chloroplasts may be discoid, plate-like, reticulate, cup-shaped, spiral or ribbon-shaped, depending on the species.
  • Most have one or more pyrenoids (storage bodies) in the chloroplast, containing protein besides starch. Some store food as oil droplets.
  • Rigid wall: inner layer cellulose, outer layer pectose.
  • Asexual reproduction by flagellated zoospores made in zoosporangia; sexual reproduction iso-, aniso- or oogamous.
  • Examples: Chlamydomonas, Volvox, Ulothrix, Spirogyra, Chara.

Phaeophyceae: brown algae

  • Mostly marine. Size ranges from simple branched filaments (Ectocarpus) to profusely branched kelps that may reach 100 m.
  • Pigments: chlorophyll a, c, carotenoids and xanthophylls. Colour ranges from olive green to brown according to the amount of fucoxanthin.
  • Food stored as complex carbohydrates: laminarin or mannitol.
  • Cellulose wall, usually covered outside by a gelatinous coat of algin. The protoplast has plastids, a central vacuole and a nucleus.
  • Body usually has a holdfast (attachment), a stipe (stalk) and a frond (leaf-like, photosynthetic).
  • Zoospores are pear-shaped (pyriform) and biflagellate, with two unequal, laterally attached flagella. Gametes are also pyriform with two lateral flagella. Fusion occurs in water, or inside the oogonium in oogamous species.
  • Examples: Ectocarpus, Dictyota, Laminaria, Sargassum, Fucus.

Rhodophyceae: red algae

  • Red colour from r-phycoerythrin. Mostly marine, with more species in warmer waters.
  • Found near the well-lit surface and at great depths where little light reaches.
  • Thallus mostly multicellular, some with complex organisation.
  • Food: floridean starch, structurally very similar to amylopectin and glycogen.
  • Asexual reproduction by non-motile spores. Sexual reproduction is oogamous, with non-motile gametes and complex post-fertilisation development.
  • Examples: Polysiphonia, Porphyra, Gracilaria, Gelidium.

3.6 Bryophytes: general features

Bryophytes include mosses and liverworts, common in moist, shaded places in the hills.

Key ideaBryophytes are the amphibians of the plant kingdom: they live on land but need water for sexual reproduction.

Salient features

  • Grow in damp, humid, shaded places; help in plant succession on bare rocks and soil.
  • Body is more differentiated than in algae: thallus-like, prostrate or erect, fixed by unicellular or multicellular rhizoids.
  • No true roots, stem or leaves, though root-like, stem-like or leaf-like parts may be present. No vascular tissue.
  • Main plant body is haploid and produces gametes, so it is the gametophyte (dominant phase).
  • Sex organs are multicellular. The antheridium (male) makes biflagellate antherozoids; the flask-shaped archegonium (female) makes a single egg.
  • Antherozoids are released into water and reach the archegonium, where fertilisation forms a zygote.
  • The zygote does not undergo meiosis at once. It forms a multicellular sporophyte that is not free-living: it stays attached to the photosynthetic gametophyte and draws nourishment from it.
  • Some sporophyte cells undergo meiosis to form haploid spores, which grow into new gametophytes.

Importance

  • Low economic value. Some mosses are food for herbaceous mammals, birds and other animals.
  • Sphagnum gives peat, used as fuel, and as packing material for sending living material because it holds water well.
  • Mosses, along with lichens, are the first colonisers of rocks. They break down rock and make it fit for higher plants.
  • Moss mats on soil soften the impact of falling rain and prevent soil erosion.
TrapThe bryophyte sporophyte is diploid but dependent on the gametophyte. Do not call it "parasitic plant" or "free-living".

3.7 Liverworts and mosses

Liverworts (e.g. Marchantia)

  • Moist, shady habitats: stream banks, marshy ground, damp soil, tree bark, deep woods
  • Thallus dorsiventral, pressed close to the substrate
  • Leafy forms bear tiny leaf-like appendages in two rows
  • Asexual: fragmentation, or gemmae
  • Sex organs on the same or different thalli
  • Sporophyte: foot, seta, capsule; spores formed by meiosis in the capsule

Mosses (e.g. Funaria, Polytrichum, Sphagnum)

  • Gametophyte has two stages: protonema and leafy stage
  • Leafy stage: upright, slender axes with spirally arranged leaves; multicellular, branched rhizoids
  • Vegetative: fragmentation and budding in secondary protonema
  • Antheridia and archegonia at the apex of leafy shoots
  • Sporophyte (foot, seta, capsule) more elaborate than in liverworts; elaborate spore dispersal
DefinitionGemmae are green, multicellular, asexual buds formed in small receptacles called gemma cups on the thallus. They detach and grow into new plants. Protonema is the creeping, green, branched, often filamentous first stage of the moss gametophyte. It develops directly from a spore, and the leafy stage arises from the secondary protonema as a lateral bud.

  • Funaria: gametophyte with sporophyte; labels: capsule, seta, leaves, main axis, rhizoids. Sphagnum: gametophyte.

3.8 Pteridophytes

Include horsetails and ferns. Used in medicine and as soil-binders; often grown as ornamentals.

Key ideaPteridophytes are the first terrestrial plants with vascular tissues (xylem and phloem). The dominant body is the sporophyte.

Salient features

  • Found in cool, damp, shady places; some do well in sandy soil.
  • Sporophyte has true roots, stem and leaves, each with well-differentiated vascular tissue.
  • Leaves are small microphylls (Selaginella) or large macrophylls (ferns).
  • Sporangia are borne on leaf-like sporophylls. In some, sporophylls form compact strobili (cones): Selaginella, Equisetum.
  • Spore mother cells undergo meiosis to form spores. Spores grow into a small, multicellular, free-living, mostly photosynthetic, thalloid gametophyte called the prothallus.
  • The prothallus needs cool, damp, shady places, and fertilisation needs water to carry antherozoids to the archegonium. So pteridophytes are restricted to narrow geographical regions.
  • The zygote grows into the multicellular, well-differentiated sporophyte, the dominant phase.

Homosporous vs heterosporous

Homosporous

  • All spores are alike
  • The majority of pteridophytes

Heterosporous

  • Two kinds: megaspores (large) and microspores (small)
  • Selaginella, Salvinia
  • Megaspore → female gametophyte; microspore → male gametophyte
NEET HighlightIn heterosporous forms, the female gametophyte stays on the parent sporophyte for some time, and the zygote develops into a young embryo inside the female gametophyte. This is a precursor to the seed habit, an important step in evolution.

3.9 Gymnosperms

DefinitionGymnosperms (gymnos = naked, sperma = seed): ovules are not enclosed by an ovary wall and stay exposed before and after fertilisation, so the seeds are naked.

Vegetative features

  • Medium to tall trees and shrubs. The giant redwood Sequoia is one of the tallest tree species.
  • Roots are generally tap roots.
    • Pinus: roots have a fungal association called mycorrhiza.
    • Cycas: small specialised coralloid roots associated with N2-fixing cyanobacteria.
  • Stem unbranched (Cycas) or branched (Pinus, Cedrus).
  • Leaves simple or compound; Cycas has pinnate leaves that persist for a few years.
  • Leaves withstand extremes of temperature, humidity and wind. In conifers, needle-like leaves reduce surface area, and a thick cuticle and sunken stomata cut water loss.

Reproductive features

  • Heterosporous: haploid microspores and megaspores form in sporangia on sporophylls. The sporophylls are arranged spirally on an axis as lax or compact strobili (cones).
  • Male strobili (microsporangiate) bear microsporophylls. Each microspore becomes a highly reduced male gametophyte of a few cells, the pollen grain, formed inside the microsporangium.
  • Female strobili (macrosporangiate) bear megasporophylls with ovules. In the nucellus, the megaspore mother cell divides by meiosis to form four megaspores. One grows into a multicellular female gametophyte with two or more archegonia, kept within the megasporangium.
  • The ovule is the nucellus together with its protective envelopes.
  • Pollen is carried by air currents to the ovule opening. A pollen tube carries male gametes to the archegonia, so no water is needed for fertilisation.
  • The zygote becomes an embryo and the ovule becomes a seed, which stays uncovered.
ExceptionPinus: male and female cones on the same tree. Cycas: male cones and megasporophylls on different trees. Unlike bryophytes and pteridophytes, gymnosperm gametophytes are not free-living; they stay inside sporangia on the sporophyte.

3.10 Angiosperms and the big picture

  • Pollen grains and ovules develop inside specialised structures called flowers; seeds are enclosed in fruits.
  • A very large group found in a wide range of habitats.
  • Size ranges from the tiny, almost microscopic Wolffia to tall Eucalyptus trees (over 100 m).
  • Provide food, fodder, fuel, medicines and many other commercial products.
  • Two classes: dicotyledons (two cotyledons in the seed) and monocotyledons (one cotyledon).
NCERT diagrams
  • Angiosperms figure: (a) a dicotyledon and (b) a monocotyledon plant. Asked as: tell them apart from leaf venation and habit.
RememberAlgae are thalloid, with no true root, stem or leaf and no vascular tissue. Among land plants, only bryophytes have a haploid dominant phase. Free-living gametophytes are found in bryophytes and pteridophytes, but not in gymnosperms or angiosperms.

★ Quick revision

  1. Artificial system (Linnaeus): few superficial characters, mainly androecium; equal weightage to vegetative and sexual characters.
  2. Natural system: Bentham and Hooker, for flowering plants. Phylogenetic system: based on evolutionary relationships.
  3. Numerical taxonomy: computer-based, all observable characters, each given equal importance. Cytotaxonomy uses chromosomes; chemotaxonomy uses chemical constituents.
  4. Cyanobacteria (blue-green algae) are no longer counted as algae.
  5. Algae fix at least half of Earth's CO2; zoospores are the most common asexual spores.
  6. Isogamous: Ulothrix (flagellated), Spirogyra (non-flagellated). Anisogamous: Chlamydomonas. Oogamous: Volvox, Fucus.
  7. Agar: Gelidium, Gracilaria. Algin: brown algae. Carrageen: red algae. Chlorella: protein supplement for space travellers.
  8. Green algae: chl a, b; starch in pyrenoids; wall of cellulose (inner) and pectose (outer); 2–8 equal apical flagella.
  9. Brown algae: chl a, c + fucoxanthin; laminarin/mannitol; algin coat; holdfast, stipe, frond; 2 unequal lateral flagella; kelps up to 100 m.
  10. Red algae: chl a, d + r-phycoerythrin; floridean starch; no flagella; oogamous; can live at great depths.
  11. Bryophytes are the amphibians of the plant kingdom; the haploid gametophyte is dominant and the sporophyte depends on it.
  12. Marchantia reproduces asexually by gemmae in gemma cups; the sporophyte has a foot, seta and capsule.
  13. Moss gametophyte: protonema (from spore), then leafy stage (lateral bud). Sphagnum gives peat.
  14. Pteridophytes are the first vascular land plants; the gametophyte is the prothallus. Strobili are seen in Selaginella and Equisetum.
  15. Heterosporous: Selaginella, Salvinia, a precursor to the seed habit.
  16. Pteridophyte classes: Psilopsida (Psilotum), Lycopsida (Selaginella, Lycopodium), Sphenopsida (Equisetum), Pteropsida (Dryopteris, Pteris, Adiantum).
  17. Gymnosperms: Cycas has coralloid roots with N2-fixing cyanobacteria; Pinus has mycorrhiza; Sequoia is among the tallest trees. Pinus bears male and female cones on the same tree; Cycas bears them on different trees.
  18. Angiosperms: seeds enclosed in fruits; size ranges from Wolffia (smallest) to Eucalyptus (over 100 m).
