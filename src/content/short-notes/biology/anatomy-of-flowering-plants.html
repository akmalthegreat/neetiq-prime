6.1 Tissues and meristematic tissues

Anatomy is the study of the internal structure of plants. Cells are organised into tissues, and tissues into organs. Internal structure also differs between monocots and dicots, and shows adaptations to the environment.

Definition A tissue is a group of cells having a common origin and usually performing a common function.

Tissues are classified into two main groups on the basis of whether the cells can divide: meristematic (dividing) and permanent (non-dividing).

Meristems

Plant growth is largely restricted to specialised regions of active cell division called meristems.

MeristemLocationRole
Apical (primary)Tips of roots and shootsProduces primary tissues; root apical meristem occupies the root tip, shoot apical meristem the distant-most region of the stem axis
Intercalary (primary)Between mature tissuesOccurs in grasses; regenerates parts removed by grazing herbivores
Lateral (secondary)Mature regions of roots and shoots, mainly of plants with a woody axisCylindrical meristems that produce secondary tissues
  • During leaf formation and stem elongation, some cells "left behind" from the shoot apical meristem constitute the axillary bud. Axillary buds sit in the axils of leaves and can form a branch or a flower.
  • Apical and intercalary meristems are primary meristems: they appear early in the life of a plant and form the primary plant body.
  • The secondary (lateral) meristem appears later than the primary meristem. Examples: fascicular vascular cambium, interfascicular cambium and cork cambium.
  • After division in primary and secondary meristems, new cells become structurally and functionally specialised and lose the ability to divide. Such cells are permanent (mature) cells and form permanent tissues.
Trap Intercalary meristem is a primary meristem even though it lies between mature tissues. "Lateral" = secondary = cambia; never apical or intercalary.
Memory trick Grass grows back from the middle: grazing cuts the top, the intercalary meristem (inter = between) regrows it.

6.2 Permanent tissues: simple tissues

Permanent tissues having all cells similar in structure and function are simple tissues; those having different types of cells are complex tissues.

Simple tissues→Parenchyma·Collenchyma·Sclerenchyma

Parenchyma

  • Forms the major component within organs.
  • Cells generally isodiametric; may be spherical, oval, round, polygonal or elongated.
  • Thin cellulose walls; closely packed or with small intercellular spaces.
  • Functions: photosynthesis, storage and secretion.

Collenchyma

  • Occurs in layers below the epidermis in dicotyledonous plants, as a homogeneous layer or in patches.
  • Cells are thickened at the corners due to deposition of cellulose, hemicellulose and pectin.
  • Oval, spherical or polygonal; often contain chloroplasts (then they assimilate food).
  • Intercellular spaces absent.
  • Gives mechanical support to growing parts such as young stem and petiole of leaf.

Sclerenchyma

  • Long, narrow cells with thick, lignified walls having few or numerous pits; usually dead and without protoplasts.
  • Two types on the basis of form, structure, origin and development: fibres and sclereids. Provides mechanical support to organs.

Fibres

  • Thick-walled, elongated and pointed cells
  • Generally occur in groups in various parts of the plant

Sclereids

  • Spherical, oval or cylindrical, highly thickened dead cells
  • Very narrow lumen
  • Fruit walls of nuts; pulp of guava, pear, sapota; seed coats of legumes; leaves of tea

6.3 Complex tissue: xylem

Complex tissues are made of more than one type of cell working together as a unit. Xylem and phloem are the two complex tissues.

Xylem is the conducting tissue for water and minerals from roots to stem and leaves; it also gives mechanical strength. It has four elements: tracheids, vessels, xylem fibres and xylem parenchyma.

ElementKey featuresLiving?
TracheidsElongated, tube-like; thick lignified walls; tapering ends; inner wall layers have thickenings of varying formDead, no protoplasm
VesselsLong cylindrical tube made of many vessel members, each with lignified walls and a large central cavity; members connected by perforations in common wallsDead, no protoplasm
Xylem fibresHighly thickened walls, obliterated central lumen; septate or aseptateDead
Xylem parenchymaThin cellulose walls; store starch or fat and substances like tannins; ray parenchyma does radial conduction of waterLiving
  • In flowering plants, tracheids and vessels are the main water-transporting elements.
  • Gymnosperms lack vessels; the presence of vessels is a characteristic feature of angiosperms.

Protoxylem, metaxylem, endarch, exarch

Primary xylem is of two types: the first-formed protoxylem and the later-formed metaxylem.

Endarch (stems)

  • Protoxylem towards the centre (pith)
  • Metaxylem towards the periphery

Exarch (roots)

  • Protoxylem towards the periphery
  • Metaxylem towards the centre
Trap Of the four xylem elements, only xylem parenchyma is living. Tracheids, vessels and xylem fibres are dead.
Memory trick EXarch = protoxylem on the EXterior (roots). ENarch = protoxylem INside, near the pith (stems).

6.4 Complex tissue: phloem

Phloem transports food materials, usually from leaves to other parts of the plant.

Angiosperm phloem: sieve tube elements, companion cells, phloem parenchyma and phloem fibres. Gymnosperms have albuminous cells and sieve cells; they lack sieve tubes and companion cells.

Sieve tube elements

  • Long tube-like structures arranged longitudinally, associated with companion cells.
  • End walls are perforated in a sieve-like manner to form sieve plates.
  • A mature sieve element has peripheral cytoplasm and a large vacuole but lacks a nucleus.
  • Its functions are controlled by the nucleus of companion cells.

Companion cells

  • Specialised parenchymatous cells closely associated with sieve tube elements.
  • Sieve tube elements and companion cells are connected by pit fields on their common longitudinal walls.
  • Companion cells help maintain the pressure gradient in the sieve tubes.

Phloem parenchyma

  • Elongated, tapering, cylindrical cells with dense cytoplasm and nucleus; cellulose wall with pits through which plasmodesmatal connections exist.
  • Stores food and substances such as resins, latex and mucilage.
  • Phloem parenchyma is absent in most monocotyledons.

Phloem fibres (bast fibres)

  • Sclerenchymatous; much elongated, unbranched, with pointed, needle-like apices; quite thick walls; dead at maturity.
  • Generally absent in primary phloem but found in secondary phloem.
  • Phloem fibres of jute, flax and hemp are used commercially.

The first-formed primary phloem is protophloem; the later-formed is metaphloem.

6.5 The tissue system: epidermal tissue system

On the basis of structure and location, there are three tissue systems:

Epidermal·Ground (fundamental)·Vascular (conducting)

The epidermal tissue system forms the outermost covering of the whole plant body and comprises epidermal cells, stomata and epidermal appendages (trichomes and hairs).

Epidermis

  • Outermost layer of the primary plant body; elongated, compactly arranged cells forming a continuous layer.
  • Usually single-layered. Cells are parenchymatous with little cytoplasm lining the wall and a large vacuole.
  • The outside is often covered by a waxy thick cuticle, which prevents water loss. Cuticle is absent in roots.

Stomata

  • Present in the epidermis of leaves; regulate transpiration and gaseous exchange.
  • Each stoma has two bean-shaped guard cells enclosing the stomatal pore. In grasses, guard cells are dumb-bell shaped.
  • Outer walls of guard cells (away from the pore) are thin; inner walls (towards the pore) are highly thickened.
  • Guard cells have chloroplasts and regulate opening and closing of stomata.
  • A few epidermal cells near the guard cells become specialised in shape and size: subsidiary cells.
Definition Stomatal apparatus = stomatal pore + guard cells + surrounding subsidiary cells.

Epidermal appendages

Root hairs

  • Unicellular elongations of epidermal cells
  • Absorb water and minerals from soil

Trichomes (stem)

  • Usually multicellular
  • Branched or unbranched; soft or stiff; may be secretory
  • Help prevent water loss due to transpiration
Trap Guard-cell wall: inner wall (towards the pore) is thick, outer wall is thin. Root hairs are unicellular; shoot trichomes are usually multicellular.

6.6 Ground and vascular tissue systems

Ground tissue system

  • All tissues except epidermis and vascular bundles form the ground tissue. It consists of simple tissues: parenchyma, collenchyma and sclerenchyma.
  • In primary stems and roots, parenchyma is present in the cortex, pericycle, pith and medullary rays.
  • In leaves, the ground tissue is made of thin-walled, chloroplast-containing cells called mesophyll.

Vascular tissue system

Consists of the complex tissues xylem and phloem, which together form vascular bundles.

Open

  • Cambium present between phloem and xylem
  • Can form secondary xylem and phloem
  • Dicot stems

Closed

  • No cambium
  • Do not form secondary tissues
  • Monocots

Radial

  • Xylem and phloem on different radii, alternating
  • e.g. roots

Conjoint

  • Xylem and phloem on the same radius
  • Common in stems and leaves
  • Phloem usually only on the outer side of xylem
NEET Highlight
  • Dicot stem: conjoint, open, endarch.
  • Monocot stem: conjoint, closed.
  • Roots: radial, exarch.

6.7 Anatomy of dicot and monocot roots

Anatomy is studied in transverse sections (T.S.) of the mature zone of roots and the internodal region of stems.

Dicot root (T.S. of sunflower root)

  1. Epidermis: outermost layer; many cells protrude as unicellular root hairs.
  2. Cortex: several layers of thin-walled parenchyma with intercellular spaces.
  3. Endodermis: innermost layer of cortex; a single layer of barrel-shaped cells without intercellular spaces. Tangential as well as radial walls have water-impermeable, waxy suberin deposited as casparian strips.
  4. Pericycle: a few layers of thick-walled parenchymatous cells next to the endodermis. Initiation of lateral roots and of vascular cambium (during secondary growth) takes place here.
  5. Pith: small or inconspicuous.
  6. Conjunctive tissue: parenchyma lying between the xylem and phloem.
  7. Vascular bundles: usually two to four xylem and phloem patches (radial, exarch). Later a cambium ring develops between xylem and phloem.
Definition Stele = all tissues on the inner side of the endodermis: pericycle, vascular bundles and pith.

Monocot root

  • Similar to the dicot root in many respects: epidermis, cortex, endodermis, pericycle, vascular bundles and pith.
  • Usually more than six xylem bundles (polyarch), whereas the dicot root has fewer.
  • Pith is large and well developed.
  • Monocot roots do not undergo secondary growth.

6.8 Anatomy of the dicot stem

T.S. of a typical young dicotyledonous stem, from outside in:

  1. Epidermis: outermost protective layer, covered by a thin cuticle; may bear trichomes and a few stomata.
  2. Cortex: multiple layers between epidermis and pericycle, in three sub-zones:
    • Hypodermis: a few layers of collenchyma just below the epidermis, giving mechanical strength to the young stem.
    • Cortical layers: rounded, thin-walled parenchyma with conspicuous intercellular spaces.
    • Endodermis: innermost cortical layer; its cells are rich in starch grains, so it is also called the starch sheath.
  3. Pericycle: on the inner side of the endodermis and above the phloem, as semi-lunar patches of sclerenchyma.
  4. Medullary rays: a few layers of radially placed parenchyma between the vascular bundles.
  5. Vascular bundles: a large number arranged in a ring; the "ring" arrangement is characteristic of the dicot stem. Each bundle is conjoint, open and with endarch protoxylem.
  6. Pith: many rounded parenchyma cells with large intercellular spaces occupying the centre.
NEET Highlight
  • Starch sheath = endodermis of dicot stem.
  • Dicot stem pericycle = sclerenchyma caps over phloem; dicot root pericycle = thick-walled parenchyma.
  • Dicot stem hypodermis = collenchyma; monocot stem hypodermis = sclerenchyma.

6.9 Anatomy of the monocot stem

  • Sclerenchymatous hypodermis.
  • A large number of scattered vascular bundles, each surrounded by a sclerenchymatous bundle sheath.
  • A large, conspicuous parenchymatous ground tissue (no clear distinction into cortex, endodermis, pericycle, pith).
  • Vascular bundles are conjoint and closed.
  • Peripheral vascular bundles are generally smaller than the centrally located ones.
  • Phloem parenchyma is absent, and water-containing cavities are present within the vascular bundles.

6.10 Dorsiventral (dicot) leaf

A vertical section through the lamina shows three main parts: epidermis, mesophyll and vascular system.

Epidermis

  • Covers the upper surface (adaxial epidermis) and lower surface (abaxial epidermis); has a conspicuous cuticle.
  • The abaxial epidermis generally bears more stomata than the adaxial; the adaxial may even lack stomata.

Mesophyll

Tissue between upper and lower epidermis; made of parenchyma with chloroplasts; carries out photosynthesis. Two types of cells:

Palisade parenchyma

  • Adaxially placed
  • Elongated cells, arranged vertically and parallel to each other

Spongy parenchyma

  • Below palisade, extends to lower epidermis
  • Oval or round, loosely arranged cells
  • Numerous large spaces and air cavities

Vascular system

  • Vascular bundles are seen in the veins and midrib; their size depends on the size of the veins.
  • Veins vary in thickness in the reticulate venation of dicot leaves.
  • Vascular bundles are surrounded by a layer of thick-walled bundle sheath cells.
Memory trick Adaxial = up (facing the stem axis) → palisade, fewer stomata. Abaxial = below → spongy side, more stomata.

6.11 Isobilateral (monocot) leaf

Similar to the dorsiventral leaf in many ways, with these characteristic differences:

  • Stomata on both surfaces of the epidermis.
  • Mesophyll is not differentiated into palisade and spongy parenchyma.
  • Parallel venation is reflected in the near-similar sizes of vascular bundles (except in main veins) in vertical sections.

Bulliform cells (grasses)

  • Certain adaxial epidermal cells along the veins become large, empty, colourless cells called bulliform cells.
  • Turgid (water absorbed) → leaf surface is exposed. Flaccid (water stress) → leaves curl inwards to minimise water loss.
Water stress→Bulliform cells flaccid→Leaf curls inwards→Less water loss

★ Quick revision

  1. Tissue = group of cells with common origin, usually performing a common function.
  2. Apical + intercalary = primary meristems; lateral (fascicular, interfascicular, cork cambium) = secondary, cylindrical.
  3. Intercalary meristem: grasses, regrowth after grazing; axillary bud = cells left behind by shoot apical meristem.
  4. Parenchyma: isodiametric, thin cellulose walls; photosynthesis, storage, secretion.
  5. Collenchyma: corner thickening (cellulose, hemicellulose, pectin), no spaces, below epidermis in dicots.
  6. Sclereids: nuts, guava, pear, sapota pulp, legume seed coats, tea leaves.
  7. Xylem: tracheids, vessels, xylem fibres, xylem parenchyma; only parenchyma is living.
  8. Gymnosperms lack vessels; vessels characterise angiosperms.
  9. Endarch (stem): protoxylem towards pith; exarch (root): protoxylem towards periphery.
  10. Sieve tube: sieve plates, peripheral cytoplasm, no nucleus; controlled by companion cell nucleus.
  11. Companion cells maintain pressure gradient; gymnosperms have sieve cells + albuminous cells instead.
  12. Phloem parenchyma absent in most monocots; phloem fibres absent in primary phloem; jute, flax, hemp fibres.
  13. Three tissue systems: epidermal, ground (fundamental), vascular (conducting).
  14. Cuticle absent in roots; root hairs unicellular, shoot trichomes usually multicellular.
  15. Guard cells: bean-shaped (dumb-bell in grasses); inner wall thick, outer thin; have chloroplasts.
  16. Stomatal apparatus = pore + guard cells + subsidiary cells.
  17. Leaf ground tissue = mesophyll.
  18. Open bundle = cambium present (dicot stem); closed = no cambium (monocots); radial in roots; conjoint in stems and leaves.
  19. Root endodermis: barrel-shaped cells, suberin casparian strips; pericycle gives lateral roots.
  20. Stele = pericycle + vascular bundles + pith; conjunctive tissue lies between xylem and phloem.
  21. Dicot root 2–4 xylem patches, small pith; monocot root polyarch (>6), large pith, no secondary growth.
  22. Dicot stem: collenchymatous hypodermis, starch sheath, sclerenchymatous pericycle caps, ring of conjoint open endarch bundles.
  23. Monocot stem: sclerenchymatous hypodermis, scattered sheathed closed bundles, no phloem parenchyma, water cavities.
  24. Dorsiventral leaf: palisade (adaxial) + spongy; more stomata abaxially; thick-walled bundle sheath.
  25. Isobilateral leaf: stomata on both surfaces, undifferentiated mesophyll, bulliform cells curl leaf under water stress.
