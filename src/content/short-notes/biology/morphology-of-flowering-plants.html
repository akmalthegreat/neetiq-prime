5.1 The root

Angiosperms show huge diversity in external form, but every flowering plant is built from the same parts: roots, stems, leaves, flowers and fruits. The underground part is the root system; the part above ground is the shoot system. Morphology is the study of these external forms.

Three kinds of root systems

Tap root system

  • Typical of dicotyledons.
  • The radicle elongates directly to form the primary root, which grows into the soil.
  • It bears lateral roots of several orders: secondary, tertiary, and so on.
  • Primary root + its branches = tap root system, e.g. mustard.

Fibrous root system

  • Typical of monocotyledons.
  • The primary root is short-lived and is replaced by a large number of roots arising from the base of the stem.
  • Example: wheat.

Adventitious roots

  • Roots that arise from any part of the plant other than the radicle.
  • Examples: grass, Monstera, banyan tree.

Functions of the root system

  • Absorption of water and minerals from the soil.
  • Proper anchorage to the plant parts.
  • Storage of reserve food material.
  • Synthesis of plant growth regulators.
Trap "Synthesis of plant growth regulators" is a root function listed by NCERT. Students often tick only absorption and anchorage in a "which of these is a root function" question.

Modifications of root

Roots in some plants change their shape and structure to do jobs other than absorption and conduction of water and minerals.

FunctionModified rootNCERT examples
Food storageSwollen tap rootsCarrot, turnip
Food storageSwollen adventitious rootsSweet potato
SupportProp roots: hanging roots from branches that reach the soilBanyan tree
SupportStilt roots: supporting roots from the lower nodes of the stemMaize, sugarcane
RespirationPneumatophores: roots that come out of the ground and grow vertically upwardsRhizophora (swampy areas)
NEET Highlight
  • Pneumatophores help to get oxygen for respiration in plants growing in swampy areas such as Rhizophora.
  • Carrot and turnip store food in the tap root, but sweet potato stores it in an adventitious root. NEET pairs these as "odd one out".
  • Banyan: prop roots (from branches). Maize and sugarcane: stilt roots (from lower nodes of stem).
Exception Potato, ginger, turmeric, zaminkand and Colocasia are not roots. They are underground stems (they have nodes, internodes and buds). Sweet potato is a root.

5.2 The stem

Definition The stem is the ascending part of the axis bearing branches, leaves, flowers and fruits. It develops from the plumule of the embryo of a germinating seed.
  • The stem bears nodes (regions where leaves are borne) and internodes (portions between two nodes).
  • It bears buds, which may be terminal or axillary.
  • Generally green when young; later often becomes woody and dark brown.

Main functions

  • Spreading out branches bearing leaves, flowers and fruits
  • Conducting water, minerals and photosynthates

Extra functions in some plants

  • Storage of food
  • Support
  • Protection
  • Vegetative propagation
Key idea Root comes from the radicle; stem comes from the plumule. Presence of nodes, internodes and buds is how you tell an underground stem from a root.

Modifications of stem

  • Opuntia is a flattened stem, not a leaf; its leaves are reduced to spines.

5.3 The leaf

Definition The leaf is a lateral, generally flattened structure borne on the stem. It develops at the node and bears a bud in its axil. The axillary bud later develops into a branch.
  • Leaves originate from shoot apical meristems and are arranged in an acropetal order.
  • They are the most important vegetative organs for photosynthesis.

Parts of a typical leaf

PartKey points
Leaf baseAttaches leaf to the stem; may bear two small leaf-like lateral stipules. In monocots the leaf base expands into a sheath covering the stem partially or wholly. In some leguminous plants the leaf base becomes swollen: the pulvinus.
PetioleHolds the blade to light. Long, thin, flexible petioles let the blade flutter in wind, cooling the leaf and bringing fresh air to its surface.
Lamina (leaf blade)Green expanded part with veins and veinlets; a prominent central midrib. Veins give rigidity to the blade and act as channels of transport for water, minerals and food. Shape, margin, apex, surface and incision of the lamina vary between leaves.

Venation

Venation = arrangement of veins and veinlets in the lamina.

Reticulate

Veinlets form a network. Generally in dicotyledons.

Parallel

Veins run parallel to each other in the lamina. Characteristic of most monocotyledons.

Types of leaves

  • Simple leaf: lamina is entire, or if incised, the incisions do not touch the midrib.
  • Compound leaf: incisions reach up to the midrib, breaking the lamina into a number of leaflets.

Pinnately compound

Many leaflets on a common axis, the rachis, which represents the midrib. e.g. neem.

Palmately compound

Leaflets attached at a common point, the tip of the petiole. e.g. silk cotton.

NEET Highlight A bud is present in the axil of the petiole in both simple and compound leaves, but not in the axil of the leaflets of a compound leaf. This is how you tell a compound leaf from a twig bearing simple leaves.

Phyllotaxy

Phyllotaxy = pattern of arrangement of leaves on the stem or branch.

TypeLeaves per nodeExamples
AlternateA single leaf at each node, in alternate mannerChina rose, mustard, sunflower
OppositeA pair of leaves at each node, opposite each otherCalotropis, guava
WhorledMore than two leaves at a node, forming a whorlAlstonia
Memory trick Alstonia = All around (whorled). Calotropis and Guava "C-G face each other" (opposite). China rose, mustard, sunflower take turns (alternate).

Modifications of leaves

ModificationFunctionExamples
Leaf tendrilsClimbingPeas
SpinesDefenceCacti
Fleshy leavesFood storageOnion, garlic
Expanded green petiole (leaves small, short-lived)Petiole synthesises foodAustralian acacia

Trap In Australian acacia the leaves are small and short-lived; it is the petiole that expands, turns green and makes food. In Opuntia it is the stem. Both are arid-adapted, so NEET loves to swap them.

5.4 The inflorescence

Key idea A flower is a modified shoot in which the shoot apical meristem changes to a floral meristem. Internodes do not elongate and the axis gets condensed; the apex produces floral appendages laterally at successive nodes instead of leaves.
  • When a shoot tip transforms into a flower, the flower is always solitary.
  • Inflorescence = arrangement of flowers on the floral axis.
  • Two main types, depending on whether the apex develops into a flower or continues to grow.
FeatureRacemoseCymose
Main axisContinues to grow (unlimited)Terminates in a flower (limited growth)
Position of flowersBorne laterallyTerminal flower first, then lateral
Order of developmentAcropetal succession (oldest at base, youngest near apex)Basipetal order (oldest at tip / centre)
Memory trick Racemose Runs on (axis keeps growing) → Acropetal (towards Apex). Cymose is Capped by a flower → Basipetal (towards Base).

5.5 The flower

The flower is the reproductive unit in angiosperms, meant for sexual reproduction. A typical flower has four whorls arranged successively on the swollen end of the stalk (pedicel), called the thalamus or receptacle.

Calyx→Corolla→Androecium→Gynoecium
  • Calyx and corolla = accessory organs; androecium and gynoecium = reproductive organs.
  • In some flowers like lily, calyx and corolla are not distinct and are called perianth.
  • Bisexual: both androecium and gynoecium present. Unisexual: only stamens or only carpels.

Symmetry

TypeMeaningExamples
Actinomorphic (radial, ⊕)Can be divided into two equal radial halves in any radial plane through the centreMustard, datura, chilli
Zygomorphic (bilateral, %)Divisible into two similar halves in only one particular vertical planePea, gulmohur, bean, Cassia
Asymmetric (irregular)Cannot be divided into two similar halves by any vertical planeCanna
  • Trimerous, tetramerous, pentamerous: floral appendages in multiples of 3, 4 or 5 respectively.
  • Bracts = reduced leaves at the base of the pedicel. Flowers with bracts are bracteate; without bracts, ebracteate.

Position of floral parts on thalamus

TypeArrangementOvaryExamples
HypogynousGynoecium occupies the highest position; other parts below itSuperiorMustard, china rose, brinjal
PerigynousGynoecium in the centre; other parts on the rim of the thalamus almost at the same levelHalf-inferiorPlum, rose, peach
EpigynousMargin of thalamus grows upward, encloses the ovary completely and fuses with it; other parts arise above the ovaryInferiorGuava, cucumber, ray florets of sunflower
Trap Rose is perigynous (half-inferior), but china rose is hypogynous (superior). Guava and cucumber are epigynous. Brinjal (Solanaceae) is hypogynous.

5.5.1 Parts of a flower: calyx and corolla

Calyx (sepals)

  • Outermost whorl of the flower.
  • Sepals generally green, leaf-like; protect the flower in the bud stage.
  • Gamosepalous (sepals united) or polysepalous (sepals free).

Corolla (petals)

  • Petals usually brightly coloured to attract insects for pollination.
  • Gamopetalous (united) or polypetalous (free).
  • Shape: tubular, bell-shaped, funnel-shaped or wheel-shaped.

Aestivation

Definition Aestivation is the mode of arrangement of sepals or petals in a floral bud with respect to the other members of the same whorl.
TypeArrangementExamples
ValvateSepals or petals just touch one another at the margins without overlappingCalotropis
TwistedOne margin of each appendage overlaps that of the next, and so on (regular, one direction)China rose, lady's finger, cotton
ImbricateMargins overlap one another but not in any particular directionCassia, gulmohur
Vexillary / papilionaceousFive petals: the largest standard (vexillum) overlaps the two lateral wings, which overlap the two smallest anterior petals, the keelPea, bean
NEET Highlight
  • Vexillary order of overlap: standard → wings → keel (1 standard, 2 wings, 2 keel petals). The two keel petals are the smallest and anterior.
  • China rose appears again and again: twisted aestivation, monoadelphous stamens, axile placentation, hypogynous, alternate phyllotaxy.
Memory trick Valvate = Calotropis ("Very Close, just touching"). Twisted = China rose, Lady's finger, Cotton ("Twist the CLC"). Imbricate = Cassia, Gulmohur ("I Can't Guess the direction").
NCERT diagrams

5.5.2 Androecium and gynoecium

Androecium (stamens)

  • Each stamen represents the male reproductive organ: a stalk (filament) and an anther.
  • Each anther is usually bilobed, and each lobe has two chambers, the pollen-sacs, where pollen grains are produced.
  • A sterile stamen is called a staminode.
ConditionMeaningExample
EpipetalousStamens attached to petalsBrinjal
EpiphyllousStamens attached to perianthLily
PolyandrousStamens free—
MonoadelphousUnited into one bundleChina rose
DiadelphousUnited into two bundlesPea
PolyadelphousUnited into more than two bundlesCitrus
Unequal filamentsVariation in filament length within a flowerSalvia, mustard
Memory trick Count the bundles: Mono-china (1, china rose), Di-pea (2, pea), Poly-citrus (many, Citrus). Epipetalous = petal (brinjal); epiphyllous = perianth (lily).

Gynoecium (carpels)

  • The female reproductive part, made up of one or more carpels.
  • A carpel has three parts: stigma, style and ovary.
  • Ovary: enlarged basal part, bearing one or more ovules attached to a flattened, cushion-like placenta.
  • Style: elongated tube connecting ovary to stigma.
  • Stigma: usually at the tip of the style; the receptive surface for pollen grains.

Apocarpous

More than one carpel, free. e.g. lotus, rose.

Syncarpous

Carpels fused. e.g. mustard, tomato.

Key idea After fertilisation, ovules develop into seeds and the ovary matures into a fruit.

Placentation

Definition Placentation is the arrangement of ovules within the ovary.
TypeDescriptionExamples
MarginalPlacenta forms a ridge along the ventral suture; ovules borne on it in two rowsPea
AxilePlacenta is axial; ovules attached to it in a multilocular ovaryChina rose, tomato, lemon
ParietalOvules on the inner wall (peripheral part) of the ovary; ovary one-chambered but becomes two-chambered by a false septumMustard, Argemone
Free centralOvules on a central axis; septa absentDianthus, Primrose
BasalPlacenta at the base of the ovary; single ovule attached to itSunflower, marigold
Trap
  • Parietal ovary looks two-chambered, but the partition is a false septum (mustard, Argemone). Axile ovaries have true septa.
  • Free central and axile both have ovules on a central axis; the difference is septa absent in free central.
  • Mustard: parietal placentation, syncarpous, hypogynous. Tomato: axile, syncarpous.
Memory trick Ma-P (Marginal-Pea) · Axile = CTL (China rose, Tomato, Lemon) · Pa-MA (Parietal: Mustard, Argemone) · Free-DP (Dianthus, Primrose) · Basal = SM (Sunflower, Marigold).

5.6 The fruit

The fruit is a characteristic feature of flowering plants.

Definition A fruit is a mature or ripened ovary, developed after fertilisation. If a fruit forms without fertilisation of the ovary, it is a parthenocarpic fruit.
  • A fruit has a wall, the pericarp, and seeds. The pericarp may be dry or fleshy.
  • A thick, fleshy pericarp is differentiated into three layers:
Epicarp (outer)→Mesocarp (middle)→Endocarp (inner)

Drupe: mango and coconut

  • In mango and coconut the fruit is a drupe. Drupes develop from monocarpellary superior ovaries and are one-seeded.
LayerMangoCoconut
EpicarpThinOuter skin
MesocarpFleshy, edibleFibrous
EndocarpStony hardHard shell
NEET Highlight Edible part of mango = fleshy mesocarp. Coconut mesocarp is fibrous. Both are drupes from a monocarpellary superior ovary, each with a single seed.

5.7 The seed

Ovules develop into seeds after fertilisation. A seed is made of a seed coat and an embryo. The embryo has a radicle, an embryonal axis, and one cotyledon (wheat, maize) or two cotyledons (gram, pea).

Dicotyledonous seed

  • Seed coat has two layers: outer testa and inner tegmen.
  • Hilum: a scar on the seed coat through which the developing seed was attached to the fruit.
  • Micropyle: a small pore above the hilum.
  • Inside: an embryonal axis and two cotyledons; cotyledons are often fleshy and full of reserve food.
  • Radicle and plumule lie at the two ends of the embryonal axis.
  • In some seeds such as castor, the endosperm, formed as a result of double fertilisation, is a food-storing tissue.
  • In bean, gram and pea, endosperm is not present in the mature seed: non-endospermous.

Monocotyledonous seed (maize grain)

  • Monocot seeds are generally endospermic, but some, as in orchids, are non-endospermic.
  • In cereals such as maize the seed coat is membranous and generally fused with the fruit wall.
  • Endosperm is bulky and stores food.
  • The outer covering of the endosperm separates the embryo by a proteinous layer, the aleurone layer.
  • Embryo is small, situated in a groove at one end of the endosperm.
  • It has one large, shield-shaped cotyledon, the scutellum, and a short axis with plumule and radicle.
  • The plumule and radicle are enclosed in sheaths called coleoptile and coleorhiza respectively.
FeatureDicot seed (gram, pea, bean)Monocot seed (maize)
CotyledonsTwo, fleshy, store foodOne, shield-shaped scutellum
Seed coatTesta + tegmenMembranous, fused with fruit wall
EndospermAbsent in mature seed of bean, gram, pea (present in castor)Bulky; aleurone layer (proteinous) outside
SheathsNoneColeoptile (plumule), coleorhiza (radicle)
Trap
  • Castor is a dicot but endospermic. Orchids are monocots but non-endospermic. Classic exception pair.
  • Coleoptile covers the plumule, coleorhiza covers the radicle ("rhiza = root").
  • The aleurone layer is proteinous, not starchy.

5.8 Semi-technical description of a flowering plant

A plant is described briefly in simple scientific language and in a fixed sequence:

Habit→Vegetative: root, stem, leaves→Floral: inflorescence, flower parts→Floral diagram + floral formula

Symbols of the floral formula

SymbolMeaningSymbolMeaning
BrBracteate⚥Bisexual
KCalyx♂ / ♀Male / female
CCorolla⊕Actinomorphic
PPerianth%Zygomorphic
AAndroeciumG with line belowSuperior ovary
GGynoeciumG with line aboveInferior ovary
NEET Highlight
  • Fusion (cohesion within a whorl) is shown by enclosing the number in brackets, e.g. K(5).
  • Adhesion (fusion between whorls) is shown by a line drawn above the symbols of the floral parts, e.g. an arc over C and A for epipetalous stamens.
  • Underline below G = superior; bar above G = inferior. Students often reverse this.

Floral diagram

  • Gives the number of floral parts, their arrangement and their relation to one another.
  • Position of the mother axis with respect to the flower is shown by a dot on the top of the floral diagram.
  • Calyx, corolla, androecium and gynoecium are drawn in successive whorls: calyx outermost, gynoecium in the centre.
  • The floral formula also shows cohesion and adhesion within parts of whorls and between whorls.

★ Quick revision

  1. Tap root from radicle (dicots, mustard); fibrous root from stem base (monocots, wheat); adventitious from any part other than radicle (grass, Monstera, banyan).
  2. Root functions include synthesis of plant growth regulators.
  3. Storage roots: carrot, turnip (tap); sweet potato (adventitious).
  4. Prop roots: banyan. Stilt roots: maize, sugarcane. Pneumatophores: Rhizophora (O₂ for respiration).
  5. Stem develops from the plumule; has nodes, internodes, terminal and axillary buds.
  6. Underground storage stems (also perennation): potato, ginger, turmeric, zaminkand, Colocasia.
  7. Stem tendrils (from axillary buds): cucumber, pumpkin, watermelon, grapevine. Thorns: Citrus, Bougainvillea.
  8. Flattened stem: Opuntia; fleshy cylindrical stem: Euphorbia.
  9. Mint, jasmine: arching lateral branch; Pistia, Eichhornia: rosette + roots at nodes; banana, pineapple, Chrysanthemum: basal underground branches.
  10. Leaves arise from shoot apical meristem in acropetal order; pulvinus in some legumes; sheathing leaf base in monocots.
  11. Bud in axil of petiole, never in axil of leaflets. Pinnate: neem (rachis). Palmate: silk cotton.
  12. Phyllotaxy: alternate (china rose, mustard, sunflower), opposite (Calotropis, guava), whorled (Alstonia).
  13. Leaf tendril: pea; spines: cacti; fleshy leaves: onion, garlic; green petiole: Australian acacia; insectivorous: pitcher plant, Venus-fly trap.
  14. Racemose: axis grows, acropetal. Cymose: axis ends in flower, basipetal.
  15. Perianth: lily. Actinomorphic: mustard, datura, chilli. Zygomorphic: pea, gulmohur, bean, Cassia. Asymmetric: canna.
  16. Hypogynous (superior): mustard, china rose, brinjal. Perigynous (half-inferior): plum, rose, peach. Epigynous (inferior): guava, cucumber, ray florets of sunflower.
  17. Aestivation: valvate Calotropis; twisted china rose, lady's finger, cotton; imbricate Cassia, gulmohur; vexillary pea, bean.
  18. Anther bilobed, each lobe with two pollen-sacs; sterile stamen = staminode.
  19. Epipetalous: brinjal; epiphyllous: lily. Monoadelphous: china rose; diadelphous: pea; polyadelphous: Citrus. Unequal filaments: Salvia, mustard.
  20. Apocarpous: lotus, rose. Syncarpous: mustard, tomato.
  21. Placentation: marginal pea; axile china rose, tomato, lemon; parietal mustard, Argemone (false septum); free central Dianthus, Primrose; basal sunflower, marigold.
  22. Fruit = ripened ovary; parthenocarpic = without fertilisation. Drupe (mango, coconut) from monocarpellary superior ovary, one-seeded; mango mesocarp edible, coconut mesocarp fibrous.
  23. Castor: endospermic dicot. Bean, gram, pea: non-endospermous. Orchids: non-endospermic monocot.
  24. Maize: aleurone layer (proteinous), scutellum, coleoptile (plumule), coleorhiza (radicle).
