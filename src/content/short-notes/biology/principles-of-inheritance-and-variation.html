4.1 Mendel’s laws of inheritance

  • Gregor Mendel conducted hybridisation experiments on garden peas for seven years (1856–1863). He used large sampling, statistical analysis and mathematical logic.
  • He used 14 true-breeding pea varieties, i.e., 7 pairs of contrasting traits. True-breeding lines show stable trait inheritance over several generations of continuous self-pollination.

4.2 Inheritance of one gene

  • Tall × dwarf → F1 all tall; F1 selfed → F2 3 tall : 1 dwarf. Dwarf trait reappears unchanged in F2 — no blending.
  • Mendel’s ‘factors’ = genes, the units of inheritance. Slightly different forms of the same gene coding for a pair of contrasting traits = alleles.
  • Homozygous: TT or tt; heterozygous: Tt.
Test crossAn organism with the dominant phenotype (unknown genotype) is crossed with the recessive parent. Result 1 : 1 → it was heterozygous; all dominant → it was homozygous.

Mendel’s first two laws

Law of Dominance

  • Characters are controlled by discrete units called factors
  • Factors occur in pairs
  • In a dissimilar pair, one member dominates (dominant) the other (recessive)

Law of Segregation

  • Alleles do not blend; during gamete formation the two alleles of a pair segregate, so each gamete receives only one
  • Homozygous parent → one kind of gamete; heterozygous → two kinds in equal proportion
NEET HighlightThe law of segregation is the universal law (no exceptions). The law of dominance fails in incomplete dominance and co-dominance.

4.2.2 Incomplete dominance

  • Snapdragon (dog flower, Antirrhinum sp.): RR red × rr white → Rr pink. F2 = 1 red : 2 pink : 1 white; phenotypic ratio = genotypic ratio.
Concept of dominanceA modified allele may make a normal, less efficient, non-functional enzyme or no enzyme. If it makes a non-functional enzyme or none, the phenotype depends only on the unmodified allele → unmodified allele is dominant, modified is recessive.

4.2.2 Co-dominance and multiple alleles: ABO blood groups

  • In co-dominance the F1 resembles both parents.
  • ABO groups are controlled by gene I, which has three alleles IA, IB and i. IA and IB produce slightly different sugars on the RBC surface; i produces no sugar.
  • IA and IB are completely dominant over i; together they are co-dominant (blood group AB).
  • Three alleles → six genotypes, four phenotypes. ABO is also an example of multiple alleles (more than two alleles for one character), which can be found only when a population is studied.

4.3.1 Chromosomal theory of inheritance

  • Mendel’s work (published 1865) remained unrecognised till 1900: poor communication, “discrete factors” clashed with apparently continuous variation, mathematics in biology was new, and he had no physical proof of factors.
  • In 1900, de Vries, Correns and von Tschermak independently rediscovered Mendel’s results.
  • Walter Sutton and Theodore Boveri noted that chromosome behaviour parallels gene behaviour; Sutton united chromosomal segregation with Mendelian principles → chromosomal theory of inheritance.
  • Experimental verification: Thomas Hunt Morgan with Drosophila melanogaster.
Why DrosophilaGrows on simple synthetic medium; life cycle about two weeks; one mating gives many progeny; clear sex differentiation; many hereditary variations visible under low-power microscopes.

4.3.2 Linkage and recombination

  • Morgan crossed yellow-bodied, white-eyed females with brown-bodied, red-eyed males; the F2 ratio deviated very significantly from 9:3:3:1.
  • When two genes are on the same chromosome, parental combinations are much more frequent than non-parental ones.
  • Morgan coined linkage (physical association of genes on a chromosome) and recombination (generation of non-parental combinations).

Genetic mapsMorgan’s student Alfred Sturtevant used recombination frequency as a measure of distance between genes and mapped their positions. Genetic maps were the starting point for genome sequencing, e.g. the Human Genome Project.
TrapHigher recombination % = genes farther apart (loosely linked). The cross was yellow-bodied white-eyed female × brown-bodied red-eyed male.

4.4 Polygenic inheritance

  • Traits controlled by three or more genes; also influenced by environment; e.g. human skin colour, human height.
  • Skin colour with three genes A, B, C: AABBCC darkest, aabbcc lightest, three dominant + three recessive alleles = intermediate. The number of each type of allele determines darkness or lightness.

4.5 Pleiotropy

  • A single gene exhibits multiple phenotypic expressions, usually through its effect on metabolic pathways.
  • Example: phenylketonuria — single gene mutation in the enzyme phenylalanine hydroxylase → mental retardation and reduced hair and skin pigmentation.
Memory trickPolygenic = many genes → one trait; Pleiotropy = one gene → many traits.

4.6 Sex determination

  • Henking (1891) traced a nuclear structure through spermatogenesis in insects; 50% of sperms received it. He named it the X body; it was later shown to be the X-chromosome.

Humans23 pairs: 22 pairs autosomes + one pair sex chromosomes. Males produce 50% X and 50% Y sperms; females produce only X ova. The genetic makeup of the sperm determines the sex; probability of male or female is 50% each.

Honey bee: haplodiploid system

  • Fertilised egg → female (queen or worker), diploid, 32 chromosomes. Unfertilised egg → male (drone) by parthenogenesis, haploid, 16 chromosomes.
  • Males produce sperms by mitosis, have no father and cannot have sons, but have a grandfather and can have grandsons.
TrapIn birds the female decides sex (ZW). In grasshopper both sexes differ in chromosome number (XO male has one less). Drone sperms are made by mitosis, not meiosis.

4.7 Mutation

  • Mutation alters DNA sequences, changing genotype and phenotype. With recombination, it is a source of variation.
  • Loss (deletion) or gain (insertion/duplication) of DNA segments → chromosomal aberrations, commonly seen in cancer cells.
  • Change in a single base pair = point mutation, e.g. sickle cell anaemia. Deletions/insertions of base pairs → frame-shift mutations.
  • Agents causing mutation = mutagens, e.g. UV radiation.

4.8.1 Mendelian disorders

Caused by alteration or mutation of a single gene: haemophilia, cystic fibrosis, sickle-cell anaemia, colour blindness, phenylketonuria, thalassemia etc.

DisorderInheritanceKey facts (NCERT)
HaemophiliaSex (X)-linked recessiveA protein of the clotting cascade is affected → non-stop bleeding from a simple cut. Passed from carrier female to sons. Haemophilic female is extremely rare (mother must be carrier and father haemophilic). Queen Victoria was a carrier.
Sickle-cell anaemiaAutosomal recessiveAlleles HbA, HbS; only HbSHbS diseased; HbAHbS = carriers (sickle-cell trait). Glutamic acid → valine at the 6th position of β-globin; codon GAG → GUG. Under low O2 tension, Hb polymerises → RBC becomes sickle shaped.
PhenylketonuriaAutosomal recessive (inborn error of metabolism)Lacks enzyme converting phenylalanine → tyrosine; phenylalanine → phenylpyruvic acid; accumulation in brain → mental retardation; excreted in urine (poor kidney absorption)
ThalassemiaAutosomal recessiveMutation/deletion → reduced synthesis of α or β globin → anaemia. α-thalassemia: genes HBA1 and HBA2 on chromosome 16 (four genes; more affected → less α-globin). β-thalassemia: gene HBB on chromosome 11.
Colour blindnessSex (X)-linked recessiveDefect in red or green cone; occurs in about 8% of males and 0.4% of females. Son of a carrier woman has a 50% chance; a daughter is colour blind only if mother is a carrier and father colour blind.
NEET HighlightThalassemia is a quantitative problem (too few globin molecules); sickle-cell anaemia is a qualitative problem (incorrectly functioning globin).
TrapHaemophilia and colour blindness are X-linked; sickle-cell, thalassemia, PKU are autosomal. α-thalassemia = chromosome 16; β = chromosome 11.

4.8.2 Chromosomal disorders

  • Caused by absence, excess or abnormal arrangement of chromosomes.
  • Failure of segregation of chromatids → gain or loss of chromosomes = aneuploidy (trisomy / monosomy).
  • Failure of cytokinesis after telophase → increase in a whole set = polyploidy, often seen in plants.
SyndromeKaryotypeFeatures (NCERT)
