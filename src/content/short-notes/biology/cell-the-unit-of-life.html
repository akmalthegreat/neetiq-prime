8.1 What is a cell?

A piece of rock or wood shows no life; anything alive is built of cells. Some organisms are a single cell (unicellular), others are made of many cells (multicellular).

Definition The cell is the fundamental structural and functional unit of all living organisms.
  • Unicellular organisms are capable of (i) independent existence and (ii) performing the essential functions of life.
  • Anything less than a complete structure of a cell does not ensure independent living. That is why the cell is the unit of life.

Who saw what first

ScientistContribution
Anton Von LeeuwenhoekFirst saw and described a live cell
Robert BrownLater discovered the nucleus (described it in 1831)

The invention of the microscope, and later the electron microscope, revealed the structural details of the cell.

8.2 Cell theory

YearScientistWhat they said
1838Matthias Schleiden, a German botanistExamined many plants; all plants are composed of different kinds of cells which form the tissues of the plant.
1839Theodore Schwann, a zoologist (NCERT calls him British)Studied animal cells; reported a thin outer layer, now called the plasma membrane. From plant tissues he concluded that the presence of a cell wall is a unique character of plant cells.
1838–39Schleiden + SchwannHypothesis: bodies of animals and plants are composed of cells and products of cells; together they formulated the cell theory.
1855Rudolf VirchowFirst explained that cells divide and new cells are formed from pre-existing cells — Omnis cellula-e cellula. He modified the hypothesis into its final form.
Gap in the original theory Schleiden and Schwann's cell theory did not explain how new cells were formed. Virchow filled this gap.
Cell theory as understood today
  1. All living organisms are composed of cells and products of cells.
  2. All cells arise from pre-existing cells.
Schleiden 1838 (plants)→Schwann 1839 (animals, plasma membrane)→Virchow 1855 (Omnis cellula-e cellula)
Memory trick Alphabet and calendar agree: Schleiden (38, botanist) comes before Schwann (39, zoologist); Virchow comes last (55) and gives the "Very last" missing piece — origin of new cells.

8.3 An overview of the cell

Onion peel cell (plant)

  • Distinct cell wall as the outer boundary
  • Cell membrane just within it

Human cheek cell (animal)

  • Outer membrane is the delimiting structure
  • No cell wall
  • Inside each cell is a dense, membrane-bound nucleus. The nucleus contains the chromosomes, which in turn contain the genetic material, DNA.
  • Cells with membrane-bound nuclei are eukaryotic; cells lacking a membrane-bound nucleus are prokaryotic.
  • In both, a semi-fluid matrix, the cytoplasm, fills the cell. The cytoplasm is the main arena of cellular activities — reactions here keep the cell in the 'living state'.
  • Eukaryotic cells also have membrane-bound organelles: endoplasmic reticulum (ER), Golgi complex, lysosomes, mitochondria, microbodies and vacuoles. Prokaryotes lack such organelles.
NEET Highlight
  • Ribosomes are non-membrane-bound organelles found in all cells — eukaryotic as well as prokaryotic.
  • In a eukaryotic cell, ribosomes occur in the cytoplasm, on the rough ER, and also inside chloroplasts (in plants) and mitochondria.
  • Animal cells have another non-membrane-bound organelle, the centrosome, which helps in cell division.

8.4 Prokaryotic cells

Prokaryotes: bacteria, blue-green algae, mycoplasma and PPLO (Pleuro Pneumonia Like Organisms). They are generally smaller and multiply more rapidly than eukaryotic cells.

  • Wide variety of shapes and size, but four basic shapes: bacillus (rod-like), coccus (spherical), vibrio (comma-shaped) and spirillum (spiral).
  • Despite varied shapes and functions, the organisation of the prokaryotic cell is fundamentally similar.
  • All prokaryotes have a cell wall surrounding the cell membrane, except in mycoplasma.
  • The fluid matrix filling the cell is the cytoplasm.
  • There is no well-defined nucleus; the genetic material is basically naked, not enveloped by a nuclear membrane. Genomic DNA = single chromosome / circular DNA.
  • No organelles like those of eukaryotes, except ribosomes.

Plasmids

  • Besides genomic DNA, many bacteria have small circular DNA outside the genomic DNA, called plasmids.
  • Plasmid DNA confers certain unique phenotypic characters, e.g. resistance to antibiotics.
  • Plasmid DNA is used to monitor bacterial transformation with foreign DNA (biotechnology).

Unique to prokaryotes

  • Inclusions (reserve material lying free in the cytoplasm).
  • Mesosome — a specialised differentiated form of the cell membrane, essentially infoldings of the cell membrane — is characteristic of prokaryotes.

8.4.1 Cell envelope and its modifications

Most prokaryotic cells, particularly bacteria, have a chemically complex cell envelope.

Glycocalyx (outermost)→Cell wall→Plasma membrane (innermost)
  • The envelope is a tightly bound three-layered structure: outermost glycocalyx, then cell wall, then plasma membrane.
  • Each layer has a distinct function, but they act together as a single protective unit.

Gram staining

Bacteria are classified as Gram positive or Gram negative on the basis of differences in their cell envelopes and their response to the staining procedure developed by Gram.

Gram positive

  • Take up the Gram stain

Gram negative

  • Do not get stained

The three layers

LayerKey points
GlycocalyxDiffers in composition and thickness among bacteria. Loose sheath = slime layer; thick and tough = capsule.
Cell wallDetermines the shape of the cell and gives strong structural support to prevent the bacterium from bursting or collapsing.
Plasma membraneSemi-permeable; interacts with the outside world; structurally similar to that of eukaryotes.

Mesosome

  • Formed by extensions of the plasma membrane into the cell, as vesicles, tubules and lamellae.
  • Functions: cell wall formation, DNA replication and distribution to daughter cells, respiration, secretion, increasing the surface area of the plasma membrane and its enzymatic content.
NEET Highlight In some prokaryotes such as cyanobacteria, other membranous extensions into the cytoplasm, called chromatophores, contain pigments.

Surface structures

  • Bacteria may be motile or non-motile. Motile ones have thin filamentous extensions from the cell wall, the flagella; number and arrangement of flagella vary.
  • The bacterial flagellum has three parts: filament, hook and basal body. The filament is the longest part and extends from the cell surface to the outside.

Pili

  • Elongated tubular structures
  • Made of a special protein

Fimbriae

  • Small bristle-like fibres sprouting out of the cell
  • In some bacteria, help attach to rocks in streams and to host tissues
Trap Pili and fimbriae are surface structures but play no role in motility. Only flagella move the bacterium.
Memory trick Flagellum parts from outside in: F-H-B — "Fish Hook on a Boat": Filament, Hook, Basal body.

8.4.2 Ribosomes and inclusion bodies

  • In prokaryotes, ribosomes are associated with the plasma membrane of the cell.
  • Size about 15 nm × 20 nm; two subunits, 50S and 30S, which together form the 70S prokaryotic ribosome.
  • Ribosomes are the site of protein synthesis.
Polysome Several ribosomes attached to a single mRNA form a chain called a polyribosome or polysome. The ribosomes of a polysome translate the mRNA into proteins.

Inclusion bodies

  • Reserve material in prokaryotic cells is stored in the cytoplasm as inclusion bodies.
  • They are not bound by any membrane and lie free in the cytoplasm, e.g. phosphate granules, cyanophycean granules and glycogen granules.
  • Gas vacuoles are found in blue-green and purple and green photosynthetic bacteria.
Trap 70S = 50S + 30S, not 80S. Svedberg units are not additive (they depend on shape and density, not just mass).

8.5 Eukaryotic cells

Eukaryotes include all the protists, plants, animals and fungi.

  • Extensive compartmentalisation of cytoplasm through membrane-bound organelles.
  • An organised nucleus with a nuclear envelope.
  • A variety of complex locomotory and cytoskeletal structures.
  • Genetic material organised into chromosomes.

8.5.1 Cell membrane

Its detailed structure was studied only after the advent of the electron microscope in the 1950s.

Composition

  • Chemical studies on human red blood cells (RBCs) showed the membrane is mainly made of lipids arranged in a bilayer.
  • Lipids are arranged with their polar heads towards the outer sides and hydrophobic tails towards the inner part — this keeps the non-polar tails of saturated hydrocarbons away from the aqueous environment.
  • The lipid component is mainly phosphoglycerides.
  • Membranes also contain protein and carbohydrate. The protein : lipid ratio varies a lot between cell types.
NEET Highlight Human RBC membrane: approximately 52 per cent protein and 40 per cent lipids.

Membrane proteins

Peripheral proteins

  • Lie on the surface of the membrane
  • Easier to extract

Integral proteins

  • Partially or totally buried in the membrane
  • Harder to extract

The classification into integral or peripheral is based on the ease of extraction.

Fluid mosaic model

Definition Singer and Nicolson (1972) proposed the widely accepted fluid mosaic model: the quasi-fluid nature of lipid enables lateral movement of proteins within the overall bilayer. The ability to move within the membrane is measured as its fluidity.

The fluid nature is important for cell growth, formation of intercellular junctions, secretion, endocytosis, cell division etc.

Transport across the membrane

One of the most important functions of the plasma membrane is transport of molecules across it. The membrane is selectively permeable to some molecules present on either side.

8.5.2 Cell wall

A non-living rigid structure forming an outer covering for the plasma membrane of fungi and plants.

Functions

  • Gives shape to the cell; protects from mechanical damage and infection.
  • Helps in cell-to-cell interaction.
  • Provides a barrier to undesirable macromolecules.

Composition

Algae

  • Cellulose, galactans, mannans
  • Minerals like calcium carbonate

Other plants

  • Cellulose, hemicellulose, pectins
  • Proteins

Layers

LayerKey points

Middle lamellaMainly calcium pectate; holds or glues neighbouring cells together.

8.5.3 Endomembrane system

Each membranous organelle is distinct in structure and function, but several work in a coordinated way.

Definition Endomembrane system = endoplasmic reticulum (ER), Golgi complex, lysosomes and vacuoles.
Trap Mitochondria, chloroplasts and peroxisomes are not part of the endomembrane system, because their functions are not coordinated with the above components.

A. Endoplasmic reticulum (ER)

  • EM studies show a network or reticulum of tiny tubular structures scattered in the cytoplasm.
  • ER divides the intracellular space into two compartments: luminal (inside ER) and extra-luminal (cytoplasm).

Rough ER (RER)

  • Ribosomes attached to outer surface
  • Frequent in cells actively involved in protein synthesis and secretion
  • Extensive and continuous with the outer membrane of the nucleus

Smooth ER (SER)

  • No ribosomes
  • Major site for synthesis of lipid
  • In animal cells, lipid-like steroidal hormones are made in SER

B. Golgi apparatus

  • Camillo Golgi (1898) first observed densely stained reticular structures near the nucleus, later named Golgi bodies.
  • Made of many flat, disc-shaped sacs or cisternae of 0.5 µm to 1.0 µm diameter, stacked parallel to each other; the number of cisternae per complex varies.
  • Cisternae are concentrically arranged near the nucleus with a distinct convex cis (forming) face and a concave trans (maturing) face.
  • The cis and trans faces are entirely different but interconnected.

Cis face

  • Convex
  • Forming face
  • Receives vesicles from ER

Trans face

  • Concave
  • Maturing face
  • Releases packaged material

Functions of Golgi

  • Principally packaging materials, to be delivered either to intracellular targets or secreted outside the cell.
  • Vesicles from the ER fuse with the cis face and move towards the maturing face — this is why the Golgi apparatus stays in close association with the ER.
  • Many proteins made by ribosomes on the ER are modified in the Golgi cisternae before release from the trans face.
  • Golgi apparatus is the important site of formation of glycoproteins and glycolipids.
Protein made on RER→ER vesicle→Cis face (fusion)→Modified in cisternae→Trans face→Secretion / lysosome

C. Lysosomes

  • Membrane-bound vesicular structures formed by the process of packaging in the Golgi apparatus.
  • Very rich in almost all types of hydrolytic enzymes (hydrolases — lipases, proteases, carbohydrases), optimally active at acidic pH.
  • These enzymes can digest carbohydrates, proteins, lipids and nucleic acids.

D. Vacuoles

  • Membrane-bound space in the cytoplasm containing water, sap, excretory products and other materials not useful for the cell.
  • Bound by a single membrane called the tonoplast.
  • In plant cells vacuoles can occupy up to 90 per cent of the volume of the cell.
  • In plants, the tonoplast transports ions and other materials against concentration gradients into the vacuole, so their concentration is significantly higher in the vacuole than in the cytoplasm.
Vacuole typeOrganismFunction
Contractile vacuoleAmoebaOsmoregulation and excretion
Food vacuoleMany cells, e.g. protistsFormed by engulfing food particles
Central (sap) vacuolePlant cellsStorage; up to 90% of cell volume
Memory trick Endomembrane = "Every Good Little Vesicle": ER, Golgi, Lysosome, Vacuole. The Mitochondria, Chloroplasts and Peroxisomes ("MCP") stay out.

8.5.4 Mitochondria

  • Unless specifically stained, mitochondria are not easily visible under the microscope.
  • Number per cell varies with its physiological activity; shape and size also vary considerably.
  • Typically sausage-shaped or cylindrical, diameter 0.2–1.0 µm (average 0.5 µm) and length 1.0–4.1 µm.

Structure

  • Double membrane-bound: outer and inner membranes divide the lumen into two aqueous compartments — the outer compartment and the inner compartment.
  • The inner compartment is the matrix.
  • The outer membrane forms the continuous limiting boundary of the organelle.
  • The inner membrane forms infoldings called cristae towards the matrix; cristae increase the surface area.
  • The two membranes have their own specific enzymes linked to mitochondrial function.

Functions

  • Sites of aerobic respiration; produce cellular energy as ATP — hence 'power houses' of the cell.
NEET Highlight The matrix has a single circular DNA molecule, a few RNA molecules, ribosomes (70S) and the components needed for protein synthesis. Mitochondria divide by fission.
NCERT diagrams

Chloroplasts

  • Majority of chloroplasts of green plants are found in the mesophyll cells of leaves.
  • Lens-shaped, oval, spherical, discoid or even ribbon-like; length 5–10 µm, width 2–4 µm.
  • Number varies from 1 per cell in Chlamydomonas (a green alga) to 20–40 per cell in the mesophyll.

  • Space limited by the inner membrane = stroma.
  • Organised flattened membranous sacs in the stroma = thylakoids.
  • Thylakoids are stacked like piles of coins called grana (singular: granum).
  • Flat membranous tubules called stroma lamellae connect the thylakoids of different grana.
  • The thylakoid membrane encloses a space called the lumen.

Stroma contains

  • Enzymes for synthesis of carbohydrates and proteins
  • Small, double-stranded circular DNA
  • Ribosomes

Thylakoids contain

  • Chlorophyll pigments

8.5.6 Ribosomes

  • Granular structures first observed under the electron microscope as dense particles by George Palade (1953).
  • Composed of ribonucleic acid (RNA) and proteins; not surrounded by any membrane.
  • Eukaryotic ribosomes are 80S; prokaryotic ribosomes are 70S.

8.5.7 Cytoskeleton, cilia and flagella

Cytoskeleton

Definition An elaborate network of filamentous proteinaceous structures in the cytoplasm made of microtubules, microfilaments and intermediate filaments.

Functions: mechanical support, motility and maintenance of the shape of the cell.

Cilia and flagella (8.5.8)

  • Hair-like outgrowths of the cell membrane.
  • Cilia: small; work like oars, moving either the cell or the surrounding fluid.
  • Flagella: comparatively longer; responsible for cell movement.
  • Prokaryotic bacteria also have flagella, but these are structurally different from eukaryotic flagella.

Ultrastructure (electron microscope)

  • Covered with the plasma membrane.
  • The core, the axoneme, has many microtubules running parallel to the long axis.
  • Axoneme usually has nine pairs of doublets of radially arranged peripheral microtubules and a pair of centrally located microtubules — the 9 + 2 array.
  • The central tubules are connected by bridges and enclosed by a central sheath.
  • The central sheath is connected to one tubule of each peripheral doublet by a radial spoke — so there are nine radial spokes.
  • Peripheral doublets are also interconnected by linkers.
  • Both cilium and flagellum emerge from centriole-like structures called basal bodies.
Trap Count the microtubules: 9 doublets × 2 + 2 central = 20 in a 9 + 2 axoneme; a centriole has 9 triplets × 3 = 27 and no central pair. Radial spokes: 9 in both.
NCERT diagrams
  • Section of cilia/flagella: (a) electron micrograph (b) cross-section showing inner structure — labels to know: plasma membrane, peripheral doublet, central microtubules, central sheath, radial spoke, interdoublet bridge/linker; asked as: 9 + 2 arrangement, number of radial spokes.

8.5.9 Centrosome and centrioles

  • Centrosome is an organelle usually containing two cylindrical centrioles, surrounded by amorphous pericentriolar materials.
  • The two centrioles lie perpendicular to each other; each has a cartwheel organisation.
  • Made of nine evenly spaced peripheral fibrils of tubulin protein; each peripheral fibril is a triplet; adjacent triplets are linked.
  • The central part of the proximal region is also proteinaceous, called the hub, connected to the peripheral triplets by radial spokes made of protein.
NEET Highlight — functions Centrioles form the basal body of cilia or flagella, and the spindle fibres that give rise to the spindle apparatus during cell division in animal cells.

Axoneme (cilium / flagellum)

  • 9 peripheral doublets
  • 2 central microtubules in a sheath
  • Covered by plasma membrane

Centriole / basal body

  • 9 peripheral triplets (tubulin)
  • Central proteinaceous hub, no central tubules
  • Not membrane-bound

8.5.10 Nucleus

  • First described by Robert Brown as early as 1831.
  • The material of the nucleus stained by basic dyes was named chromatin by Flemming.
  • The interphase nucleus (non-dividing cell) has highly extended, elaborate nucleoprotein fibres (chromatin), a nuclear matrix, and one or more spherical nucleoli.

Nuclear envelope

  • Two parallel membranes with a space of 10 to 50 nm between them, the perinuclear space; it forms a barrier between nuclear material and cytoplasm.
  • The outer membrane is usually continuous with the ER and also bears ribosomes.
  • At many places the envelope is interrupted by minute nuclear pores, formed by the fusion of its two membranes.
  • Nuclear pores allow movement of RNA and protein molecules in both directions between nucleus and cytoplasm.

Number of nuclei

  • Normally one nucleus per cell, but variations in number are frequently observed (some cells have more than one nucleus).
Exception — cells without nucleus Some mature cells even lack a nucleus, e.g. erythrocytes of many mammals and sieve tube cells of vascular plants.

Nucleoplasm and nucleolus

  • The nuclear matrix or nucleoplasm contains the nucleolus and chromatin.
  • Nucleoli are spherical; their content is continuous with the rest of the nucleoplasm because the nucleolus is not membrane-bound.
  • The nucleolus is the site of active ribosomal RNA (rRNA) synthesis.
  • Larger and more numerous nucleoli occur in cells actively carrying out protein synthesis.

Chromatin and chromosomes

  • Interphase nucleus: loose, indistinct network of nucleoprotein fibres = chromatin. During cell division, structured chromosomes are seen in place of the nucleus.
  • Chromatin contains DNA and some basic proteins called histones, some non-histone proteins and also RNA.
  • A single human cell has approximately two metre long thread of DNA distributed among its forty-six (23 pairs) chromosomes.
  • Every chromosome has a primary constriction or centromere, on the sides of which are disc-shaped structures called kinetochores.

★ Quick revision

  2. Schleiden 1838 (botanist, plants), Schwann 1839 (zoologist, plasma membrane; cell wall unique to plant cells).

  11. Inclusion bodies (no membrane): phosphate, cyanophycean, glycogen granules; gas vacuoles in blue-green, purple and green photosynthetic bacteria.
  12. Plant cell: wall, plastids, large central vacuole; animal cell: centrioles.
  13. Membrane: lipid bilayer (mainly phosphoglycerides); RBC membrane 52% protein, 40% lipid.
  14. Fluid mosaic model: Singer and Nicolson 1972 — lateral movement of proteins in quasi-fluid lipid.
  15. Active transport uses ATP, against gradient — Na⁺/K⁺ pump. Middle lamella = calcium pectate; plasmodesmata connect cytoplasm; secondary wall laid inside primary wall.
  16. Endomembrane system = ER, Golgi, lysosomes, vacuoles (not mitochondria, chloroplasts, peroxisomes).
  17. RER: protein synthesis/secretion; SER: lipids, steroidal hormones (animals).
  18. Golgi (Camillo Golgi 1898): cisternae 0.5–1.0 µm; cis = convex forming, trans = concave maturing; packaging; glycoproteins and glycolipids.
  19. Lysosomes: hydrolases active at acidic pH; vacuole bound by tonoplast, up to 90% of plant cell volume; contractile vacuole in Amoeba for osmoregulation and excretion.
  20. Mitochondria: 0.2–1.0 µm (avg 0.5) × 1.0–4.1 µm; cristae; circular DNA, 70S ribosomes; divide by fission.
  21. Chloroplast: 5–10 × 2–4 µm; 1 in Chlamydomonas, 20–40 in mesophyll; chlorophyll in thylakoids; amyloplast (starch, potato), elaioplast (oils), aleuroplast (proteins).
  22. Ribosomes: Palade 1953; 80S = 60S + 40S; S = Svedberg unit (sedimentation coefficient).
  23. Axoneme 9 + 2 with 9 radial spokes; centriole 9 triplets of tubulin + hub (cartwheel); centrioles form basal bodies and spindle.
  24. Perinuclear space 10–50 nm; nuclear pores pass RNA and protein both ways; RBCs of many mammals and sieve tubes lack nucleus; nucleolus makes rRNA.
  25. Human cell ~2 m DNA in 46 chromosomes; centromere with kinetochores; metacentric, sub-metacentric, acrocentric, telocentric; satellite from secondary constriction.
