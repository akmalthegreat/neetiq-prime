10.0 Biotechnology applications

  • Applications: therapeutics, diagnostics, GM crops, processed food, bioremediation, waste treatment, energy production.
  • Three critical research areas: (i) the best catalyst (improved organism, usually a microbe, or pure enzyme); (ii) optimal conditions for the catalyst; (iii) downstream processing to purify the product.

10.1 Biotechnological applications in agriculture

Options to increase food production
(i) Agro-chemical based agriculture; (ii) organic agriculture; (iii) genetically engineered crop-based agriculture
  • The Green Revolution tripled food supply, mainly through better management practices and agrochemicals (fertilisers, pesticides), which are often too expensive for farmers in the developing world.
  • Plants, bacteria, fungi and animals whose genes have been altered by manipulation = GMO (genetically modified organisms).
Benefits of GM plants (NCERT)
(i) More tolerant to abiotic stresses (cold, drought, salt, heat)
(ii) Reduced reliance on chemical pesticides (pest-resistant crops)
(iii) Reduced post-harvest losses
(iv) Increased efficiency of mineral usage (prevents early exhaustion of soil fertility)
(v) Enhanced nutritional value, e.g. golden rice (vitamin A enriched)
Also tailor-made plants supplying starches, fuels and pharmaceuticals to industries

10.1 Bt crops

  • Some strains of Bacillus thuringiensis make proteins that kill lepidopterans (tobacco budworm, armyworm), coleopterans (beetles) and dipterans (flies, mosquitoes).
  • Bt forms protein crystals containing an insecticidal toxin, present as an inactive protoxin, so it does not kill the bacterium itself.
  • In the insect gut, alkaline pH solubilises the crystals → active toxin binds midgut epithelial cells → pores → cell swelling and lysis → death.
  • Toxin genes = cry genes, incorporated into crops like cotton; choice depends on crop and pest because most Bt toxins are insect-group specific.

10.1 Pest resistant plants — RNA interference

  • Nematode Meloidegyne incognitia infects the roots of tobacco plants, greatly reducing yield.
  • RNAi occurs in all eukaryotes as cellular defence: a complementary dsRNA binds a specific mRNA and prevents its translation (silencing). Source of dsRNA: viruses with RNA genomes or transposons replicating via RNA intermediates.
NEET HighlightUsing Agrobacterium vectors, nematode-specific genes were introduced so the host produced both sense and anti-sense RNA → dsRNA → RNAi silenced the nematode’s mRNA → the parasite could not survive.

10.2 Biotechnological applications in medicine

  • rDNA enables mass production of safe, more effective drugs that do not induce unwanted immune responses. About 30 recombinant therapeutics are approved worldwide; 12 are marketed in India.

10.2.1 Genetically engineered insulin

  • Earlier insulin came from the pancreas of slaughtered cattle and pigs, causing allergy in some patients.
  • Insulin has two short polypeptide chains, A and B, linked by disulphide bridges. It is made as a pro-hormone with an extra stretch, the C peptide, which is removed during maturation.
Eli Lilly, 1983The American company prepared two DNA sequences for the A and B chains of human insulin, introduced them into plasmids of E. coli to make the chains separately, then extracted and combined them by creating disulphide bonds. The main challenge was assembling insulin into its mature form.
TrapC peptide is absent in mature insulin. The A and B chains were produced separately, not as pro-insulin.

10.2.2 Gene therapy

  • Gene therapy: methods to correct a gene defect diagnosed in a child/embryo by inserting a normal gene into cells and tissues to compensate for the non-functional gene.
  • First clinical gene therapy: 1990, a 4-year-old girl with adenosine deaminase (ADA) deficiency. ADA is crucial for the immune system; the disorder is due to deletion of the ADA gene.
Treatment of ADA deficiencyNote
Bone marrow transplantationNot completely curative
Enzyme replacement therapyFunctional ADA given by injection; not completely curative
Gene therapyPatient’s lymphocytes grown in culture; functional ADA cDNA introduced using a retroviral vector; cells returned. Cells are not immortal → periodic infusion needed.

10.2.3 Molecular diagnosis

  • Conventional methods (serum, urine analysis) cannot give early detection. Techniques: recombinant DNA technology, PCR and ELISA.
  • PCR detects very low concentrations of bacteria or virus (before symptoms) by amplifying their nucleic acid; routinely used to detect HIV in suspected AIDS patients and mutations in suspected cancer patients.
  • A single-stranded DNA/RNA tagged with a radioactive molecule (probe) hybridises to complementary DNA, detected by autoradiography; a clone with a mutated gene will not appear on the film.
  • ELISA works on antigen–antibody interaction; detects antigens (proteins, glycoproteins) or antibodies against the pathogen.
TrapIn probe-based detection the mutated clone gives no signal (no complementarity). ELISA = antigen-antibody; PCR = nucleic acid amplification.

10.3 Transgenic animals

Animals whose DNA has been manipulated to possess and express an extra (foreign) gene. Rats, rabbits, pigs, sheep, cows and fish have been made, but over 95% of transgenic animals are mice.

UseExamples (NCERT)
Normal physiology and developmentHow genes are regulated, e.g. insulin-like growth factor

Biological productsHuman protein α-1-antitrypsin to treat emphysema; attempts for PKU and cystic fibrosis. 1997: first transgenic cow Rosie gave human-protein-enriched milk (2.4 g/L) containing human alpha-lactalbumin, nutritionally more balanced for babies.

10.4 Ethical issues

  • The Indian Government set up GEAC (Genetic Engineering Appraisal Committee) to decide on the validity of GM research and the safety of introducing GM organisms for public services.
  • Patents on products and technologies using genetic materials, plants and biological resources long identified and used by farmers and indigenous people caused public anger.
Basmati caseAbout 2,00,000 rice varieties in India; 27 documented varieties of Basmati. In 1997, an American company got patent rights on Basmati through the US Patent and Trademark Office; the ‘new’ variety was derived from Indian farmers’ varieties crossed with semi-dwarf varieties. Attempts were also made to patent uses of turmeric and neem.
  • Biopiracy: use of bio-resources by multinational companies and other organisations without proper authorisation from the countries and people concerned and without compensatory payment.
  • Industrialised nations are rich financially but poor in biodiversity and traditional knowledge; developing nations are rich in both.
  • The Indian Parliament cleared the second amendment of the Indian Patents Bill, covering patent terms, emergency provisions and R&D initiatives.
