17.1 Types of movement

Cells of the human body exhibit three main types of movement:

TypeWhere / how
AmoeboidSpecialised cells like macrophages and leucocytes in blood. Effected by pseudopodia formed by streaming of protoplasm; cytoskeletal elements like microfilaments are also involved.
CiliaryInternal tubular organs lined by ciliated epithelium. Coordinated movement of cilia in the trachea removes dust particles and foreign substances; passage of ova through the female reproductive tract is also facilitated by ciliary movement.
MuscularMovement of limbs, jaws, tongue etc. Contractile property of muscles is used for locomotion and other movements.
Key ideaAll locomotions are movements, but all movements are not locomotions. Locomotion = voluntary movement resulting in a change of place/location.

17.2 Muscle

Muscle is a specialised tissue of mesodermal origin. About 40–50% of the body weight of a human adult is contributed by muscles. Properties: excitability, contractility, extensibility and elasticity.

MuscleLocationAppearanceControlRole
SkeletalAttached to skeletal componentsStriatedVoluntaryLocomotory actions, changes of body posture
VisceralInner walls of hollow visceral organs (alimentary canal, reproductive tract)Smooth (non-striated)InvoluntaryTransport of food through the gut and gametes through the genital tract
CardiacHeartStriated, branching patternInvoluntaryPumping of blood
TrapCardiac muscle is striated yet involuntary — the classic exception. Visceral = smooth = involuntary.

17.2.1 Structure of a skeletal muscle

Muscle→Fascicles (held by fascia)→Muscle fibres→Myofibrils→Sarcomeres
  • Muscle bundles or fascicles are held together by a common collagenous connective tissue layer called fascia.
  • Each muscle fibre is lined by the sarcolemma enclosing the sarcoplasm. A muscle fibre is a syncytium (many nuclei).
  • The endoplasmic reticulum, i.e., sarcoplasmic reticulum, is the store house of calcium ions.
  • A large number of parallel filaments, the myofilaments or myofibrils, fill the sarcoplasm.
Band / lineContains / meaning
I-band (isotropic, light)Actin (thin filaments)
A-band (anisotropic, dark)Myosin (thick filaments)
Z-lineElastic fibre bisecting each I-band; thin filaments are firmly attached to it
M-lineThin fibrous membrane holding thick filaments together in the middle of the A-band
H-zoneCentral part of the thick filament not overlapped by thin filaments
SarcomerePortion between two successive Z-lines; functional unit of contraction
Memory trick“I am thIn and Light, A is thick And dArk.” Z = Zone boundary of the sarcomere; M = Middle of A-band; H = Hole (no overlap).

17.2.2 Structure of contractile proteins

Actin (thin filament)

  • Two F (filamentous) actins helically wound; each F-actin is a polymer of monomeric G (globular) actins
  • Two filaments of tropomyosin run along the F-actins
  • Complex protein troponin at regular intervals on tropomyosin; at rest, a troponin subunit masks the active binding sites for myosin

Myosin (thick filament)

  • Polymer of monomeric meromyosins
  • Each meromyosin: globular head with short arm = heavy meromyosin (HMM); tail = light meromyosin (LMM)
  • HMM projects outward as the cross arm
  • Globular head is an active ATPase enzyme with binding sites for ATP and active sites for actin
TrapTroponin and tropomyosin are on the thin (actin) filament, not on myosin. HMM = head + short arm; LMM = tail.

17.2.3 Mechanism of muscle contraction

Sliding filament theory: contraction of a muscle fibre takes place by the sliding of thin filaments over thick filaments.

  1. CNS sends a signal via a motor neuron. A motor neuron + the muscle fibres connected to it = motor unit. Junction with sarcolemma = neuromuscular junction / motor-end plate.
  2. Neurotransmitter acetylcholine is released → action potential in the sarcolemma.
  3. Action potential spreads → Ca++ released into sarcoplasm.
  4. Ca++ binds a subunit of troponin → removes masking of active sites on actin.
  5. Using energy from ATP hydrolysis, myosin head binds actin → cross bridge → pulls actin towards the centre of the A-band; Z-lines are pulled inward → sarcomere shortens.
  6. Myosin releases ADP and Pi → relaxed state; a new ATP binds and the cross-bridge is broken; cycle repeats.
  7. Continues till Ca++ is pumped back to sarcoplasmic cisternae → actin masked again → Z-lines return → relaxation.
NEET HighlightDuring contraction the I-band shortens (and the H-zone narrows) but the A-band retains its length.

Red fibres (aerobic)

  • High myoglobin (red O2-storing pigment)
  • Plenty of mitochondria; use large amounts of O2 for ATP

White fibres

  • Very little myoglobin; few mitochondria
  • High amount of sarcoplasmic reticulum; depend on anaerobic process
FatigueRepeated activation leads to accumulation of lactic acid due to anaerobic breakdown of glycogen → fatigue.

17.3 Skeletal system

  • Bone has a very hard matrix due to calcium salts; cartilage has a slightly pliable matrix due to chondroitin salts.
  • Humans have 206 bones and a few cartilages: axial skeleton (80 bones) and appendicular skeleton (126 bones).

Girdles

Pectoral girdle (each half)

  • Clavicle + scapula
  • Scapula: large triangular flat bone in dorsal thorax between 2nd and 7th ribs; ridge = spine, projecting as acromion (articulates with clavicle)
  • Below acromion: glenoid cavity → articulates with head of humerus (shoulder joint)
  • Clavicle: long slender bone with two curvatures = collar bone

Pelvic girdle

  • Two coxal bones, each formed by fusion of ilium, ischium and pubis
  • At the point of fusion: acetabulum, where the thigh bone articulates
  • Two halves meet ventrally as the pubic symphysis (fibrous cartilage)
TrapCarpals = 8, tarsals = 7. Glenoid cavity = shoulder (humerus); acetabulum = hip (femur).
