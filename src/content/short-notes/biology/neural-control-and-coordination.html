18.1 Neural system

  • The neural system of all animals is composed of highly specialised cells called neurons, which can detect, receive and transmit different kinds of stimuli.
  • The neural system and the endocrine system jointly coordinate and integrate all the activities of the organs so that they function in a synchronised fashion. Neural system provides an organised network of point-to-point connections for quick coordination.

18.3 Neuron as structural and functional unit

A neuron is composed of three major parts: cell body, dendrites and axon.

PartFeatures (NCERT)
Cell bodyCytoplasm with typical cell organelles and certain granular bodies called Nissl’s granules
DendritesShort fibres which branch repeatedly; also contain Nissl’s granules; transmit impulses towards the cell body
AxonLong fibre, distal end branched; each branch ends as a bulb-like synaptic knob with synaptic vesicles containing neurotransmitters. Transmits impulses away from the cell body to a synapse or neuromuscular junction.

Types of neurons (by number of axons and dendrites)

TypeStructureFound in
MultipolarOne axon + two or more dendritesCerebral cortex
BipolarOne axon + one dendriteRetina of eye
UnipolarCell body with one axon onlyUsually found in the embryonic stage

Myelinated vs non-myelinated axons

Myelinated

  • Enveloped with Schwann cells, which form a myelin sheath around the axon
  • Gaps between adjacent myelin sheaths = nodes of Ranvier
  • Found in spinal and cranial nerves

Non-myelinated

  • Enclosed by a Schwann cell that does not form a myelin sheath
  • Commonly found in autonomous and somatic neural systems
TrapNissl’s granules are in the cell body and dendrites, not in the axon. Bipolar = retina; multipolar = cerebral cortex; unipolar = embryo.

18.3.1 Generation and conduction of nerve impulse

Neurons are excitable cells because their membranes are in a polarised state. Different types of ion channels on the neural membrane are selectively permeable to different ions.

Resting state

  • Axonal membrane is comparatively more permeable to K+ and nearly impermeable to Na+; also impermeable to the negatively charged proteins in the axoplasm.
  • Axoplasm: high K+ and negatively charged proteins, low Na+. Outside fluid: low K+, high Na+.
  • Gradients are maintained by the sodium-potassium pump, which transports 3 Na+ outwards for 2 K+ into the cell (active transport).
  • Outer surface becomes positive, inner surface negative → membrane is polarised. This potential difference is the resting potential.

Action potential and conduction

  1. Stimulus at site A → membrane becomes freely permeable to Na+ → rapid influx of Na+ → polarity reversed (outside negative, inside positive) → depolarised.
  2. Potential difference at site A = action potential, which is the nerve impulse.
  3. Site B (ahead) is still positive outside → current flows A → B on the inner surface and B → A on the outer surface → polarity reversed at B → impulse is conducted along the axon.
  4. Rise in Na+ permeability is extremely short-lived; quickly followed by rise in K+ permeability → K+ diffuses out → resting potential restored (repolarisation); fibre becomes responsive again.
Memory trick“Salt In, Potassium Out”: depolarisation = Na+ in; repolarisation = K+ out. Pump = 3 Na out, 2 K in (“3 out, 2 in”).
TrapAt rest the membrane is more permeable to K+, not Na+. The Na–K pump is active transport (uses ATP); the Na+ influx during an impulse is passive (through channels).

18.3.2 Transmission of impulses

A nerve impulse is transmitted from one neuron to another through junctions called synapses, formed by the membranes of a pre-synaptic neuron and a post-synaptic neuron, which may or may not be separated by a gap called the synaptic cleft.

Electrical synapseChemical synapse
MembranesIn very close proximitySeparated by a fluid-filled synaptic cleft
MechanismElectric current flows directly from one neuron to the other; similar to conduction along a single axonUses chemicals called neurotransmitters
SpeedAlways fasterSlower
OccurrenceRare in our systemCommon

Steps at a chemical synapse

Impulse reaches axon terminal→Synaptic vesicles fuse with membrane→Neurotransmitter released into cleft→Binds receptors on post-synaptic membrane→Ion channels open → new potential
Key ideaThe new potential developed in the post-synaptic neuron may be either excitatory or inhibitory.

18.4 Central neural system

The brain is the central information processing organ and acts as the command and control system. It controls voluntary movements, balance, functioning of vital involuntary organs (lungs, heart, kidneys etc.), thermoregulation, hunger and thirst, circadian (24-hour) rhythms, activities of several endocrine glands and human behaviour; it is also the site for processing vision, hearing, speech, memory, intelligence, emotions and thoughts.

18.4.1 Forebrain

Consists of the cerebrum, thalamus and hypothalamus.

Cerebrum

  • Forms the major part of the human brain. A deep cleft divides it longitudinally into left and right cerebral hemispheres, connected by a tract of nerve fibres called the corpus callosum.
  • The layer of cells covering the hemispheres is the cerebral cortex, thrown into prominent folds. It is called grey matter because neuron cell bodies are concentrated here.
  • Cerebral cortex contains motor areas, sensory areas and large regions that are neither clearly sensory nor motor — association areas — responsible for complex functions like intersensory associations, memory and communication.
  • Fibres of the tracts covered with myelin sheath form the inner part of the hemispheres → opaque white appearance → white matter.
StructureFunction / feature
ThalamusCerebrum wraps around it; major coordinating centre for sensory and motor signalling
HypothalamusLies at the base of the thalamus; centres controlling body temperature, urge for eating and drinking; contains neurosecretory cells that secrete hypothalamic hormones
Limbic lobe / limbic systemInner parts of hemispheres + deep structures like amygdala, hippocampus. Along with the hypothalamus, regulates sexual behaviour, expression of emotional reactions (excitement, pleasure, rage, fear) and motivation.
TrapCortex = grey matter (cell bodies, outside); inner part = white matter (myelinated tracts). Corpus callosum joins the two hemispheres.

18.4.2 Midbrain

  • Located between the thalamus/hypothalamus of the forebrain and the pons of the hindbrain.
  • A canal called the cerebral aqueduct passes through the midbrain.
  • Dorsal portion consists mainly of four round swellings (lobes) called corpora quadrigemina.

18.4.3 Hindbrain

PartFeature / function
PonsFibre tracts that interconnect different regions of the brain
CerebellumVery convoluted surface to provide additional space for many more neurons
Medulla (oblongata)Connected to the spinal cord; contains centres controlling respiration, cardiovascular reflexes and gastric secretions
NEET HighlightBrain stem = midbrain + pons + medulla oblongata; it forms the connections between the brain and the spinal cord. The cerebellum is not part of the brain stem.
TrapCorpora quadrigemina = midbrain (not hindbrain). Respiration and cardiovascular centres = medulla. Temperature, hunger, thirst = hypothalamus.

★ Quick revision

  1. Neurons detect, receive and transmit stimuli; neural + endocrine systems coordinate organs.
  2. Hydra: network of neurons; insects: brain + ganglia; vertebrates: more developed.
  3. CNS = brain + spinal cord (processing and control); PNS = all nerves associated with CNS.
  4. Afferent fibres: organs → CNS; efferent fibres: CNS → organs.
  5. Somatic: CNS → skeletal muscles; autonomic: CNS → involuntary organs and smooth muscles (sympathetic + parasympathetic); visceral NS: nerves, fibres, ganglia, plexuses between CNS and viscera.
  6. Neuron: cell body (Nissl’s granules), dendrites (towards cell body, have Nissl’s granules), axon (away; synaptic knobs with neurotransmitter vesicles).
  7. Multipolar: cerebral cortex; bipolar: retina; unipolar: embryonic stage.
  8. Myelinated (Schwann cell myelin sheath, nodes of Ranvier): spinal and cranial nerves; non-myelinated: autonomous and somatic systems.
  9. Rest: membrane more permeable to K⁺, nearly impermeable to Na⁺; inside high K⁺ and negative proteins; outside high Na⁺.
  10. Na⁺–K⁺ pump: 3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in; outside positive, inside negative = polarised; resting potential.
  11. Stimulus → Na⁺ influx → polarity reversed (depolarised) → action potential = nerve impulse.
  12. Current: inside A → B, outside B → A; impulse moves forward. K⁺ efflux restores resting potential.
  13. Synapse: pre- and post-synaptic membranes, synaptic cleft.
  14. Electrical synapse: close membranes, direct current, faster, rare. Chemical synapse: cleft, neurotransmitters, receptors, ion channels; excitatory or inhibitory potential.
  15. Brain controls voluntary movement, balance, vital organs, thermoregulation, hunger, thirst, circadian rhythms, endocrine glands, behaviour.
  16. Meninges: dura mater (outer), arachnoid (thin middle), pia mater (inner).
  17. Forebrain: cerebrum (hemispheres joined by corpus callosum; cortex = grey matter with motor, sensory, association areas; inner white matter), thalamus (sensory and motor coordination), hypothalamus (temperature, eating, drinking, hypothalamic hormones).
  18. Limbic system (amygdala, hippocampus) + hypothalamus: sexual behaviour, emotions, motivation.
  19. Midbrain: cerebral aqueduct, corpora quadrigemina.
  20. Hindbrain: pons (interconnecting tracts), cerebellum (convoluted, more neurons), medulla (respiration, cardiovascular reflexes, gastric secretion).
  21. Brain stem = midbrain + pons + medulla.
