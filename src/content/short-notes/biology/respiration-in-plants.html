12.0 Why cells respire

All living organisms need energy for absorption, transport, movement, reproduction and even breathing. This energy comes from the oxidation of food (macromolecules).

  • Only green plants and cyanobacteria can make their own food by photosynthesis (light energy → chemical energy stored in carbohydrates such as glucose, sucrose, starch).
  • Even in green plants, not every cell photosynthesises: only cells containing chloroplasts, mostly in the superficial layers, carry out photosynthesis. All non-green organs, tissues and cells still need food for oxidation, so food must be translocated to them.
  • Animals are heterotrophs: they get food from plants directly (herbivores) or indirectly (carnivores); saprophytes such as fungi depend on dead and decaying matter.
  • Ultimately, all the food respired for life processes comes from photosynthesis.
Definition: Cellular respiration The breaking of the C–C bonds of complex compounds through oxidation within the cells, leading to the release of a considerable amount of energy, is called respiration. The compounds oxidised are called respiratory substrates.

Respiratory substrates

  • Usually carbohydrates are oxidised to release energy; but proteins, fats and even organic acids can be used as respiratory substances in some plants, under certain conditions.
  • Glucose is the most common (favoured) respiratory substrate.

How the energy is handled

  • Energy within the substrate is not released all at once, in one step (as in combustion); it is released in a series of slow, step-wise reactions controlled by enzymes, and is trapped as chemical energy in the form of ATP.
  • The energy released by oxidation is not used directly; it is used to synthesise ATP, which is broken down whenever energy is needed. Hence ATP acts as the energy currency of the cell.
  • Energy trapped in ATP drives the various energy-requiring processes of the organism.
  • The carbon skeleton produced during respiration is used as precursors for biosynthesis of other molecules in the cell.
ProcessWhere in a eukaryotic cell
Photosynthesis (making food)Chloroplasts
Breakdown of food to release energyCytoplasm and mitochondria (mitochondria only in eukaryotes)
Key idea Respiration = controlled, stepwise oxidation inside cells → energy captured as ATP + carbon skeletons for biosynthesis. Combustion = uncontrolled, one-step oxidation → energy lost mostly as heat.

12.1 Do plants breathe?

Yes, plants need O2 for respiration and they give out CO2. So plants must have a system to make O2 available, but they do so without specialised respiratory organs.

NEET Highlight Unlike animals, plants have no specialised organs for gaseous exchange; they have stomata and lenticels for this purpose.

Why plants manage without lungs

  1. Each plant part takes care of its own gas-exchange needs. There is very little transport of gases from one plant part to another.
  2. Plants do not present great demands for gas exchange. Roots, stems and leaves respire at rates far lower than animals do.
  3. Only during photosynthesis are large volumes of gases exchanged, and each leaf is well adapted to take care of its own needs then. When cells photosynthesise, O2 availability is not a problem, because O2 is released within the cell.
  4. The distance gases must diffuse is not great, even in large, bulky plants. Each living cell lies quite close to the surface of the plant.

Leaves

Gas exchange through stomata; leaf cells are thin and close to air spaces.

Stems

Living cells are organised in thin layers inside and beneath the bark; they also have openings called lenticels.

Interior cells

Cells in the interior are dead and provide only mechanical support. Most living cells thus have at least part of their surface in contact with air.

Parenchyma

Loose packing of parenchyma cells in leaves, stems and roots provides an interconnected network of air spaces.

Combustion of glucose vs respiration

Complete combustion of glucose:

C6H12O6 + 6O2→6CO2 + 6H2O + Energy
  • In combustion, most of the energy is given out as heat.
  • If this energy is to be useful to the cell, it must be used to synthesise other molecules. So the plant cell catabolises glucose in such a way that not all the liberated energy goes out as heat: glucose is oxidised in several small steps, some of which are large enough to be coupled to ATP synthesis.

Anaerobic beginnings

  • The first cells on this planet lived in an atmosphere that lacked oxygen. Even today, many organisms live in anaerobic conditions; some are facultative and others obligate anaerobes.
  • All living organisms retain the enzymatic machinery to partially oxidise glucose without the help of oxygen. This breakdown of glucose to pyruvic acid is called glycolysis.
Trap "Plants breathe" does not mean plants have breathing organs. Breathing (ventilation) needs organs; plants only show diffusion-based gas exchange via stomata and lenticels.

12.2 Glycolysis

Definition Glycolysis (Greek glycos = sugar, lysis = splitting) is the process by which glucose undergoes partial oxidation to form two molecules of pyruvic acid.
  • The scheme was given by Gustav Embden, Otto Meyerhof and J. Parnas; hence it is also called the EMP pathway.
  • In anaerobic organisms, glycolysis is the only process in respiration.
  • Glycolysis occurs in the cytoplasm of the cell and is present in all living organisms.
  • It does not require O2; it is the common first stage of both aerobic and anaerobic respiration.

Where the glucose comes from (in plants)

  • In plants, glucose is derived from sucrose, the end product of photosynthesis, or from storage carbohydrates.
  • Sucrose is converted into glucose and fructose by the enzyme invertase; both these monosaccharides readily enter the glycolytic pathway.
  • Glucose and fructose are phosphorylated to give rise to glucose-6-phosphate by the enzyme hexokinase.
Key idea Glycolysis is a chain of ten reactions, each under the control of a different enzyme, converting glucose (6C) into 2 pyruvate (3C).

The sequence (know every intermediate)

Glucose→G-6-P→F-6-P→F-1,6-bisP→PGAL + DHAP→BPGA→PGA→2-PG→PEP→Pyruvic acid

Key reactions NCERT stresses

  • Glucose-6-phosphate is isomerised to fructose-6-phosphate.
  • Fructose-1,6-bisphosphate is split into dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP) and 3-phosphoglyceraldehyde (PGAL). The two trioses are interconvertible.
  • There is one step where NADH + H+ is formed from NAD+: when PGAL is oxidised (with inorganic phosphate) to 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate (BPGA). Two redox-equivalents are removed (as two hydrogen atoms) from PGAL and transferred to NAD+.
  • Conversion of BPGA → 3-phosphoglyceric acid (PGA) is an energy-yielding process; this energy is trapped by forming ATP.
  • Another ATP is synthesised during the conversion of PEP to pyruvic acid.
  • 2-phosphoglycerate → PEP involves removal of water.
NEET Highlight: ATP use in glycolysis ATP is utilised at two steps: (1) glucose → glucose-6-phosphate, and (2) fructose-6-phosphate → fructose-1,6-bisphosphate.

Trap
  • ATP in glycolysis is made directly by substrate-level phosphorylation (BPGA → PGA and PEP → pyruvate), not by the ETS.
  • Glycolysis yields 4 ATP gross but 2 ATP net. If asked "directly synthesised", read carefully: 4 are formed, 2 are consumed.
  • Invertase acts on sucrose; hexokinase phosphorylates glucose and fructose. Don't swap them.

Fate of pyruvic acid

Pyruvic acid is the key product of glycolysis. What happens next depends on the cell's need:

Lactic acid fermentation

Anaerobic; e.g. some bacteria, animal muscle in O2 shortage.

Alcoholic fermentation

Anaerobic; e.g. yeast.

Aerobic respiration

Krebs cycle + ETS; needs O2; complete oxidation to CO2 + H2O.
  • Fermentation takes place under anaerobic conditions in many prokaryotes and unicellular eukaryotes.
  • For complete oxidation of glucose to CO2 and H2O, organisms adopt the Krebs' cycle, also called aerobic respiration; this requires O2.
Memory trick "Good Girls Find Friends; Parties Bring People Together, PEP Pays": Glucose, G-6-P, F-6-P, F-1,6-bisP, PGAL, BPGA, PGA, 2-PG, PEP, Pyruvate. ATP spent at the two "F"-related hexose steps; ATP earned after the two "B/P" acid steps.

12.3 Fermentation

Definition In fermentation, e.g. by yeast, the incomplete oxidation of glucose is achieved under anaerobic conditions by sets of reactions where pyruvic acid is converted to CO2 and ethanol.

Alcoholic fermentation (yeast)

Pyruvic acid→Acetaldehyde + CO2→Ethanol
  • Enzymes: pyruvic acid decarboxylase and alcohol dehydrogenase catalyse these reactions.
  • Step 1 (decarboxylase) removes CO2; step 2 (alcohol dehydrogenase) reduces acetaldehyde using NADH + H+ → NAD+.

Lactic acid fermentation

  • Other organisms, like some bacteria, produce lactic acid from pyruvic acid.
  • In animal cells too, like muscles during exercise, when oxygen is inadequate for cellular respiration, pyruvic acid is reduced to lactic acid by lactate dehydrogenase.
  • No CO2 is released in lactic acid fermentation.
Key idea The reducing agent is NADH + H+, which is reoxidised to NAD+ in both processes. Regenerating NAD+ is what lets glycolysis keep running without O2.

Why fermentation is inefficient

  • In both lactic acid and alcohol fermentation, not much energy is released: less than seven per cent of the energy in glucose is released, and not all of it is trapped as high-energy bonds of ATP.
  • The processes are hazardous: either acid or alcohol is produced.
  • Net ATP synthesised when one glucose is fermented to alcohol or lactic acid = 2 ATP (only from glycolysis).
  • Yeasts poison themselves to death when the concentration of alcohol reaches about 13 per cent.
  • Hence the maximum alcohol concentration in naturally fermented beverages is about 13%; beverages with higher alcohol content are made by distillation.

  • No ATP is made in the pyruvate → ethanol or pyruvate → lactate steps; all 2 ATP come from glycolysis.

  • Muscle cramps/fatigue during heavy exercise are linked to lactic acid accumulation (anaerobic glycolysis in muscle).

12.4 Aerobic respiration

For aerobic respiration to take place within mitochondria, the final product of glycolysis, pyruvate, is transported from the cytoplasm into the mitochondria.

Definition Aerobic respiration is the process that leads to a complete oxidation of organic substances in the presence of oxygen, releasing CO2, water and a large amount of energy present in the substrate. It is much more common in higher organisms.

The two crucial events

1. Complete oxidation of pyruvate

Stepwise removal of all the hydrogen atoms, leaving three molecules of CO2.
Site: matrix of mitochondria.

2. Electron transfer to O2

Passing the electrons removed as part of the H atoms to molecular O2, with simultaneous synthesis of ATP.
Site: inner mitochondrial membrane.

Oxidative decarboxylation of pyruvate (link reaction)

  • Pyruvate, formed by glycolytic catabolism of carbohydrates in the cytosol, enters the mitochondrial matrix and undergoes oxidative decarboxylation by a complex set of reactions catalysed by pyruvic dehydrogenase.
  • The reactions need several coenzymes, including NAD+ and Coenzyme A.
Pyruvic acid + CoA + NAD+→Acetyl CoA + CO2 + NADH + H+

Conditions written over the arrow: Mg2+, pyruvate dehydrogenase (in mitochondrial matrix).

  • During this process, two molecules of NADH are produced from the metabolism of two molecules of pyruvic acid (i.e. per glucose).
  • Acetyl CoA then enters a cyclic pathway, the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, more commonly called Krebs' cycle after Hans Krebs, who first elucidated it.
Trap
  • The link reaction is not part of glycolysis and not technically a step of the Krebs cycle; it bridges them. It occurs in the matrix.
  • It already releases 1 CO2 per pyruvate: of the 3 CO2 per pyruvate, 1 comes here and 2 in the Krebs cycle.
  • Metal ion cofactor here: Mg2+.

12.4.1 Tricarboxylic acid (Krebs') cycle

Steps

  1. The TCA cycle starts with the condensation of the acetyl group with oxaloacetic acid (OAA) and water to yield citric acid. Catalysed by citrate synthase; a molecule of CoA is released.
  2. Citrate is isomerised to isocitrate.
  3. Two successive steps of decarboxylation follow, forming α-ketoglutaric acid and then succinyl-CoA.
  4. In the remaining steps, succinyl-CoA is oxidised to OAA, allowing the cycle to continue.
  5. During conversion of succinyl-CoA to succinic acid, a molecule of GTP is synthesised. This is a substrate-level phosphorylation. In a coupled reaction, GTP is converted to GDP with simultaneous synthesis of ATP from ADP.
  6. There are three points in the cycle where NAD+ is reduced to NADH + H+ and one point where FAD+ is reduced to FADH2.

Summary equation (per pyruvic acid)

Pyruvic acid + 4NAD+ + FAD+ + 2H2O + ADP + Pi→3CO2 + 4NADH + 4H+ + FADH2 + ATP

(Over the arrow: mitochondrial matrix.) This combines the link reaction and one turn of the cycle.

Key idea: keeping the cycle running Continued oxidation of acetyl CoA via the TCA cycle requires the continued replenishment of OAA, the first member of the cycle. It also requires regeneration of NAD+ and FAD+ from NADH and FADH2 respectively.
NEET Highlight Till the end of the TCA cycle, glucose has been broken down to release CO2, and eight molecules of NADH + H+ and two of FADH2 have been synthesised, besides just two molecules of ATP in the TCA cycle. (8 NADH = 2 from link reaction + 6 from two turns of the cycle.) The NADH and FADH2 must now be oxidised through the ETS, where the bulk of ATP is made.

12.4.2 Electron transport system (ETS) and oxidative phosphorylation

The energy stored in NADH + H+ and FADH2 is released when they are oxidised through the electron transport system, with the electrons passed on to O2, forming H2O.

Definition The metabolic pathway through which the electron passes from one carrier to another is called the electron transport system (ETS); it is present in the inner mitochondrial membrane.

Path of electrons

  1. Electrons from NADH produced in the mitochondrial matrix during the citric acid cycle are oxidised by NADH dehydrogenase (complex I), and the electrons are transferred to ubiquinone, located within the inner membrane.
  2. Ubiquinone also receives reducing equivalents via FADH2 (complex II), which is generated during oxidation of succinate in the citric acid cycle.
  3. The reduced ubiquinone (ubiquinol) is oxidised with the transfer of electrons to cytochrome c via the cytochrome bc1 complex (complex III).
  4. Cytochrome c is a small protein attached to the outer surface of the inner membrane and acts as a mobile carrier for the transfer of electrons between complex III and complex IV.
  5. Complex IV is the cytochrome c oxidase complex, containing cytochromes a and a3 and two copper centres.
  6. When electrons pass from one carrier to another via complex I to IV, they are coupled to ATP synthase (complex V) for the production of ATP from ADP and inorganic phosphate.

ATP yield and the role of oxygen

  • The number of ATP molecules synthesised depends on the nature of the electron donor: oxidation of one NADH gives 3 ATP, while one FADH2 gives 2 ATP.
  • FADH2 gives fewer ATP because its electrons enter at ubiquinone, by-passing complex I.
  • Although aerobic respiration takes place only in the presence of O2, the role of oxygen is limited to the terminal stage of the process.
  • Yet the presence of O2 is vital, since it drives the whole process by removing hydrogen from the system. Oxygen acts as the final hydrogen acceptor.
Oxidative phosphorylation Unlike photophosphorylation, where light energy is used to produce the proton gradient for phosphorylation, in respiration the energy of oxidation–reduction is used for the same purpose. Hence the process is called oxidative phosphorylation.

ATP synthase (complex V)

F1 headpiece

A peripheral membrane protein complex containing the site for synthesis of ATP from ADP and inorganic phosphate.

F0

An integral membrane protein complex that forms the channel through which protons cross the inner membrane.
  • Passage of protons through the F0 channel is coupled to the catalytic site of F1 for the production of ATP.
  • For each ATP produced, 2H+ pass through F0 from the intermembrane space to the matrix, down the electrochemical proton gradient.
Trap
  • Protons are pumped out into the intermembrane space by the ETS; they flow back into the matrix through F0. In chloroplasts the gradient is across the thylakoid membrane (lumen → stroma).
  • Cytochrome c is on the outer surface of the inner membrane, not in the outer membrane.
  • Copper centres are in complex IV; cytochromes a and a3 are also in complex IV.
  • F1 = synthesis site (peripheral); F0 = proton channel (integral). Easy swap in MCQs.
Memory trick "F-one makes one (ATP); F-zero is the door (for H+)." Complexes in order: "New Students Bring Coffee Often": NADH dehydrogenase (I), Succinate/FADH2 (II), bc1 (III), cyt c, Oxidase (IV).

12.5 The respiratory balance sheet

It is possible to calculate the net gain of ATP for every glucose molecule oxidised, but only on certain assumptions:

  1. There is a sequential, orderly pathway functioning, with one substrate forming the next, and with glycolysis, TCA cycle and ETS pathway following one after another.
  2. The NADH synthesised in glycolysis is transferred into the mitochondria and undergoes oxidative phosphorylation.
  3. None of the intermediates in the pathway are utilised to synthesise any other compound.
  4. Only glucose is being respired; no other alternative substrates enter the pathway at any intermediary stage.
Reality check These assumptions are not really valid in a living system: all pathways work simultaneously and do not take place one after another; substrates enter and are withdrawn as necessary; ATP is used as and when needed; and enzymatic rates are controlled by multiple means. Yet the exercise helps us appreciate the beauty and efficiency of the living system in extracting and storing energy.

12.6 Amphibolic pathway

Glucose is the favoured substrate for respiration. All carbohydrates are usually first converted into glucose before being used for respiration. Other substrates can also be respired, but they do not enter at the first step:

SubstrateBroken down intoEntry point in respiratory pathway
CarbohydratesSimple sugars (glucose)Glycolysis (start)
FatsGlycerol + fatty acidsFatty acids → degraded to acetyl CoA; glycerol → converted to PGAL
ProteinsAmino acids (by proteases)Each amino acid, after deamination, enters (depending on its structure) within the Krebs cycle, or as pyruvate or acetyl CoA

Why "amphibolic"?

  • Because respiration involves breakdown of substrates, it has traditionally been considered a catabolic process and the respiratory pathway a catabolic pathway.
  • But when the organism needs to synthesise fatty acids, acetyl CoA is withdrawn from the respiratory pathway. Hence the respiratory pathway is involved in both synthesis and breakdown of fatty acids.
  • Similarly, during breakdown and synthesis of proteins, respiratory intermediates form the link.
  • Breaking down processes in a living organism = catabolism; synthesis = anabolism. Because the respiratory pathway is involved in both, it is better to consider it an amphibolic pathway rather than a catabolic one.
Key idea Respiratory intermediates (pyruvate, acetyl CoA, Krebs-cycle acids) are the crossroads of metabolism: carbohydrates, fats and proteins can be broken down through them, and the same intermediates supply carbon skeletons for synthesis.
Trap
  • Fatty acids enter as acetyl CoA (not pyruvate); glycerol enters at PGAL (glycolysis).
  • Amino acids are deaminated first; entry point varies with the amino acid.
  • Amphibolic = amphi (both) catabolic + anabolic.

12.7 Respiratory quotient (RQ)

Definition The ratio of the volume of CO2 evolved to the volume of O2 consumed in respiration is called the respiratory quotient (RQ) or respiratory ratio.
RQ=Volume of CO2 evolved ÷ Volume of O2 consumed

RQ depends on the type of respiratory substrate used during respiration.

Carbohydrates: RQ = 1

When carbohydrates are used as substrate and are completely oxidised, RQ = 1, because equal amounts of CO2 and O2 are evolved and consumed.

C6H12O6 + 6O2→6CO2 + 6H2O + Energy⇒RQ = 6CO2/6O2 = 1.0

Fats: RQ < 1

When fats are used, RQ is less than 1. Calculation for the fatty acid tripalmitin:

2(C51H98O6) + 145O2→102CO2 + 98H2O + energy⇒RQ = 102/145 = 0.7

Proteins: RQ ≈ 0.9

When proteins are respiratory substrates, the ratio would be about 0.9.

SubstrateRQWhy
Carbohydrate (glucose)1.0CO2 evolved = O2 used
Protein≈ 0.9Slightly more O2 needed
Fat (tripalmitin)0.7Fats are poor in O2, so need much more O2 to oxidise
NEET Highlight In living organisms, respiratory substrates are often more than one; pure proteins or fats are never used as respiratory substrates. So measured RQ values are mixtures of these ideal values.
Trap
  • RQ = CO2/O2, not O2/CO2.
  • Order: Carbohydrate (1) > Protein (0.9) > Fat (0.7).
  • Tripalmitin numbers: 2 molecules, 145 O2, 102 CO2, 98 H2O.
Memory trick "Carbs are Complete (1), Proteins are Pretty close (0.9), Fats Fall short (0.7)."

★ Quick revision

  1. Respiration = breaking of C–C bonds of complex compounds by oxidation within cells, releasing energy.
  2. Compounds oxidised = respiratory substrates; usually carbohydrates; also proteins, fats, organic acids.
  3. ATP = energy currency; carbon skeletons from respiration act as precursors for biosynthesis.
  4. Plants have no specialised respiratory organs; gas exchange via stomata and lenticels.
  5. Plant parts respire far slower than animals; each part handles its own gas exchange; loose parenchyma gives interconnected air spaces.
  6. Glycolysis = EMP pathway (Embden, Meyerhof, Parnas); in cytoplasm; in all organisms; only respiratory process in anaerobes.
  7. Sucrose → glucose + fructose by invertase; phosphorylation by hexokinase.
  8. Glycolysis: 10 reactions; ATP used at glucose → G-6-P and F-6-P → F-1,6-bisP.
  9. Only NADH-forming step: PGAL → BPGA. ATP formed at BPGA → PGA and PEP → pyruvate.
  10. Net yield of glycolysis: 2 pyruvate, 2 ATP, 2 NADH + H+.
  11. Yeast: pyruvate → ethanol + CO2 (pyruvic acid decarboxylase, alcohol dehydrogenase).
  12. Muscle/bacteria: pyruvate → lactic acid (lactate dehydrogenase). NADH reoxidised to NAD+ in both.
  13. Fermentation releases < 7% of glucose energy; net 2 ATP; yeast dies at ~13% alcohol.
  14. Pyruvate → acetyl CoA + CO2 + NADH in matrix by pyruvic dehydrogenase (Mg2+, NAD+, CoA).
  15. Krebs (TCA) cycle by Hans Krebs: acetyl CoA + OAA + H2O → citric acid (citrate synthase); CoA released.
  16. Per turn: 3 NADH, 1 FADH2, 1 GTP → ATP (substrate-level at succinyl-CoA → succinic acid).
  17. Pyruvic acid + 4NAD+ + FAD+ + 2H2O + ADP + Pi → 3CO2 + 4NADH + 4H+ + FADH2 + ATP.
  18. ETS in inner mitochondrial membrane: I NADH dehydrogenase, II FADH2, ubiquinone, III cyt bc1, cyt c (mobile, outer surface), IV cyt c oxidase (a, a3, 2 Cu).
