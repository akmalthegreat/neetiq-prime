7.0 Health

DefinitionHealth is a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being.
  • Health is affected by genetic disorders, infections and life style (food and water, rest and exercise, habits).
  • Good health: balanced diet, personal hygiene, regular exercise (and yoga); awareness about diseases, vaccination, proper disposal of wastes, control of vectors, hygienic food and water.
  • Diseases that easily spread from an infected person to a healthy one are infectious; others (e.g. cancer) are non-infectious. Pathogens include bacteria, viruses, fungi, protozoans and helminths.

7.1 Common diseases in humans

DiseasePathogenKey facts (NCERT)
TyphoidSalmonella typhi (bacterium)Enters small intestine via contaminated food and water, spreads by blood. Sustained high fever (39–40 °C), weakness, stomach pain, constipation, headache, loss of appetite; intestinal perforation and death in severe cases. Confirmed by Widal test. Mary Mallon (‘Typhoid Mary’), a cook and carrier, spread it for years.
PneumoniaStreptococcus pneumoniae and Haemophilus influenzaeInfects the alveoli, which fill with fluid. Fever, chills, cough, headache; in severe cases lips and finger nails turn grey to bluish. Spread by droplets/aerosols and sharing glasses and utensils.
Common coldRhino virusesInfect nose and respiratory passage, not lungs. Nasal congestion, sore throat, hoarseness, cough, headache, tiredness for 3–7 days; spread by droplets or contaminated objects (pens, books, cups, doorknobs, keyboard, mouse).

Other bacterial diseases: dysentery, plague, diphtheria.

TrapWidal test = typhoid (not malaria). Common cold does not infect the lungs; pneumonia does.

7.1 Malaria

  • Caused by Plasmodium: P. vivax, P. malariae and P. falciparum. Malignant malaria by P. falciparum is the most serious and can be fatal.
  • Enters humans as sporozoites (infectious form) through the bite of an infected female Anopheles mosquito.
  • Parasites multiply first in liver cells, then attack RBCs and rupture them, releasing the toxin haemozoin → chill and high fever recurring every 3–4 days.
NEET HighlightPlasmodium needs two hosts: human and mosquito. Sexual stages (gametocytes) develop in human RBCs; fertilisation and development occur in the mosquito’s stomach; sporozoites are stored in its salivary glands.
TrapInfective stage to humans = sporozoite. Fever is due to haemozoin. Gametocytes form in humans, but fertilisation is in the mosquito.

Prevention and control

  • Personal and public hygiene: proper disposal of waste and excreta; periodic cleaning and disinfection of water reservoirs, pools, cesspools and tanks; hygiene in public catering.
  • Vector-borne diseases: avoid stagnant water, clean household coolers, use mosquito nets, introduce fish like Gambusia (feed on mosquito larvae), spray insecticides, wire mesh on doors and windows. Dengue and chikungunya are spread by Aedes.
  • Vaccines and immunisation have eradicated smallpox and controlled polio, diphtheria, pneumonia and tetanus; antibiotics and drugs help treat diseases.

7.2 Immunity

7.2.1 Innate immunity (non-specific, present at birth)

BarrierExamples (NCERT)
PhysicalSkin (main barrier); mucus coating of respiratory, gastrointestinal and urogenital tracts
PhysiologicalAcid in stomach, saliva in mouth, tears from eyes
CellularPMNL (neutrophils), monocytes, natural killer cells (lymphocytes) in blood and macrophages in tissues phagocytose microbes
CytokineVirus-infected cells secrete interferons, which protect non-infected cells

7.2.2 Acquired immunity (pathogen specific, has memory)

  • First encounter → primary response (low intensity); later encounter → highly intensified secondary or anamnestic response due to memory.
  • Carried out by B-lymphocytes (produce antibodies) and T-lymphocytes (do not secrete antibodies but help B cells).
  • Each antibody has four peptide chains: two small light chains and two longer heavy chains → H2L2. Types: IgA, IgM, IgE, IgG.

Humoral immune response

  • By antibodies in blood (B cells)

Cell-mediated immunity (CMI)

  • Mediated by T-lymphocytes
  • Responsible for graft rejection — body differentiates ‘self’ and ‘non-self’
  • Tissue and blood group matching needed; patient takes immuno-suppressants lifelong

7.2.3–7.2.4 Active and passive immunity; vaccination

Active immunityPassive immunity
HowHost exposed to antigens (living/dead microbes, proteins) and makes its own antibodiesReady-made antibodies given directly
SpeedSlow, takes time for full responseImmediate
ExamplesNatural infection; deliberate immunisation with microbesColostrum (rich in IgA); antibodies from mother through placenta; antitoxin for tetanus; snake antivenom
  • Vaccination is based on the memory of the immune system: antigenic proteins or inactivated/weakened pathogen (vaccine) generate antibodies and memory B and T cells.
  • When a quick response is needed (e.g. tetanus, snakebite), preformed antibodies or antitoxin are injected = passive immunisation.
  • Recombinant DNA technology produces antigenic polypeptides in bacteria or yeast, e.g. hepatitis B vaccine from yeast.
TrapColostrum antibody = IgA; allergy antibody = IgE. Antitoxin/antivenom = passive; vaccine = active.

7.2.5–7.2.7 Allergies, auto immunity and the immune system

TopicKey facts (NCERT)
AllergyExaggerated response to environmental antigens = allergens (dust mites, pollens, animal dander). Antibodies are IgE. Histamine and serotonin released from mast cells. Symptoms: sneezing, watery eyes, running nose, difficulty in breathing. Treated with anti-histamine, adrenalin and steroids. More children in metro cities suffer allergies and asthma, possibly due to a protected environment early in life.
Auto immunityBody attacks self-cells due to genetic and unknown reasons, e.g. rheumatoid arthritis

Lymphoid organs

Primary lymphoid organs

  • Bone marrow and thymus: immature lymphocytes become antigen-sensitive
  • Bone marrow: main lymphoid organ; all blood cells produced
  • Thymus: lobed, near the heart, beneath the breastbone; large at birth, reduces to very small by puberty
  • Both provide micro-environments for development of T-lymphocytes

Secondary lymphoid organs

  • Spleen, lymph nodes, tonsils, Peyer’s patches of small intestine and appendix
  • Sites where lymphocytes meet antigens and proliferate into effector cells
  • Spleen: bean shaped; filters blood; reservoir of erythrocytes
  • Lymph nodes: trap antigens in lymph and tissue fluid
NEET HighlightMALT (mucosa associated lymphoid tissue) in the lining of respiratory, digestive and urogenital tracts makes up about 50% of lymphoid tissue in the human body.

7.3 AIDS

  • Acquired Immuno Deficiency Syndrome: acquired (not congenital); syndrome = group of symptoms. First reported in 1981; killed more than 25 million people.
  • Caused by HIV, a retrovirus with an envelope enclosing an RNA genome.
  • Transmission: (a) sexual contact, (b) contaminated blood, (c) sharing infected needles, (d) mother to child through placenta. Not spread by mere touch; only through body fluids. Time-lag usually 5–10 years.
  • Virus enters macrophages; RNA genome → viral DNA by reverse transcriptase; viral DNA integrates into host DNA; macrophages act as an HIV factory.
  • HIV enters helper T-lymphocytes (TH); their number falls progressively → fever, diarrhoea, weight loss → opportunistic infections by Mycobacterium, viruses, fungi and Toxoplasma.
  • Diagnosis: ELISA (enzyme linked immuno-sorbent assay). Anti-retroviral drugs only partially effective — prolong life but cannot prevent death.
PreventionNo cure → prevention is best. NACO (National AIDS Control Organisation), NGOs and WHO work on: safe blood from blood banks, disposable needles and syringes, free condoms, controlling drug abuse, safe sex, regular check-ups in susceptible populations.

7.4 Cancer

  • Normal cells show contact inhibition (contact with other cells inhibits their growth); cancer cells have lost it → divide continuously → tumours.

Benign tumour

  • Remains confined to original location; little damage

Malignant tumour

  • Mass of neoplastic / tumour cells; grow rapidly, invade and damage tissues, starve normal cells
  • Cells spread through blood and start new tumours = metastasis (most feared property)
CarcinogensExamples
PhysicalIonising (X-rays, gamma rays) and non-ionising (UV) radiations cause DNA damage
ChemicalChemicals in tobacco smoke — major cause of lung cancer
BiologicalOncogenic viruses with viral oncogenes; cellular oncogenes (c-onc) / proto-oncogenes in normal cells, when activated, cause oncogenic transformation

Detection and treatment

  • Biopsy and histopathological studies; blood and bone marrow tests for leukaemias.
  • Radiography (X-rays); CT uses X-rays for a 3-D image; MRI uses strong magnetic fields and non-ionising radiations. Antibodies against cancer-specific antigens; molecular biology to detect inherited susceptibility genes.
  • Treatment: surgery, radiation therapy, chemotherapy (side effects: hair loss, anaemia) and immunotherapy with biological response modifiers such as α-interferon, which activates the immune system.
TrapMRI uses no ionising radiation; CT uses X-rays. α-interferon is an immunotherapy, not chemotherapy.

7.5 Drugs and alcohol abuse

Drug classSourceKey facts (NCERT)
Opioids — heroin (smack)Latex of poppy, Papaver somniferumBind opioid receptors in CNS and GI tract. Heroin = diacetylmorphine, white, odourless, bitter crystals by acetylation of morphine; snorted or injected; a depressant. Morphine is an effective sedative and painkiller after surgery.
CannabinoidsInflorescences of Cannabis sativaInteract with cannabinoid receptors in the brain. Marijuana, hashish, charas, ganja; inhaled or eaten; affect the cardiovascular system; abused by some sportspersons.
Coca alkaloid — cocaine (coke, crack)Erythroxylum coca, native to South AmericaInterferes with transport of dopamine; usually snorted; potent CNS stimulant (euphoria, energy); excess → hallucinations.
  • Other hallucinogenic plants: Atropa belladona and Datura. Barbiturates, amphetamines, benzodiazepines (used for depression and insomnia) are often abused.
  • Tobacco (used > 400 years) contains nicotine, an alkaloid that stimulates the adrenal gland to release adrenaline and noradrenaline → ↑ BP and heart rate. Smoking → cancers of lung, urinary bladder, throat, bronchitis, emphysema, coronary heart disease, gastric ulcer; ↑ CO in blood, ↓ haem-bound O2. Tobacco chewing → oral cancer.

Adolescence, addiction and effects

  • Adolescence: 12–18 years. Causes: curiosity, adventure, experimentation, stress, pressure to excel, media glamour, unsupportive family, peer pressure.
  • Addiction: psychological attachment to effects such as euphoria; tolerance rises so higher doses are needed. Dependence: unpleasant withdrawal syndrome (anxiety, shakiness, nausea, sweating) if the dose is stopped abruptly.
  • Effects: reckless behaviour, vandalism, violence; overdose → coma and death (respiratory/heart failure, cerebral haemorrhage); IV users risk AIDS and hepatitis B; chronic use damages nervous system and liver (cirrhosis); harms the foetus.
Anabolic steroid side effects — femalesMales
Masculinisation, aggressiveness, mood swings, depression, abnormal menstrual cycles, excess facial/body hair, enlarged clitoris, deepening of voiceAcne, aggressiveness, mood swings, depression, reduced testicle size, decreased sperm production, kidney and liver dysfunction, breast enlargement, premature baldness, enlarged prostate

In adolescents: severe acne and premature closure of growth centres of long bones → stunted growth. Prevention: avoid undue peer pressure, education and counselling, help from parents and peers, look for danger signs, seek professional help.

TrapHeroin = depressant; cocaine = stimulant (dopamine). Cannabinoids act on the cardiovascular system. Nicotine acts via the adrenal gland.
