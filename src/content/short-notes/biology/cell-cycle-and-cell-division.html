10.1 Cell cycle

All organisms, even the largest, start life from a single cell. Growth and reproduction both depend on cells dividing; every cell division produces two daughter cells, which can themselves grow and divide again. This repeating growth-plus-division is the cell cycle.

DefinitionThe sequence of events by which a cell duplicates its genome, synthesises the other constituents of the cell and eventually divides into two daughter cells is termed the cell cycle.
  • Three things happen in a coordinated way: DNA synthesis, synthesis of other cell constituents and division. The events are under genetic control.
  • Cell growth (in terms of cytoplasmic increase) is a continuous process, but DNA synthesis occurs only during one specific stage of the cycle.
  • The replicated chromosomes (DNA) are then distributed to the daughter nuclei by a complex series of events during cell division.
Key ideaGrowth (cytoplasm) = continuous; DNA replication = only in S phase. NEET pairs these two statements and asks which is true.

10.1.1 Phases of cell cycle

Durations to remember

CellDuration of one cell cycle
Typical human cell (in culture)Divides once in approximately 24 hours
YeastAbout 90 minutes

The duration of the cycle varies from organism to organism and from cell type to cell type.

Two basic phases

Interphase

  • Phase between two successive M phases
  • Lasts more than 95% of the duration of the cell cycle
  • Called the resting phase, but the cell is busy preparing for division: cell growth + DNA replication in an orderly manner
  • Divided into G1, S and G2

M phase (Mitosis phase)

  • The phase when actual cell division (mitosis) occurs
  • In the 24-hour human cell cycle, cell division proper lasts for only about an hour
  • Starts with karyokinesis (nuclear division, separation of daughter chromosomes)
  • Usually ends with cytokinesis (division of cytoplasm)
Trap“Resting phase” is a misnomer. Interphase is metabolically the busiest phase: the cell grows, replicates DNA and makes proteins. Only G0 cells are truly non-proliferating — and even they are metabolically active.

The three sub-phases of interphase

NEET HighlightIf the initial DNA amount is 2C, after S phase it is 4C; chromosome number stays 2n. Each chromosome now has two sister chromatids, so chromatid number doubles but chromosome number (counted by centromeres) does not.

Quiescent stage (G0)

  • Some cells in the adult animals do not appear to exhibit division (e.g., heart cells); many others divide only occasionally, as needed to replace cells lost because of injury or cell death.
  • These cells, which do not divide further, exit G1 to enter an inactive stage called the quiescent stage (G0).
  • Cells in G0 remain metabolically active but no longer proliferate unless called on to do so depending on the requirement of the organism.
TrapG0 branches off from G1, not from G2 or M. G0 cells are inactive in division, not metabolically dead.

Where mitosis occurs

Animals

  • Mitotic division is seen only in the diploid somatic cells

Plants

  • Mitotic divisions occur in both haploid and diploid cells
  • e.g. the haploid gametophyte of plants with alternation of generations grows by mitosis
ExceptionMale honey bees (drones) are haploid and produce gametes by mitosis (covered in the reproduction chapters). For this chapter, stick to the NCERT rule: animals — diploid somatic cells only.

10.2 M phase (mitosis)

  • The M phase is the most dramatic period of the cell cycle, involving a major reorganisation of virtually all components of the cell.
  • Since the number of chromosomes in the parent and progeny cells is the same, mitosis is also called equational division.
  • Nuclear division (karyokinesis) is divided into four stages for convenience: Prophase → Metaphase → Anaphase → Telophase.
Key ideaCell division is a progressive process and very clear-cut lines cannot be drawn between the various stages. The four stages are a convenience for study.
Prophase→Metaphase→Anaphase→Telophase→Cytokinesis

10.2.1 Prophase

  • First stage of karyokinesis; follows the S and G2 phases of interphase.
  • In S and G2, the new DNA molecules formed are not distinct but intertwined.
  • Prophase is marked by the initiation of condensation of chromosomal material. The chromosomal material untangles during the process of chromatin condensation.
  • The centriole, which had undergone duplication during S phase of interphase, now begins to move towards opposite poles of the cell.

Completion of prophase is marked by

  1. Chromosomal material condenses to form compact mitotic chromosomes. Chromosomes are seen to be composed of two chromatids attached together at the centromere.
  2. Initiation of the assembly of mitotic spindle — the microtubules, the proteinaceous components of the cell cytoplasm, help in the process.
  • Each centrosome radiates out microtubules called asters. The two asters together with spindle fibres form the mitotic apparatus.
  • Cells at the end of prophase, when viewed under the microscope, do not show Golgi complexes, endoplasmic reticulum, nucleolus and the nuclear envelope.
TrapCentrioles duplicate in S phase (interphase) but move to the poles in prophase. Asking “centriole duplication occurs in…” → S phase, not prophase.

10.2.2 Metaphase

  • The complete disintegration of the nuclear envelope marks the start of the second phase of mitosis; chromosomes spread through the cytoplasm.
  • By now condensation of chromosomes is complete. This is the stage at which the morphology of chromosomes is most easily studied.
  • A metaphase chromosome = two sister chromatids held together by the centromere.
  • Small disc-shaped structures at the surface of the centromeres are called kinetochores. These serve as the sites of attachment of spindle fibres (formed by the spindle fibres) to the chromosomes.
  • Chromosomes are moved to the spindle equator. One chromatid of each chromosome is connected by its kinetochore to spindle fibres from one pole, and its sister chromatid to spindle fibres from the opposite pole.
  • The plane of alignment of the chromosomes at metaphase is referred to as the metaphase plate.
Key features of metaphase(i) Spindle fibres attach to kinetochores of chromosomes. (ii) Chromosomes are moved to the spindle equator and get aligned along the metaphase plate through spindle fibres to both poles.
NEET HighlightKaryotyping and counting chromosomes use metaphase cells — chromosomes are maximally condensed and best seen. “Best stage to study chromosome morphology” = metaphase.

10.2.3 Anaphase

  • At the onset, each chromosome arranged at the metaphase plate is split simultaneously and the two daughter chromatids, now referred to as chromosomes of the future daughter nuclei, begin their migration towards the two opposite poles.
  • As each chromosome moves away from the equatorial plate, the centromere of each chromosome remains directed towards the pole and hence at the leading edge, with the arms of the chromosome trailing behind.
Key events of anaphase(i) Centromeres split and chromatids separate. (ii) Chromatids move to opposite poles.
TrapThe moment sister chromatids separate, each is called a chromosome. So in mitotic anaphase the cell temporarily holds double the chromosome number (4n), though DNA is still 4C.

10.2.4 Telophase

  • Final stage of karyokinesis. The chromosomes that have reached their respective poles decondense and lose their individuality.
  • Individual chromosomes can no longer be seen; chromatin material collects in a mass at each of the two poles.
Key events of telophase(i) Chromosomes cluster at opposite spindle poles and their identity is lost as discrete elements. (ii) Nuclear envelope develops around the chromosome clusters at each pole, forming two daughter nuclei. (iii) Nucleolus, Golgi complex and ER reform.
Memory trickTelophase = reverse prophase. Prophase: chromosomes condense; nucleolus, nuclear envelope, Golgi, ER disappear. Telophase: chromosomes decondense; all four reappear.

10.2.5 Cytokinesis

Mitosis divides the duplicated chromosomes into daughter nuclei (karyokinesis); the cell itself is divided into two daughter cells by a separate process — cytokinesis — at the end of which cell division is complete.

Animal cell

  • Achieved by the appearance of a furrow in the plasma membrane
  • The furrow gradually deepens and ultimately joins in the centre, dividing the cytoplasm into two
  • Direction: periphery → centre (centripetal)

Plant cell

  • Enclosed by a relatively inextensible cell wall, so the mechanism differs
  • Wall formation starts in the centre of the cell and grows outward to meet the existing lateral walls
  • Begins with a simple precursor, the cell-plate, which represents the middle lamella between the walls of two adjacent cells
  • Direction: centre → periphery (centrifugal)
  • At the time of cytoplasmic division, organelles like mitochondria and plastids get distributed between the two daughter cells.
  • In some organisms karyokinesis is not followed by cytokinesis, giving a multinucleate condition — a syncytium (e.g., liquid endosperm in coconut).
TrapThe cell plate becomes the middle lamella, not the primary wall. And the coconut example is the liquid endosperm (free-nuclear), not the white kernel.

10.3 Significance of mitosis

Mitosis or the equational division is usually restricted to the diploid cells only. However, in some lower plants and in some social insects, haploid cells also divide by mitosis. It results in the production of diploid daughter cells with identical genetic complement, usually.

RoleDetail (NCERT example)
GrowthThe growth of multicellular organisms is due to mitosis.
Nucleo-cytoplasmic ratioCell growth results in disturbing the ratio between the nucleus and the cytoplasm; the cell must divide to restore the nucleo-cytoplasmic ratio.

10.4 Meiosis

Sexual reproduction involves fusion of two gametes, each with a complete haploid set of chromosomes. Gametes are formed from specialised diploid cells. This specialised kind of cell division that reduces the chromosome number by half, resulting in the production of haploid daughter cells, is called meiosis.

  • Meiosis ensures the production of the haploid phase in the life cycle of sexually reproducing organisms, whereas fertilisation restores the diploid phase.
  • Meiosis occurs during gametogenesis in plants and animals. This leads to the formation of haploid gametes.

Key features of meiosis

  • Meiosis involves two sequential cycles of nuclear and cell division called meiosis I and meiosis II but only a single cycle of DNA replication.
  • Meiosis I is initiated after the parental chromosomes have replicated to produce identical sister chromatids at the S phase.
  • Meiosis involves pairing of homologous chromosomes and recombination between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes.
  • Four haploid cells are formed at the end of meiosis II.

Each meiotic division has four phases: prophase, metaphase, anaphase, telophase (I and II).

10.4.1 Meiosis I — Prophase I

Prophase of the first meiotic division is typically longer and more complex when compared to prophase of mitosis. Based on chromosomal behaviour it is subdivided into five phases:

Leptotene→Zygotene→Pachytene→Diplotene→Diakinesis
Memory trick“Lazy Zebras Prefer Dancing Daily” — Leptotene, Zygotene, Pachytene, Diplotene, Diakinesis.

Leptotene

  • Chromosomes become gradually visible under the light microscope.
  • The compaction of chromosomes continues throughout leptotene.

Zygotene

  • Chromosomes start pairing together; this process of association is called synapsis. Such paired chromosomes are called homologous chromosomes.
  • Electron micrographs show that chromosome synapsis is accompanied by the formation of a complex structure called the synaptonemal complex.
  • The complex formed by a pair of synapsed homologous chromosomes is called a bivalent or a tetrad.

Pachytene

  • A longer stage than leptotene and zygotene. The four chromatids of each bivalent become distinct and clearly appear as tetrads.
  • Characterised by the appearance of recombination nodules, the sites at which crossing over occurs between non-sister chromatids of the homologous chromosomes.
  • Crossing over = exchange of genetic material between two homologous chromosomes. Crossing over is also an enzyme-mediated process and the enzyme involved is called recombinase.
  • Crossing over leads to recombination of genetic material on the two chromosomes.
  • Recombination between homologous chromosomes is completed by the end of pachytene, leaving the chromosomes linked at the sites of crossing over.

Diplotene

  • Marked by the dissolution of the synaptonemal complex and the tendency of the recombined homologous chromosomes of the bivalents to separate from each other except at the sites of crossovers.
  • These X-shaped structures are called chiasmata.
  • In oocytes of some vertebrates, diplotene can last for months or years.

Diakinesis

  • Marked by terminalisation of chiasmata.
  • Chromosomes are fully condensed and the meiotic spindle is assembled to prepare the homologous chromosomes for separation.
  • By the end of diakinesis, the nucleolus disappears and the nuclear envelope breaks down.
  • Diakinesis represents the transition to metaphase.

TrapCrossing over occurs in pachytene, but chiasmata become visible in diplotene. Synaptonemal complex: forms in zygotene, dissolves in diplotene. Crossing over is between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes — never between sister chromatids.
NEET HighlightA bivalent = 1 pair of homologues = 2 chromosomes = 4 chromatids = 2 centromeres. In a human meiocyte (2n = 46): 23 bivalents, 92 chromatids at pachytene.

10.4.1 Meiosis I — Metaphase I to Telophase I

Metaphase I

  • The bivalent chromosomes align on the equatorial plate.
  • Microtubules from the opposite poles of the spindle attach to the pair of homologous chromosomes.

Anaphase I

  • The homologous chromosomes separate, while sister chromatids remain associated at their centromeres.

Telophase I

  • Nuclear membrane and nucleolus reappear; cytokinesis follows — this is called the dyad of cells.
  • In many cases the chromosomes do undergo some dispersion, but they do not reach the extremely extended state of the interphase nucleus.

Interkinesis

  • The stage between the two meiotic divisions is called interkinesis and is generally short lived.
  • There is no replication of DNA during interkinesis.
  • Interkinesis is followed by prophase II, a much simpler prophase than prophase I.
Trap — anaphase comparisonMitotic anaphase: centromeres split, sister chromatids separate. Anaphase I: homologous chromosomes separate; centromeres do not split. Anaphase II: centromeres split, sister chromatids separate (like mitosis).

10.4.2 Meiosis II

Meiosis II is initiated immediately after cytokinesis, usually before the chromosomes have fully elongated. In contrast to meiosis I, meiosis II resembles a normal mitosis.

StageEvents
Prophase IINuclear membrane disappears by the end of prophase II; chromosomes again become compact.

Anaphase IIBegins with the simultaneous splitting of the centromere of each chromosome (which was holding the sister chromatids together), allowing them to move towards opposite poles by shortening of microtubules attached to kinetochores.

TrapNCERT uses “tetrad” twice: a tetrad of chromatids (= bivalent, prophase I) and a tetrad of cells (end of telophase II). Similarly, dyad of cells = end of telophase I. Read the question carefully.

10.5 Significance of meiosis

  • Meiosis is the mechanism by which conservation of specific chromosome number of each species is achieved across generations in sexually reproducing organisms, even though the process, paradoxically, results in reduction of chromosome number by half.
  • It also increases the genetic variability in the population of organisms from one generation to the next.
  • Variations are very important for the process of evolution.
Key ideaMeiosis halves (2n → n), fertilisation restores (n + n → 2n): the species number stays constant. Variation comes from crossing over (pachytene) and independent separation of homologues (anaphase I).

★ Quick revision

  1. Cell cycle: genome duplication + synthesis of constituents + division; under genetic control.
  2. Cytoplasmic growth is continuous; DNA synthesis occurs only in S phase.
  3. Human cell in culture: ~24 h cycle, M phase ~1 h; yeast: ~90 min.
  4. Interphase (G1, S, G2) lasts more than 95% of the cycle; it is the “resting” but busy phase.
  5. G1: growth, no DNA replication. S: 2C → 4C, chromosome number unchanged (2n). G2: proteins for mitosis.
  6. Animal cells: DNA replicates in nucleus, centriole duplicates in cytoplasm during S phase.
  7. G0 (quiescent): exits from G1; metabolically active but not proliferating; e.g. heart cells.
  8. Animals: mitosis only in diploid somatic cells; plants: in both haploid and diploid cells.
  9. Mitosis = equational division; stages are continuous, not clear-cut.
  10. Prophase: condensation begins; centrioles move to poles; asters + spindle = mitotic apparatus; Golgi, ER, nucleolus, nuclear envelope gone by its end.
  11. Metaphase begins with complete disintegration of nuclear envelope; best stage to study chromosome morphology.
  12. Kinetochores: disc-shaped structures on centromeres; attachment sites of spindle fibres.
  13. Anaphase: centromeres split; chromatids move to poles, centromere leading, arms trailing.
  14. Telophase: chromosomes decondense; nuclear envelope, nucleolus, Golgi, ER reform.
  15. Cytokinesis: furrow in animals (outside in); cell plate in plants (centre outwards) = middle lamella.
  16. Syncytium: karyokinesis without cytokinesis, e.g. liquid endosperm of coconut.
  17. Mitosis restores the nucleo-cytoplasmic ratio; replaces epidermis, gut lining, blood cells; meristems (apical, lateral cambium) give lifelong plant growth.
  18. Meiosis: two divisions, one DNA replication, four haploid cells; occurs during gametogenesis.
  19. Prophase I order: leptotene, zygotene, pachytene, diplotene, diakinesis.
  20. Zygotene: synapsis, synaptonemal complex, bivalent/tetrad.
  21. Pachytene: recombination nodules, crossing over between non-sister chromatids; enzyme recombinase.
  22. Diplotene: synaptonemal complex dissolves; chiasmata; can last months/years in vertebrate oocytes.
  23. Diakinesis: terminalisation of chiasmata; transition to metaphase.
  24. Anaphase I: homologues separate, sister chromatids stay joined; telophase I gives a dyad; no DNA replication in interkinesis.
  25. Anaphase II: centromeres split; telophase II gives a tetrad of four haploid cells; meiosis conserves chromosome number and creates variation.
