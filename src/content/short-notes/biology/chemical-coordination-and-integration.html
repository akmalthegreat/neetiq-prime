19.1 Endocrine glands and hormones

  • Endocrine glands lack ducts and are hence called ductless glands. Their secretions are called hormones.
  • Classical definition: a chemical produced by endocrine glands and released into the blood and transported to a distantly located target organ.
  • Current scientific definition: hormones are non-nutrient chemicals which act as intercellular messengers and are produced in trace amounts.
  • Invertebrates possess very simple endocrine systems with few hormones; a large number of chemicals act as hormones in vertebrates.
Key ideaThe neural system gives point-to-point, quick coordination but its effects are short-lived; nerve fibres do not innervate all cells. The endocrine system provides continuous, chemical coordination through hormones.

19.2 Human endocrine system

Organised endocrine bodies: pituitary, pineal, thyroid, adrenal, pancreas, parathyroid, thymus and gonads (testis in males, ovary in females). Some other organs, e.g. gastrointestinal tract, liver, kidney, heart, also produce hormones.

19.2.1 Hypothalamus

  • Basal part of the diencephalon (forebrain); regulates a wide spectrum of body functions.
  • Contains groups of neurosecretory cells called nuclei which produce hormones that regulate the synthesis and secretion of pituitary hormones.

Releasing hormones

  • Stimulate secretion of pituitary hormones
  • e.g. GnRH (gonadotrophin releasing hormone) → pituitary releases gonadotrophins

Inhibiting hormones

  • Inhibit secretion of pituitary hormones
  • e.g. Somatostatin → inhibits release of growth hormone
  • These hormones pass through axons, are released from nerve endings and reach the pituitary through a portal circulatory system; they regulate the anterior pituitary.
  • The posterior pituitary is under the direct neural regulation of the hypothalamus.

19.2.2 Pituitary gland

Located in a bony cavity called the sella tursica; attached to the hypothalamus by a stalk. Divided anatomically into adenohypophysis and neurohypophysis.

19.2.3 Pineal gland

  • Located on the dorsal side of the forebrain; secretes melatonin.
  • Regulates the 24-hour (diurnal) rhythm: maintains normal rhythms of sleep-wake cycle and body temperature; also influences metabolism, pigmentation, menstrual cycle and defence capability.

19.2.4 Thyroid gland

  • Two lobes on either side of the trachea, interconnected by a thin flap of connective tissue called the isthmus.
  • Made of follicles and stromal tissues. Follicular cells synthesise tetraiodothyronine / thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3). Iodine is essential for normal synthesis.
  • Thyroid hormones regulate basal metabolic rate, support RBC formation, control metabolism of carbohydrates, proteins and fats, and influence water and electrolyte balance.
  • Thyroid also secretes a protein hormone, thyrocalcitonin (TCT), which regulates blood calcium levels.

19.2.5 Parathyroid gland

  • Four parathyroid glands on the back side of the thyroid, one pair in each lobe; secrete the peptide parathyroid hormone (PTH), regulated by circulating Ca2+.
  • PTH increases blood Ca2+: stimulates bone resorption (demineralisation), reabsorption of Ca2+ by renal tubules and Ca2+ absorption from digested food → hypercalcemic hormone.
  • PTH + TCT together maintain calcium balance.
NEET HighlightPTH raises blood calcium (hypercalcemic); TCT (thyrocalcitonin) lowers it. They act antagonistically.

19.2.6 Thymus

  • Lobular structure between the lungs, behind the sternum, on the ventral side of the aorta; major role in development of the immune system.
  • Secretes peptide hormones called thymosins: differentiate T-lymphocytes (cell-mediated immunity) and promote antibody production (humoral immunity).
  • Thymus degenerates in old individuals → less thymosin → weak immune responses in old persons.

19.2.7 Adrenal gland

One pair, at the anterior part of each kidney. Central adrenal medulla and outer adrenal cortex.

Adrenal medulla

  • Secretes adrenaline (epinephrine) and noradrenaline (norepinephrine) = catecholamines; rapidly secreted in stress/emergency → emergency hormones or hormones of Fight or Flight.
  • Increase alertness, pupillary dilation, piloerection (raising of hairs), sweating; increase heart beat, strength of heart contraction and rate of respiration.
  • Stimulate breakdown of glycogen (↑ blood glucose), and of lipids and proteins.

Adrenal cortex

Three layers (inner → outer): zona reticularis, zona fasciculata, zona glomerulosa. Secretes hormones called corticoids.

CorticoidMain hormoneActions
Glucocorticoids (carbohydrate metabolism)CortisolStimulate gluconeogenesis, lipolysis, proteolysis; inhibit cellular uptake of amino acids; maintain cardiovascular and kidney functions; anti-inflammatory, suppresses immune response; stimulates RBC production

Memory trickCortex layers outer → inner: “GFR” — Glomerulosa, Fasciculata, Reticularis (“salt, sugar, sex” in that order).

19.2.9 Testis

  • A pair in the scrotal sac; primary sex organ and endocrine gland; made of seminiferous tubules and stromal/interstitial tissue.
  • Leydig (interstitial) cells in intertubular spaces produce androgens, mainly testosterone.
Actions of androgens
Development, maturation and function of male accessory sex organs: epididymis, vas deferens, seminal vesicles, prostate, urethra
Muscular growth, facial and axillary hair, aggressiveness, low pitch of voice
Major stimulatory role in spermatogenesis; act on CNS → male sexual behaviour (libido)
Anabolic effects on protein and carbohydrate metabolism

19.2.10 Ovary

  • A pair in the abdomen; primary female sex organ, produces one ovum each menstrual cycle; secretes steroid hormones estrogen and progesterone.
  • Estrogen: mainly from growing ovarian follicles. After ovulation the ruptured follicle becomes the corpus luteum, which secretes mainly progesterone.

Estrogen

  • Growth and activities of female secondary sex organs
  • Development of growing ovarian follicles
  • Female secondary sex characters (e.g. high pitch of voice), mammary gland development
  • Regulates female sexual behaviour

Progesterone

  • Supports pregnancy
  • Acts on mammary glands: formation of alveoli (sacs that store milk) and milk secretion

19.4 Mechanism of hormone action

  • Hormones act by binding to specific proteins called hormone receptors, located in target tissues only. Each receptor is specific to one hormone.
  • Membrane-bound receptors are on the cell membrane; intracellular receptors are inside the cell, mostly nuclear receptors.
  • Binding forms a hormone-receptor complex → biochemical changes in the target tissue.
Chemical natureExamples (NCERT)
Peptide, polypeptide, proteinInsulin, glucagon, pituitary hormones, hypothalamic hormones
SteroidsCortisol, testosterone, estradiol, progesterone
IodothyroninesThyroid hormones
Amino-acid derivativesEpinephrine

Membrane-bound receptors

  • Hormone normally does not enter the target cell
  • Generates second messengers (e.g. cyclic AMP, IP3, Ca++) that regulate cellular metabolism
  • e.g. protein/peptide hormones

Intracellular receptors

  • e.g. steroid hormones, iodothyronines
  • Hormone-receptor complex interacts with the genome → regulates gene expression or chromosome function
NEET HighlightLipid-soluble hormones (steroids, thyroid hormones) cross the membrane and act on genes; water-soluble hormones (proteins, peptides, epinephrine) act at the membrane through second messengers.

★ Quick revision

  1. Endocrine glands are ductless; hormones = non-nutrient chemicals, intercellular messengers, produced in trace amounts.
  2. Glands: pituitary, pineal, thyroid, adrenal, pancreas, parathyroid, thymus, gonads; GIT, liver, kidney, heart also make hormones.
  3. Hypothalamus (diencephalon): releasing (GnRH) and inhibiting (somatostatin → inhibits GH) hormones; portal circulation to anterior pituitary; direct neural control of posterior pituitary.
  4. Pituitary in sella tursica. Pars distalis: GH, PRL, TSH, ACTH, LH, FSH; pars intermedia: MSH; pars nervosa stores/releases oxytocin and vasopressin (made in hypothalamus).
  5. GH: gigantism (excess), pituitary dwarfism (deficit), acromegaly (excess in adults).
  6. PRL: mammary growth, milk formation; TSH → thyroid; ACTH → glucocorticoids.
  7. LH: androgens (male), ovulation and corpus luteum maintenance (female); FSH: spermatogenesis with androgens, follicle growth.
  8. MSH: melanocytes, pigmentation. Oxytocin: uterine contraction at birth, milk ejection. ADH: water reabsorption; deficiency = diabetes insipidus.
  9. Pineal (dorsal forebrain): melatonin; diurnal rhythm, sleep-wake cycle, body temperature, metabolism, pigmentation, menstrual cycle, defence.
  10. Thyroid: two lobes + isthmus; T₄ and T₃ (need iodine); BMR, RBC formation, metabolism; TCT lowers blood Ca²⁺.
  11. Goitre (iodine deficiency); cretinism (hypothyroid pregnancy); Graves’ disease = exophthalmic goitre (bulging eyes, ↑BMR, weight loss).
  12. Parathyroid (4, behind thyroid): PTH ↑ blood Ca²⁺ (bone resorption, renal reabsorption, gut absorption) = hypercalcemic.
  13. Thymus: thymosins → T-lymphocyte differentiation, antibody production; degenerates with age.
  14. Adrenal medulla: adrenaline, noradrenaline (catecholamines, fight or flight): alertness, pupil dilation, piloerection, sweating, ↑ heart rate and respiration, glycogenolysis.
  15. Adrenal cortex: zona glomerulosa (outer), fasciculata, reticularis (inner). Cortisol (gluconeogenesis, anti-inflammatory, immunosuppressive, RBC production); aldosterone (Na⁺ and water reabsorption, K⁺ excretion); androgens (puberty hair). Deficiency = Addison’s disease.
  16. Pancreas: 1–2 million islets (1–2% of tissue); α-cells glucagon (hyperglycemic; glycogenolysis, gluconeogenesis); β-cells insulin (glucose uptake, glycogenesis). Diabetes mellitus: glucosuria, ketone bodies; insulin therapy.
  17. Testis: Leydig cells → testosterone: accessory organs, secondary characters, spermatogenesis, libido, anabolic effects.
  18. Ovary: estrogen (growing follicles; secondary characters, mammary development); progesterone (corpus luteum; supports pregnancy, alveoli, milk secretion).
  19. ANF (atria) ↓ BP; erythropoietin (kidney JG cells) → RBCs; gastrin (HCl, pepsinogen); secretin (water, bicarbonate); CCK (pancreatic enzymes, bile); GIP (inhibits gastric secretion and motility).
  20. Receptors are specific; membrane-bound → second messengers (cAMP, IP₃, Ca²⁺); intracellular (steroids, iodothyronines) → gene expression.
  21. Chemical classes: peptide/protein (insulin, glucagon), steroids (cortisol, testosterone, estradiol, progesterone), iodothyronines, amino-acid derivatives (epinephrine).
