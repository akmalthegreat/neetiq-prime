5.1 The DNA

DNA is a long polymer of deoxyribonucleotides; its length is defined as the number of nucleotides or base pairs.

5.1.1 Structure of polynucleotide chain

  • A nucleotide = nitrogenous base + pentose sugar + phosphate. Sugar: ribose (RNA) or deoxyribose (DNA).
  • Purines: adenine, guanine. Pyrimidines: cytosine, uracil, thymine. Cytosine is common to DNA and RNA; thymine in DNA, uracil in RNA. Thymine = 5-methyl uracil.
  • Base + sugar (at 1′ C) via N-glycosidic linkage → nucleoside. Phosphate at 5′ C via phosphoester linkage → nucleotide. Nucleotides joined by 3′–5′ phosphodiester linkage.
  • The polymer has a free phosphate at the 5′-end and a free 3′-OH at the 3′-end. Backbone = sugar and phosphate; bases project from it.
  • In RNA every nucleotide has an extra 2′-OH on ribose.
Memory trick“Pure As Gold” → Purines = A and G. “CUT the Py(e)” → Pyrimidines = C, U, T.

History and the double helix

  • DNA was first identified as an acidic substance in the nucleus by Friedrich Meischer (1869), who named it ‘Nuclein’.
  • Watson and Crick (1953) proposed the double helix using X-ray diffraction data of Maurice Wilkins and Rosalind Franklin.
  • Base pairing was based on Erwin Chargaff’s observation: in dsDNA, A/T and G/C ratios are constant and equal to one.
  1. Two polynucleotide chains; sugar-phosphate backbone outside, bases inside.
  2. Anti-parallel polarity: one 5′→3′, the other 3′→5′.
  3. A=T by two H-bonds; G≡C by three H-bonds; a purine always faces a pyrimidine → uniform distance between strands.
  4. Right-handed coil; pitch 3.4 nm, roughly 10 bp per turn, so 0.34 nm between base pairs.
  5. Stacking of base pairs, in addition to H-bonds, confers stability.
Central dogma (Francis Crick)DNA → RNA → Protein. In some viruses information flows RNA → DNA = reverse transcription.

5.1.2 Packaging of DNA helix

  • Length of DNA in a typical mammalian cell = 6.6 × 109 bp × 0.34 × 10–9 m/bp ≈ 2.2 metres, far greater than a nucleus (~10–6 m).
  • Prokaryotes (e.g. E. coli): negatively charged DNA held with positively charged proteins in the nucleoid, organised in large loops.
  • Eukaryotes: positively charged basic proteins called histones, rich in lysine and arginine. Eight histones = histone octamer; DNA wrapped around it = nucleosome (about 200 bp).
  • Nucleosomes = repeating unit of chromatin, seen as ‘beads-on-string’ under the electron microscope; further coiled into chromatin fibres and condensed at metaphase into chromosomes.
  • Higher-level packaging needs Non-histone Chromosomal (NHC) proteins.

Euchromatin

  • Loosely packed, stains light
  • Transcriptionally active

Heterochromatin

  • Densely packed, stains dark
  • Inactive

5.2 The search for genetic material

ExperimentKey observations / conclusion
Griffith (1928) — transforming principleStreptococcus pneumoniae: S strain (smooth, mucous polysaccharide coat, virulent) kills mice; R strain (rough) does not; heat-killed S does not; heat-killed S + live R kills and live S is recovered → R was transformed by a ‘transforming principle’. Biochemical nature not defined.
Avery, MacLeod and McCarty (1933–44)Only DNA from S cells transformed R cells. Proteases and RNases did not affect transformation; DNase inhibited it → DNA is the hereditary material (not all were convinced).
Hershey and Chase (1952)Bacteriophages grown with radioactive phosphorus (32P) → radioactive DNA; with radioactive sulfur (35S) → radioactive protein. After infecting E. coli, blending removed coats and centrifugation separated phages. Bacteria were radioactive only with 32P phages → DNA enters; protein does not → unequivocal proof that DNA is genetic material.
TrapDNA contains phosphorus, not sulfur; protein contains sulfur, not phosphorus. Blender = remove viral coats; centrifuge = separate phage from bacteria.

5.3 Properties of genetic material: DNA vs RNA

  • Criteria: (i) replication; (ii) chemical and structural stability; (iii) scope for slow changes (mutation) for evolution; (iv) ability to express as Mendelian characters.
  • The 2′-OH in RNA is reactive → RNA is labile and easily degraded; RNA is also catalytic. Thymine in place of uracil adds stability to DNA.
  • RNA mutates faster → RNA viruses mutate and evolve faster. RNA can directly code for proteins; DNA depends on RNA.
NEET HighlightDNA is preferred for storage of genetic information (more stable); RNA is better for transmission (expression). RNA world: RNA was the first genetic material (and a catalyst); DNA evolved from RNA with modifications making it more stable, and its double strand allows repair.

5.4 Replication

Watson and Crick proposed semiconservative replication: each new DNA has one parental and one new strand.

Meselson and Stahl (1958)

  1. E. coli grown for many generations in 15NH4Cl (15N = heavy, non-radioactive isotope) → heavy DNA.
  2. Transferred to 14NH4Cl; DNA separated by caesium chloride (CsCl) density gradient centrifugation.
  3. After one generation (20 min): all DNA of hybrid/intermediate density.
  4. After two generations (40 min): equal amounts of hybrid and light DNA.
AlsoTaylor and colleagues (1958) used radioactive thymidine on Vicia faba (faba beans) to prove that DNA in chromosomes also replicates semiconservatively.

The machinery and the enzymes

  • Main enzyme: DNA-dependent DNA polymerase. E. coli (4.6 × 106 bp) replicates in 18 minutes → about 2000 bp per second, with high accuracy (mistakes = mutations).
  • Deoxyribonucleoside triphosphates act as substrates and provide energy (two terminal high-energy phosphates).
  • Replication occurs in a small opening of the helix = replication fork.
  • DNA polymerase works only 5′→3′: on the template with polarity 3′→5′ synthesis is continuous; on the 5′→3′ template it is discontinuous; fragments are joined by DNA ligase.
  • Polymerases cannot initiate replication; it starts at a definite origin of replication. That is why recombinant DNA needs a vector (provides the origin).
  • In eukaryotes replication occurs in the S-phase; failure of cell division after replication → polyploidy.
TrapContinuous synthesis is on the template strand with polarity 3′→5′. 15N is not radioactive; it is separated by density. Meselson–Stahl used E. coli; Taylor used Vicia faba.

5.5 Transcription

  • Copying genetic information from one strand of DNA into RNA; adenine pairs with uracil. Only a segment of DNA and only one strand is copied.
  • Both strands are not copied because: (i) one DNA segment would code for two different proteins; (ii) the two RNAs would be complementary and form dsRNA, preventing translation.

  • Promoter: binding site for RNA polymerase; its position defines template and coding strands.

5.5.2 Transcription unit and the gene

  • Cistron = DNA segment coding for a polypeptide. Structural genes are monocistronic (mostly eukaryotes) or polycistronic (mostly prokaryotes).
  • Eukaryotic genes are split: exons (expressed, appear in mature RNA) interrupted by introns (intervening sequences, absent in mature RNA).
  • Regulatory sequences are loosely called regulatory genes, though they code for no RNA or protein.

5.6 Genetic code

  • George Gamow (physicist) argued the code should be a triplet: 43 = 64 codons for 20 amino acids.
  • Har Gobind Khorana: chemical method to synthesise RNA with defined bases (homopolymers, copolymers). Marshall Nirenberg: cell-free system for protein synthesis. Severo Ochoa: enzyme polynucleotide phosphorylase for template-independent RNA synthesis.

5.6.1 Mutations and the code

  • Point mutation in β-globin: glutamate → valine → sickle cell anaemia.
  • Insertion/deletion of one or two bases changes the reading frame; of three (or multiples) adds/removes whole codons and the reading frame is unaltered beyond → frameshift insertion/deletion mutations; proof that the codon is a triplet read contiguously.

5.6.2 tRNA — the adapter molecule

  • Francis Crick postulated an adapter molecule. tRNA was earlier called sRNA (soluble RNA).
  • tRNA has an anticodon loop (complementary to the codon) and an amino acid acceptor end. tRNAs are specific for each amino acid; a special initiator tRNA; no tRNAs for stop codons.
  • Secondary structure: clover-leaf; actual 3-D structure: inverted L.
Memory trickStop codons: “U Are Away, U Are Gone, U Go Away” → UAA, UAG, UGA.

5.7 Translation

  • Polymerisation of amino acids into a polypeptide by peptide bonds, in the order dictated by mRNA.
  • Charging of tRNA / aminoacylation: amino acids are activated in the presence of ATP and linked to their cognate tRNA.
  • Ribosome = structural RNAs + about 80 different proteins; exists as large and small subunits. Translation begins when the small subunit meets mRNA. The large subunit has two sites for amino acids.
  • Ribosome is also the catalyst: 23S rRNA in bacteria acts as the enzyme — a ribozyme.
  • Translational unit is flanked by start codon (AUG) and a stop codon; untranslated regions (UTRs) are at the 5′-end (before start) and 3′-end (after stop), needed for efficient translation.
Initiation: ribosome + mRNA at AUG, initiator tRNA→Elongation: charged tRNAs pair by anticodon; ribosome moves codon to codon→Termination: release factor binds stop codon

5.8 Regulation of gene expression

  • Eukaryotes regulate at (i) transcriptional level, (ii) processing (splicing), (iii) mRNA transport from nucleus to cytoplasm, (iv) translational level.
  • In prokaryotes, control of the rate of transcriptional initiation is predominant. Regulatory proteins act positively (activators) or negatively (repressors); repressors bind the operator, adjacent to the promoter. Each operon has its own specific operator and repressor.

5.8.1 The lac operon

Elucidated by geneticist François Jacob and biochemist Jacques Monod — the first transcriptionally regulated system. A polycistronic structural gene with a common promoter and regulatory genes = operon.

  • Lactose (or allolactose) is the inducer; it inactivates the repressor so RNA polymerase can reach the promoter. A very low level of lac operon expression is always present, otherwise lactose cannot enter cells.
  • Glucose or galactose cannot act as inducers. Regulation by the repressor = negative regulation; the lac operon is also under positive regulation.
TrapThe repressor is made all the time (constitutive), not only when lactose is absent. The inducer acts on the repressor, not on the operator. y = permease, a = transacetylase.

5.9 Human Genome Project (HGP)

  • Launched in 1990, a 13-year mega project, completed in 2003; coordinated by the U.S. Department of Energy and National Institute of Health; Wellcome Trust (U.K.) became a major partner; contributions from Japan, France, Germany, China and others.
  • ~3 × 109 bp at US $3 per bp → about 9 billion US dollars; printed at 1000 letters/page and 1000 pages/book → 3300 books. Closely linked to the new field of bioinformatics.
Goals of HGP
Identify all approximately 20,000–25,000 genes; determine the sequence of 3 billion base pairs; store it in databases; improve analysis tools; transfer technologies to industry; address ethical, legal and social issues (ELSI)

Methodologies

  • Expressed Sequence Tags (ESTs): identify all genes expressed as RNA. Sequence annotation: sequence the whole genome (coding + non-coding) and later assign functions.
  • DNA cut into random fragments, cloned in hosts (bacteria, yeast) using vectors BAC (bacterial artificial chromosome) and YAC (yeast artificial chromosome); sequenced by automated sequencers based on Frederick Sanger’s method; overlapping sequences aligned by computer.
  • Chromosome 1 was the last of the 24 chromosomes to be sequenced (May 2006). Maps used polymorphism of restriction sites and microsatellites.

5.10 DNA fingerprinting

  • Identifies differences in repetitive DNA. In density gradient centrifugation, bulk DNA forms a major peak and small peaks = satellite DNA (micro-satellites, mini-satellites etc.), which usually codes for no protein but shows high polymorphism.
  • An inheritable mutation found at high frequency in a population = DNA polymorphism; more likely in non-coding DNA.
  • Developed by Alec Jeffreys, using VNTR (Variable Number of Tandem Repeats) as probe. VNTR is a mini-satellite; its size varies from 0.1 to 20 kb.
Isolate DNA→Digest with restriction endonucleases→Electrophoresis→Blot to nitrocellulose / nylon→Hybridise with labelled VNTR probe→Autoradiography
  • The band pattern differs between individuals except monozygotic (identical) twins. DNA from any tissue (blood, hair follicle, skin, bone, saliva, sperm) gives the same pattern → forensic use and paternity testing; also used for population and genetic diversity.
  • Sensitivity increased by PCR: DNA from a single cell is enough.
TrapOriginal technique used Southern blot hybridisation with a radiolabelled VNTR probe. VNTR = mini-satellite (not micro). Identical twins give the same fingerprint.
