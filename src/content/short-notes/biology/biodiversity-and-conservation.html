13.1 Biodiversity

The term biodiversity was popularised by sociobiologist Edward Wilson to describe the combined diversity at all levels of biological organisation.

LevelNCERT example

Species diversityWestern Ghats have greater amphibian species diversity than the Eastern Ghats

13.1.1 How many species are there on earth and in India?

  • IUCN (2004): slightly more than 1.5 million plant and animal species described so far.
  • Extreme estimates: 20 to 50 million; the conservative, scientifically sound estimate by Robert May: about 7 million.
  • Estimates come from comparing temperate–tropical species richness of an exhaustively studied group (insects) and extrapolating.
  • Animals > 70% of recorded species; plants (including algae, fungi, bryophytes, gymnosperms, angiosperms) no more than 22%. Insects make up more than 70% of animals — 7 out of every 10 animals.
  • Number of fungi species is more than the combined total of fishes, amphibians, reptiles and mammals.
  • Prokaryotes are not included: conventional taxonomic methods do not suit them and many are not culturable; by molecular criteria their diversity might run into millions.
IndiaIndia has only 2.4% of the world’s land area but 8.1% of global species diversity → one of the 12 mega diversity countries. Nearly 45,000 plant species and twice as many animals recorded. Using May’s estimate (only 22% recorded), India likely has more than 1,00,000 plant and 3,00,000 animal species yet to be discovered.

13.1.2 Patterns of biodiversity

(i) Latitudinal gradients

Species diversity generally decreases from the equator towards the poles. The tropics (23.5° N to 23.5° S) harbour more species than temperate or polar areas.

  • A tropical forest in Ecuador has up to 10 times as many vascular plant species as an equal temperate forest in the Midwest USA.
  • Amazonian rain forest has the greatest biodiversity on earth: > 40,000 plants, 3,000 fishes, 1,300 birds, 427 mammals, 427 amphibians, 378 reptiles, > 1,25,000 invertebrates; possibly two million undiscovered insects.
Why tropics are richer(a) Speciation is a function of time: tropics remained undisturbed for millions of years (no frequent glaciations); (b) tropical environments are less seasonal, more constant and predictable → niche specialisation; (c) more solar energy → higher productivity → indirectly greater diversity.

(ii) Species–area relationships

Alexander von Humboldt (German naturalist) observed that species richness increases with explored area, but only up to a limit. The relationship is a rectangular hyperbola; on a logarithmic scale it is a straight line.

  • log S = log C + Z log A: S = species richness, A = area, Z = slope (regression coefficient), C = Y-intercept.
  • Z = 0.1 to 0.2 regardless of taxon or region (plants in Britain, birds in California, molluscs in New York).
  • For very large areas like entire continents, slopes are much steeper: Z = 0.6 to 1.2; for frugivorous birds and mammals in tropical forests of different continents, Z = 1.15.
TrapNormal Z: 0.1–0.2; continents: 0.6–1.2; frugivores: 1.15. On a normal scale the curve is a rectangular hyperbola, not a straight line.

(iii) Importance of species diversity to the ecosystem

  • Communities with more species tend to be more stable: less year-to-year variation in productivity, resistant or resilient to disturbances, resistant to alien invasions.
  • David Tilman’s outdoor plots: more species → less year-to-year variation in total biomass and higher productivity.
Rivet popper hypothesisBy Stanford ecologist Paul Ehrlich: an airplane (ecosystem) is held by thousands of rivets (species). Popping a few may not affect flight safety, but more and more removal weakens it. Losing rivets on the wings (key species driving major functions) is more serious than on seats or windows.

13.1.3 Loss of biodiversity

  • Colonisation of tropical Pacific Islands by humans led to extinction of more than 2,000 species of native birds.
  • IUCN Red List (2004): 784 extinctions in the last 500 years (338 vertebrates, 359 invertebrates, 87 plants). Last twenty years: 27 species disappeared.
  • More than 15,500 species face threat of extinction. Amphibians appear more vulnerable.

Sixth ExtinctionThere were five mass extinctions in > 3 billion years. The ‘Sixth Extinction’ now in progress is 100 to 1,000 times faster than in pre-human times, due to human activities. Nearly half of all species might be wiped out within the next 100 years.
Effects of loss(i) Decline in plant production; (ii) lowered resistance to perturbations such as drought; (iii) increased variability in plant productivity, water use, pest and disease cycles.

Causes — ‘The Evil Quartet’

CauseKey facts (NCERT)
(i) Habitat loss and fragmentation (most important)Tropical rain forests once covered > 14% of land, now no more than 6%. Amazon (‘lungs of the planet’) cleared for soya beans and grasslands for beef cattle. Fragmentation hurts animals needing large territories and migratory animals.
(ii) Over-exploitation (‘need’ → ‘greed’)Steller’s sea cow, passenger pigeon; many marine fish over-harvested
(iii) Alien species invasionsNile perch in Lake Victoria (east Africa) → extinction of > 200 cichlid fish species. Invasive weeds: carrot grass (Parthenium), Lantana, water hyacinth (Eicchornia). Illegal African catfish Clarias gariepinus threatens native catfishes.
(iv) Co-extinctionsExtinction of a host takes its obligate associates: a host fish with its unique parasites; co-evolved plant–pollinator mutualists
Memory trickEvil Quartet = “HOAC” → Habitat loss, Over-exploitation, Alien invasion, Co-extinction.

13.2 Biodiversity conservation — why?

ArgumentDetail (NCERT)
Narrowly utilitarianDirect economic benefits: food, firewood, fibre, construction material, industrial products (tannins, lubricating oils, dyes, resins, perfumes), medicines. > 25% of drugs sold worldwide come from plants; 25,000 plant species used in traditional medicine. Bioprospecting: exploring molecular, genetic and species diversity for economically important products.
Broadly utilitarianEcosystem services: Amazon produces about 20% of earth’s atmospheric oxygen; pollination by bees, bumblebees, birds, bats; intangible aesthetic pleasures
EthicalEvery species has intrinsic value; a moral duty to care for them and pass on our biological legacy

13.2 How do we conserve biodiversity?

In situ (on site)

  • Protect the whole ecosystem — save the entire forest to save the tiger
  • Hotspots, biosphere reserves, national parks, sanctuaries, sacred groves

Ex situ (off site)

  • For threatened species needing urgent measures: taken out of their habitat to zoological parks, botanical gardens, wildlife safari parks
  • Cryopreservation of gametes, in vitro fertilisation, tissue culture, seed banks

Biodiversity hotspots

  • Regions with very high species richness and a high degree of endemism (species found nowhere else); also regions of accelerated habitat loss.
  • Initially 25 hotspots; nine more added → 34 in total.
  • Three cover India: Western Ghats and Sri Lanka, Indo-Burma, Himalaya.
  • All hotspots cover less than 2% of earth’s land; strict protection could reduce ongoing mass extinctions by almost 30%.
