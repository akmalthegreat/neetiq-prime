15.1 Blood

Blood is a special connective tissue consisting of a fluid matrix, plasma, and formed elements.

15.1.1 Plasma (~55% of blood)

  • A straw coloured, viscous fluid. 90–92% of plasma is water and proteins contribute 6–8%.
  • Plasma also contains small amounts of minerals like Na+, Ca++, Mg++, HCO3–, Cl–; glucose, amino acids, lipids etc. are always in transit.
  • Factors for coagulation (clotting) are present in plasma in an inactive form.
Plasma proteinFunction
FibrinogenClotting / coagulation of blood
GlobulinsDefence mechanisms of the body
AlbuminsOsmotic balance
DefinitionSerum = plasma without the clotting factors.

15.1.2 Formed elements (~45% of blood)

ElementCountKey facts
RBCs (erythrocytes)5–5.5 million mm–3Most abundant cells; formed in red bone marrow in adults; devoid of nucleus in most mammals; biconcave; contain haemoglobin (12–16 g/100 mL blood); average life span 120 days; destroyed in the spleen (graveyard of RBCs)
WBCs (leucocytes)6000–8000 mm–3Colourless (no haemoglobin), nucleated, relatively fewer, generally short-lived
Platelets (thrombocytes)1,50,000–3,50,000 mm–3Cell fragments produced from megakaryocytes (special cells in bone marrow); release substances involved in coagulation. Reduction leads to clotting disorders → excessive blood loss.

15.1.3 Blood groups

ABO grouping

Based on the presence or absence of two surface antigens (chemicals that can induce immune response) on RBCs, A and B. Plasma of different individuals contains natural antibodies (proteins produced in response to antigens).

Blood groupAntigens on RBCsAntibodies in plasmaDonor’s group
AAanti-BA, O
BBanti-AB, O
ABA, BnilAB, A, B, O
Onilanti-A, anti-BO
NEET HighlightGroup O = universal donor; group AB = universal recipient. Blood of a donor must be matched with that of a recipient before any transfusion to avoid clumping (destruction of RBCs).

Rh grouping

  • Rh antigen is similar to one present in Rhesus monkeys; observed on the surface of RBCs of the majority (nearly 80%) of humans → Rh positive (Rh+ve).
  • An Rh–ve person, if exposed to Rh+ve blood, will form specific antibodies against the Rh antigens.
Erythroblastosis foetalisRh incompatibility between an Rh–ve mother and her Rh+ve foetus. In the first pregnancy the Rh antigens of the foetus do not get exposed to the mother’s blood, as the two bloods are well separated by the placenta. During delivery of the first child, the mother may be exposed to small amounts of the foetal Rh+ve blood → she starts preparing antibodies. In subsequent pregnancies, these antibodies can leak into the foetal blood and destroy foetal RBCs → fatal, severe anaemia and jaundice. Avoided by administering anti-Rh antibodies to the mother immediately after the delivery of the first child.
TrapThe first child is usually safe; the problem arises from the second Rh+ve pregnancy. Mother must be Rh–ve and foetus Rh+ve (not the reverse).

15.1.4 Coagulation of blood

  • A clot or coagulum is formed mainly of a network of threads called fibrins in which dead and damaged formed elements of blood are trapped.
  • An injury or trauma stimulates platelets to release certain factors which activate the mechanism of coagulation. Certain factors released by the tissues at the site of injury can also initiate coagulation.
  • Calcium ions play a very important role in clotting.
Injury → platelets / tissue factors→Cascade → thrombokinase→Prothrombin → Thrombin→Fibrinogen → Fibrin→Clot
Key ideaFibrins are formed by conversion of inactive fibrinogens by the enzyme thrombin. Thrombins are formed from another inactive substance, prothrombin. An enzyme complex, thrombokinase, is required for this reaction; it is formed by a series of linked enzymic reactions (cascade process) involving a number of inactive factors in plasma.

15.2 Lymph (tissue fluid)

  • As blood passes through capillaries, some water along with many small water-soluble substances move out into the spaces between cells, leaving the larger proteins and most formed elements in the vessels. This fluid is called interstitial fluid or tissue fluid. It has the same mineral distribution as plasma.
  • Exchange of nutrients, gases etc. between blood and cells always occurs through this fluid.
  • An elaborate network of vessels, the lymphatic system, collects this fluid and drains it back to the major veins. The fluid present in the lymphatic system is called lymph.
  • Lymph is a colourless fluid containing specialised lymphocytes responsible for immune responses.
  • An important carrier for nutrients, hormones etc. Fats are absorbed through lymph in the lacteals present in the intestinal villi.

15.3 Circulatory pathways

Open circulatory system

  • Arthropods and molluscs
  • Blood pumped by the heart passes through large vessels into open spaces or body cavities called sinuses

Closed circulatory system

  • Annelids and chordates
  • Blood is always circulated through a closed network of vessels; more advantageous as flow can be more precisely regulated
Vertebrate groupHeartCirculation
Fishes2-chambered (1 atrium + 1 ventricle)Heart pumps out deoxygenated blood, oxygenated by gills, supplied to body → single circulation
Amphibians and reptiles (except crocodiles)3-chambered (2 atria + 1 ventricle)Left atrium gets oxygenated blood (gills/lungs/skin), right atrium gets deoxygenated blood; they mix in the single ventricle → incomplete double circulation
Crocodiles, birds and mammals4-chambered (2 atria + 2 ventricles)No mixing → double circulation
TrapCrocodiles are reptiles but have a 4-chambered heart. Fish heart carries only deoxygenated blood.

15.3.1 Human circulatory system: the heart

  • Heart is mesodermally derived; situated in the thoracic cavity between the two lungs, slightly tilted to the left; size of a clenched fist.
  • Protected by a double-walled membranous bag, pericardium, enclosing the pericardial fluid.
  • Four chambers: two relatively small upper atria and two larger lower ventricles.
Partition / valveLocation
Inter-atrial septumThin, muscular wall separating the right and left atria
Inter-ventricular septumThick-walled, separates the left and right ventricles
Atrio-ventricular septumThick fibrous tissue separating atrium and ventricle of the same side; each has an opening connecting the two chambers
Tricuspid valveBetween right atrium and right ventricle; three muscular flaps/cusps
Bicuspid (mitral) valveBetween left atrium and left ventricle
Semilunar valvesAt openings of right and left ventricles into the pulmonary artery and aorta respectively

Valves allow flow in only one direction and prevent backward flow. The entire heart is made of cardiac muscles; walls of ventricles are much thicker than those of atria.

Nodal tissue and conduction

StructureLocation / role
Sino-atrial node (SAN)Right upper corner of the right atrium. Generates the maximum number of action potentials → initiates and maintains rhythmic contraction → pacemaker
Atrio-ventricular node (AVN)Lower left corner of the right atrium, close to the atrio-ventricular septum
AV bundleContinues from AVN, passes through the atrio-ventricular septa to the top of the inter-ventricular septum, then divides into right and left bundles
Purkinje fibresMinute fibres throughout the ventricular musculature. Purkinje fibres + right and left bundles = bundle of His
Key ideaNodal musculature can generate action potentials without any external stimuli → autoexcitable. Heart normally beats 70–75 times per minute (average 72).
TrapBoth SAN and AVN are in the right atrium. Tricuspid = right side; bicuspid/mitral = left side.

15.3.2 Cardiac cycle

  1. Joint diastole: all four chambers relaxed; tricuspid and bicuspid valves open; semilunar valves closed; blood flows from pulmonary veins and vena cava into ventricles through the atria.
  2. Atrial systole: SAN fires → both atria contract simultaneously → increases flow of blood into ventricles by about 30%.
  3. Impulse conducted by AVN and AV bundle; bundle of His spreads it through the ventricles → ventricular systole; atria relax (atrial diastole) at the same time.
  4. Rising ventricular pressure closes tricuspid and bicuspid valves; further rise forces open the semilunar valves → blood enters pulmonary artery (right) and aorta (left).
  5. Ventricular diastole: pressure falls → semilunar valves close; as pressure falls further, tricuspid and bicuspid valves are pushed open by atrial pressure → joint diastole again.
QuantityValue
Duration of one cardiac cycle0.8 seconds (at 72 beats/min)
Stroke volumeEach ventricle pumps approx. 70 mL per cycle
Cardiac output = stroke volume × heart rateVolume pumped by each ventricle per minute; averages 5000 mL (5 litres). Much higher in an athlete.

First heart sound — “lub”

  • Closure of tricuspid and bicuspid valves

Second heart sound — “dub”

  • Closure of semilunar valves

15.3.3 Electrocardiograph (ECG)

A graphical representation of the electrical activity of the heart during a cardiac cycle. A standard ECG uses three electrical leads (one to each wrist and one to the left ankle).

WaveRepresents
P-waveElectrical excitation (depolarisation) of the atria → contraction of both atria
QRS complexDepolarisation of the ventricles → initiates ventricular contraction; contraction starts shortly after Q and marks the beginning of systole
T-waveReturn of ventricles from excited to normal state (repolarisation); end of T-wave marks the end of systole
NEET HighlightCounting the number of QRS complexes in a given time period gives the heart beat rate.

15.4 Double circulation

Layer of artery/veinMade of
Tunica intima (inner)Squamous endothelium
Tunica media (middle)Smooth muscle and elastic fibres; comparatively thin in veins
Tunica externa (outer)Fibrous connective tissue with collagen fibres

Pulmonary circulation

  • Right ventricle → pulmonary artery → lungs (oxygenated) → pulmonary veins → left atrium

Systemic circulation

  • Left ventricle → aorta → arteries, arterioles, capillaries → tissues → venules, veins, vena cava → right atrium
  • Hepatic portal system: the hepatic portal vein carries blood from the intestine to the liver before it is delivered to the systemic circulation.
  • A special coronary system of blood vessels is present exclusively for circulation to and from the cardiac musculature.
TrapPulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood; pulmonary vein carries oxygenated blood — the exception to “arteries carry oxygenated blood”.

15.5 Regulation of cardiac activity

  • Normal activities of the heart are regulated intrinsically, i.e., auto-regulated by specialised muscles (nodal tissue) → heart is myogenic.
  • A special neural centre in the medulla oblongata can moderate cardiac function through the autonomic nervous system (ANS).
SignalEffect
Sympathetic nervesIncrease rate of heart beat, strength of ventricular contraction → cardiac output ↑
Parasympathetic signalsDecrease rate of heart beat, speed of conduction of action potential → cardiac output ↓
Adrenal medullary hormonesCan also increase cardiac output

15.6 Disorders of circulatory system

DisorderKey points (NCERT)
High blood pressure (hypertension)Normal BP is 120/80 mm Hg (120 = systolic/pumping; 80 = diastolic/resting). Repeated readings of 140/90 or higher = hypertension. Leads to heart diseases and also affects vital organs like brain and kidney.
Coronary Artery Disease (CAD) / atherosclerosisAffects vessels supplying blood to the heart muscle; caused by deposits of calcium, fat, cholesterol and fibrous tissues → lumen of arteries becomes narrower
Angina (angina pectoris)Acute chest pain when not enough oxygen reaches the heart muscle; more common among middle-aged and elderly
Heart failureHeart not pumping blood effectively enough to meet body needs; called congestive heart failure because congestion of the lungs is a main symptom
TrapHeart failure ≠ cardiac arrest (heart stops beating) ≠ heart attack (heart muscle suddenly damaged by inadequate blood supply).
