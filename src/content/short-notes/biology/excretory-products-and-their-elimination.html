16.0 Modes of excretion

Ammonia, urea and uric acid are the major forms of nitrogenous wastes excreted by animals.

ModeWasteToxicity / water neededExamples (NCERT)
AmmonotelismAmmoniaMost toxic; needs large amount of waterMany bony fishes, aquatic amphibians, aquatic insects
UreotelismUreaLess toxicMammals, many terrestrial amphibians, marine fishes
UricotelismUric acid (pellet/paste)Least toxic; minimum loss of waterReptiles, birds, land snails, insects
  • In ammonotelic animals, ammonia (readily soluble) is excreted by diffusion across body surfaces or through gill surfaces (in fish) as ammonium ions; kidneys do not play any significant role.
  • In ureotelic animals, ammonia produced by metabolism is converted into urea in the liver, released into blood, filtered and excreted by the kidneys. Some urea may be retained in the kidney matrix of some animals to maintain a desired osmolarity.
TrapMarine fishes are ureotelic, but many bony (freshwater) fishes are ammonotelic. Urea is made in the liver, not the kidney.

16.1 Human excretory system

Consists of a pair of kidneys, one pair of ureters, a urinary bladder and a urethra.

FeatureDetail
Shape and positionReddish brown, bean-shaped; between the levels of the last thoracic and third lumbar vertebra, close to the dorsal inner wall of the abdominal cavity
Size (adult)Length 10–12 cm, width 5–7 cm, thickness 2–3 cm; average weight 120–170 g
HilumNotch on the inner concave surface through which ureter, blood vessels and nerves enter
Renal pelvis and calycesBroad funnel-shaped space inner to the hilum, with projections called calyces
ZonesOuter cortex, inner medulla; medulla divided into medullary pyramids projecting into calyces; cortex extends between pyramids as renal columns = Columns of Bertini

Nephron

Each kidney has nearly one million nephrons, the functional units. Each has two parts: glomerulus and renal tubule.

Afferent arteriole → Glomerulus → Efferent arteriole
Bowman’s capsule→PCT→Henle’s loop (descending → ascending)→DCT→Collecting duct→Renal pelvis
  • Glomerulus = tuft of capillaries formed by the afferent arteriole (fine branch of the renal artery); blood leaves by the efferent arteriole.
  • Glomerulus + Bowman’s capsule = Malpighian body or renal corpuscle.
  • Malpighian corpuscle, PCT and DCT lie in the cortex; loop of Henle dips into the medulla.
  • Efferent arteriole forms the peritubular capillaries; a minute vessel of this network runs parallel to Henle’s loop forming the U-shaped vasa recta.

Cortical nephrons

  • Majority; loop of Henle too short, extends only very little into medulla
  • Vasa recta absent or highly reduced

Juxta medullary nephrons

  • Loop of Henle very long, runs deep into the medulla
  • Well-developed vasa recta

16.2 Urine formation

Three processes: glomerular filtration, reabsorption and secretion.

Glomerular filtration (ultrafiltration)

  • On average, 1100–1200 mL of blood is filtered by the kidneys per minute, roughly 1/5th of the blood pumped out by each ventricle per minute.
  • Filtration through 3 layers: endothelium of glomerular blood vessels, epithelium of Bowman’s capsule and a basement membrane between them.
  • Epithelial cells of Bowman’s capsule, called podocytes, leave minute spaces called filtration slits or slit pores.
  • Almost all constituents of plasma except proteins pass → ultrafiltration.
NEET HighlightGFR (filtrate formed per minute) in a healthy person ≈ 125 mL/min, i.e., 180 litres per day. Urine released ≈ 1.5 L/day → nearly 99% of filtrate is reabsorbed.
JGA and GFRJuxta glomerular apparatus (JGA): special sensitive region formed by cellular modifications in the DCT and the afferent arteriole at their point of contact. A fall in GFR activates JG cells to release renin, which stimulates glomerular blood flow and brings GFR back to normal.

Reabsorption and secretion

  • Substances like glucose, amino acids, Na+ are reabsorbed actively; nitrogenous wastes are absorbed by passive transport; water reabsorption is also passive in the initial segments.
  • Tubular cells secrete H+, K+ and ammonia into the filtrate. Tubular secretion helps maintain ionic and acid-base balance.

16.3 Function of the tubules

SegmentKey functions (NCERT)
PCTLined by simple cuboidal brush border epithelium (↑ surface area). Reabsorbs nearly all essential nutrients and 70–80% of electrolytes and water. Maintains pH and ionic balance by secreting H+, ammonia, K+ and absorbing HCO3–.
Henle’s loop — descending limbPermeable to water, almost impermeable to electrolytes → filtrate gets concentrated as it moves down
Henle’s loop — ascending limbImpermeable to water, allows transport of electrolytes actively or passively → filtrate gets diluted as it moves up. Reabsorption is minimum here, but the loop maintains the high osmolarity of medullary interstitial fluid.
DCTConditional reabsorption of Na+ and water; reabsorbs HCO3–; selectively secretes H+, K+, NH3 → pH and Na–K balance
Collecting ductLarge amounts of water reabsorbed → concentrated urine. Allows small amounts of urea into the medullary interstitium to keep up osmolarity. Secretes H+ and K+.
TrapDescending limb = water out (concentrates); ascending limb = salts out, water stays (dilutes). NEET often swaps the two limbs. Maximum reabsorption happens in the PCT.

16.4 Mechanism of concentration of the filtrate

  • Mammals produce concentrated urine thanks to Henle’s loop and vasa recta. Flow in the two limbs of Henle’s loop is in opposite directions → counter current; flow in the two limbs of vasa recta is also counter current.
  • This maintains an increasing osmolarity towards the inner medulla: from 300 mOsmol L–1 in the cortex to about 1200 mOsmol L–1 in the inner medulla.
  • The gradient is mainly caused by NaCl and urea. NaCl from the ascending limb of Henle’s loop is exchanged with the descending limb of vasa recta and returned to the interstitium by the ascending vasa recta. Small amounts of urea enter the thin ascending limb and are transported back to the interstitium by the collecting tubule.
NEET HighlightHuman kidneys can produce urine nearly four times as concentrated as the initial filtrate (300 → 1200 mOsmol L–1).

16.5 Regulation of kidney function

Hormonal feedback mechanisms involving the hypothalamus, JGA and, to a certain extent, the heart.

MechanismTriggerAction
ADH (vasopressin)Excessive loss of fluid activates osmoreceptors → hypothalamus → ADH released from the neurohypophysisFacilitates water reabsorption from latter parts of the tubule → prevents diuresis. Also constricts blood vessels → ↑ BP → ↑ GFR. Increase in body fluid volume switches off osmoreceptors (feedback).
Renin–angiotensin (RAAS)Fall in glomerular blood flow / BP / GFR → JG cells release reninRenin: angiotensinogen → angiotensin I → angiotensin II. Angiotensin II is a powerful vasoconstrictor (↑ glomerular BP, ↑ GFR) and activates the adrenal cortex to release aldosterone → reabsorption of Na+ and water from distal tubule → ↑ BP and GFR
ANF (Atrial Natriuretic Factor)Increased blood flow to the atria of the heartCauses vasodilation → ↓ BP; acts as a check on the renin–angiotensin mechanism
TrapADH comes from the neurohypophysis (made in hypothalamus). Aldosterone is from the adrenal cortex, not medulla. ANF opposes RAAS.

16.6 Micturition

  • Urine is stored in the urinary bladder till a voluntary signal is given by the CNS. Stretch receptors on the bladder wall signal the CNS as it fills; the CNS initiates contraction of bladder smooth muscles and relaxation of the urethral sphincter.
  • Process = micturition; neural mechanism = micturition reflex.

16.8 Disorders of the excretory system

Disorder / treatmentKey points (NCERT)
UremiaAccumulation of urea in blood due to kidney malfunction; highly harmful, may lead to kidney failure
HaemodialysisBlood from a convenient artery → anticoagulant heparin added → dialysing unit with coiled cellophane tube in dialysing fluid (same as plasma except nitrogenous wastes) → wastes diffuse out → anti-heparin added → returned through a vein
Kidney transplantationUltimate method for acute renal failure; donor preferably a close relative to minimise rejection
Renal calculiStone or insoluble mass of crystallised salts (oxalates etc.) within the kidney
GlomerulonephritisInflammation of glomeruli of the kidney

★ Quick revision

  1. Ammonia most toxic (most water), uric acid least toxic (least water).
  2. Ammonotelic: bony fishes, aquatic amphibians, aquatic insects; ureotelic: mammals, terrestrial amphibians, marine fishes; uricotelic: reptiles, birds, land snails, insects.
  3. Urea formed in liver. Protonephridia/flame cells: flatworms, rotifers, some annelids, Amphioxus; nephridia: earthworm; Malpighian tubules: insects; green glands: prawns.
  4. Kidneys: last thoracic to 3rd lumbar vertebra; 10–12 × 5–7 × 2–3 cm; 120–170 g; hilum; columns of Bertini.
  5. ~1 million nephrons per kidney; renal corpuscle = glomerulus + Bowman’s capsule.
  6. Cortex: corpuscle, PCT, DCT; medulla: loop of Henle. Cortical nephrons (short loop, little/no vasa recta) vs juxta medullary (long loop).
  7. 1100–1200 mL blood filtered/min (~1/5 of cardiac output); 3-layer filter; podocytes with filtration slits; ultrafiltration (no proteins).
  8. GFR 125 mL/min = 180 L/day; urine 1.5 L/day; ~99% reabsorbed.
  9. JGA (DCT + afferent arteriole) releases renin when GFR falls.
  10. Active reabsorption: glucose, amino acids, Na⁺; passive: nitrogenous wastes, water (initial segments). Secretion: H⁺, K⁺, NH₃.
  11. PCT: brush border; all nutrients + 70–80% electrolytes and water; descending limb permeable to water; ascending limb permeable to electrolytes only.
  12. DCT: conditional Na⁺ and water reabsorption; collecting duct: water reabsorption, urea to interstitium.
  13. Counter current (Henle’s loop + vasa recta): 300 → 1200 mOsmol/L, by NaCl and urea; urine up to ~4× concentrated.
  14. ADH (neurohypophysis) → water reabsorption, prevents diuresis; RAAS: renin → angiotensin II (vasoconstrictor) → aldosterone (Na⁺, water reabsorption); ANF → vasodilation, checks RAAS.
  15. Micturition reflex: stretch receptors → CNS → bladder contracts, urethral sphincter relaxes.
  16. Urine 1–1.5 L/day, pH 6.0, 25–30 g urea/day; glycosuria and ketonuria indicate diabetes mellitus.
  17. Lungs ~200 mL CO₂/min; liver bile pigments; sweat (NaCl, urea, lactic acid); sebum (sterols, hydrocarbons, waxes).
  18. Uremia → haemodialysis (heparin, cellophane tube, anti-heparin); transplantation; renal calculi (oxalates); glomerulonephritis (inflamed glomeruli).
