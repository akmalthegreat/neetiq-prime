11.1 What do we know?

All animals, including humans, depend on plants for food. Green plants make the food they need, and through it all other organisms depend on them — this is photosynthesis, a physico-chemical process in which light energy drives the synthesis of organic compounds.

Why it matters
  • It is the primary source of all food on earth.
  • It is responsible for the release of oxygen into the atmosphere by green plants.

Classic school experiments

Variegated / half-covered leaf

A variegated leaf, or a leaf partly covered with black paper, is exposed to light and then tested for starch (iodine test).

Starch forms only in the green parts and only in parts exposed to light.

Proves: chlorophyll and light are needed.

Half-leaf experiment

Part of a leaf is enclosed in a test tube with KOH-soaked cotton (KOH absorbs CO₂); the other half is in air. Kept in light.

The exposed half tests positive for starch; the half inside the tube tests negative.

Proves: CO₂ is needed.

Trap KOH is used to remove CO₂, not to supply it. The part of the leaf inside the KOH tube is the one that gives a negative starch test.

11.2 Early experiments

The basic understanding of photosynthesis was built step by step through simple experiments.

ScientistExperimentConclusion
Joseph Priestley (1733–1804), in 1770Burning candle or a mouse in a closed bell jar → candle goes out / mouse suffocates. With a mint plant inside, the candle kept burning and the mouse lived.Plants restore to the air whatever breathing animals and burning candles remove. He discovered oxygen in 1774.
Jan Ingenhousz (1730–1799)Repeated Priestley's set-up in darkness and in sunlight; watched an aquatic plant in bright sunlight.Sunlight is essential — only green parts of plants restore the air. Small bubbles formed around green parts in light but not in dark; later identified as O₂.
Julius von Sachs (1854)Studied growing plants.Plants produce glucose, usually stored as starch; the green substance (chlorophyll) is located in special bodies (later called chloroplasts) inside cells, where glucose is made.
T.W. Engelmann (1843–1909)Split light with a prism; illuminated the green alga Cladophora kept in a suspension of aerobic bacteria.Bacteria gathered mainly in blue and red light — the first action spectrum of photosynthesis. It resembles the absorption spectra of chlorophyll a and b.
Cornelius van Niel (1897–1985)Worked on purple and green bacteria.Photosynthesis is a light-dependent reaction in which hydrogen from a suitable oxidisable compound reduces CO₂ to carbohydrates. The O₂ evolved by green plants comes from H₂O, not from CO₂.
Memory trick Order of ideas: Priestley (air restored) → Ingenhousz (light + green) → Sachs (glucose, chloroplast) → Engelmann (blue & red) → Van Niel (O₂ from water). "PISE V".

The equations

By the middle of the 19th century it was known that plants use light energy to make carbohydrates from CO₂ and water:

CO₂ + H₂O→ light →[CH₂O] + O₂

van Niel's general equation (H₂A = hydrogen donor):

2H₂A + CO₂→ light →2A + CH₂O + H₂O
  • In green plants: H₂O is the hydrogen donor and is oxidised to O₂.
  • In purple and green sulphur bacteria: the donor is H₂S, and the oxidation product is sulphur or sulphate, not O₂.
  • The idea that O₂ comes from water was later proved using radioisotopic techniques.
Key idea The correct equation for photosynthesis is:
6CO₂ + 12H₂O → (light) → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6H₂O + 6O₂
Twelve water molecules are needed because all six O₂ (12 O atoms) come from water; 6 new water molecules are formed.
Trap Photosynthesis is not a single-step reaction; the equation only sums up a multistep process. Sulphur bacteria photosynthesise without releasing O₂.

11.3 Where does photosynthesis take place?

  • It takes place in green leaves, and also in other green parts of the plant.
  • Within the leaf, it occurs mainly in the mesophyll cells, which contain a large number of chloroplasts.
  • Chloroplasts usually align along the walls of mesophyll cells so that they receive an optimum quantity of incident light.

Inside the chloroplast

  • A membranous system of grana, stroma lamellae and a fluid stroma (matrix).
  • Clear division of labour: the membrane system traps light energy and makes ATP and NADPH; enzymatic reactions in the stroma fix CO₂ into sugar, which forms starch.

Trap The "dark reaction" is a misnomer: it does not need darkness and is not light-independent — it depends on the products (ATP, NADPH) of the light reaction. Better names: carbon reactions or biosynthetic phase.

11.5 What is light reaction?

Definition Light reactions or the 'photochemical' phase include light absorption, water splitting, oxygen release, and the formation of high-energy chemical intermediates, ATP and NADPH.

Light Harvesting Complexes (LHC)

  • Pigments are organised into two discrete photochemical light harvesting complexes (LHC) within Photosystem I (PS I) and Photosystem II (PS II).
  • Each LHC is made of hundreds of pigment molecules bound to proteins.
  • Each photosystem has all the pigments (except one molecule of chlorophyll a) forming a light harvesting system, also called antennae.
  • Antennae make photosynthesis more efficient by absorbing different wavelengths of light.
  • The single chlorophyll a molecule forms the reaction centre.

11.6 The electron transport

Non-cyclic flow (Z scheme)

  1. The reaction centre of PS II absorbs 680 nm (red) light; electrons are excited and jump to an orbit farther from the nucleus. They are picked up by an electron acceptor.
  2. The acceptor passes them to an electron transport system of cytochromes. The movement is downhill in terms of oxidation-reduction (redox) potential.
  3. The electrons are passed to the pigments of PS I, not used up.
  4. At the same time, electrons in the reaction centre of PS I are excited by 700 nm light and transferred to another acceptor with a greater redox potential.
  5. These electrons move downhill again to a molecule of energy-rich NADP⁺, reducing it to NADPH + H⁺.
Key idea The whole scheme of electron transfer — from PS II, uphill to the acceptor, down the ETS to PS I, excitation, transfer to another acceptor and finally down to NADP⁺ — is called the Z scheme, because of its characteristic shape when all the carriers are placed in sequence on a redox potential scale.

Splitting of water

  • Removing electrons from PS II leaves a gap that must be filled. Electrons that replace those lost from PS II come from the splitting of water.
  • The water splitting complex is associated with PS II, which is physically located on the inner side of the thylakoid membrane.
  • Water is split into H⁺, [O] and electrons. The protons accumulate in the lumen of the thylakoid; oxygen is released as a by-product.
2H₂O→4H⁺ + O₂ + 4e⁻
NEET Highlight Oxygen is a by-product of photosynthesis, and it comes from water at PS II. The electrons from water ultimately reduce NADP⁺.

NEET Highlight
  • The membranes of stroma lamellae lack PS II as well as the NADP reductase enzyme, so the excited electron is not passed to NADP⁺ but cycled back to PS I.
  • Cyclic flow also occurs when only light of wavelengths beyond 680 nm is available for excitation.

Chemiosmotic hypothesis — how ATP is made

ATP synthesis is linked to the development of a proton gradient across the thylakoid membrane. Protons accumulate inside the membrane, in the lumen (in respiration they accumulate in the intermembrane space of mitochondria).

Three causes of the proton gradient

  1. Splitting of water occurs on the inner side of the membrane, so protons (H⁺) from water accumulate in the lumen.
  2. As electrons move through the photosystems, protons are carried across the membrane: the primary acceptor of electrons, on the outer side of the membrane, passes its electron to an H carrier, removing a proton from the stroma; when the electron is passed on to a carrier on the inner side, the proton is released into the lumen.
  3. NADP reductase is located on the stroma side; reducing NADP⁺ to NADPH + H⁺ removes protons from the stroma.
Key idea Result: protons decrease in the stroma and accumulate in the lumen → a proton gradient, i.e. a decrease in pH in the lumen. The breakdown of this gradient releases the energy used for ATP synthesis.

ATP synthase (ATPase)

CF₀

Embedded in the thylakoid membrane; forms a transmembrane channel that carries out facilitated diffusion of protons across the membrane.

CF₁

Protrudes on the outer surface of the thylakoid membrane, on the side facing the stroma. Proton flow causes a conformational change in CF₁, making the enzyme synthesise several ATP molecules.

Chemiosmosis needs A membrane, a proton pump, a proton gradient and ATP synthase (ATPase). Energy is used to pump protons across the membrane, creating a high proton concentration in the lumen; ATPase has a channel that lets protons diffuse back across, releasing enough energy to activate ATPase to make ATP.
Trap
  • Protons flow from lumen → stroma through CF₀; ATP is therefore formed on the stroma side (where CF₁ sits) — exactly where the Calvin cycle needs it.
  • Do not confuse: CF₀ = channel in membrane; CF₁ = head that makes ATP.
Memory trick Light-reaction products: ATP, NADPH, O₂ — "A N Other day of light". O₂ diffuses out; ATP and NADPH go to the stroma.

11.7 Where are the ATP and NADPH used?

Products of the light reaction are ATP, NADPH and O₂. O₂ diffuses out of the chloroplast, while ATP and NADPH drive the processes that make food (sugars) — the biosynthetic phase.

  • This phase does not directly depend on light, but it depends on light-reaction products (ATP, NADPH) besides CO₂ and H₂O.
  • If light is switched off, biosynthesis continues for some time and then stops; it starts again when light is restored.

The primary acceptor of CO₂

  • Melvin Calvin used radioactive ¹⁴C in algal photosynthesis and found that the first CO₂-fixation product is a 3-carbon organic acid — 3-phosphoglyceric acid (PGA).
  • Later, another group found plants in which the first stable product was a 4-carbon organic acid, oxaloacetic acid (OAA).
  • Hence two groups: C₃ pathway (first product 3-PGA) and C₄ pathway (first product OAA).
  • The primary CO₂ acceptor in C₃ plants is a 5-carbon ketose sugar — ribulose bisphosphate (RuBP).
Trap Scientists first expected a 2-carbon acceptor (since the product had 3 carbons). After many years of work, the acceptor turned out to be the 5-carbon RuBP, giving two molecules of 3-PGA.

The Calvin cycle

Calvin's pathway operates cyclically — RuBP is regenerated. The Calvin pathway occurs in all photosynthetic plants, whether they have C₃ or C₄ pathways. It occurs in the stroma.

11.8 The C₄ pathway

Plants adapted to dry tropical regions have the C₄ pathway. Though their first CO₂-fixation product is the C₄ oxaloacetic acid, they use the C₃ (Calvin) pathway as the main biosynthetic pathway.

How C₄ plants are special They have a special type of leaf anatomy, tolerate higher temperatures, show a response to high light intensities, lack photorespiration and have greater productivity of biomass.

Kranz anatomy

  • The large cells around the vascular bundles of C₄ plants are called bundle sheath cells, and leaves with such anatomy have 'Kranz' anatomy. 'Kranz' means 'wreath' — a reflection of the arrangement of cells.
  • Bundle sheath cells may form several layers around the vascular bundles.
  • They have a large number of chloroplasts, thick walls impervious to gaseous exchange and no intercellular spaces.
  • Compare by cutting T.S. of leaves of a C₄ plant (maize, sorghum) with a C₃ plant (wheat, rice).

Hatch and Slack pathway

  1. The primary CO₂ acceptor is a 3-carbon molecule, phosphoenol pyruvate (PEP), present in the mesophyll cells. The enzyme is PEP carboxylase (PEPcase).
  2. The mesophyll cells lack RuBisCO. The C₄ acid OAA is formed here.
  3. OAA then forms other 4-carbon acids such as malic acid or aspartic acid in the mesophyll, which are transported to the bundle sheath cells.
  4. In the bundle sheath, these C₄ acids are broken down to release CO₂ and a 3-carbon molecule.
  5. The 3-carbon molecule goes back to the mesophyll and is converted to PEP again, completing the cycle.
  6. The CO₂ released in the bundle sheath enters the C₃ (Calvin) pathway — the pathway common to all plants. Bundle sheath cells are rich in RuBisCO but lack PEPcase.
Trap
  • C₄ plants do not skip the Calvin cycle — it runs in their bundle sheath cells.
  • In C₄ plants, initial carboxylation (by PEPcase) is in the mesophyll; the Calvin cycle is in the bundle sheath.
  • PEP is 3-carbon; RuBP is 5-carbon; OAA is 4-carbon; PGA is 3-carbon.

11.9 Photorespiration

  • RuBisCO is the most abundant enzyme in the world.
  • Its active site can bind both CO₂ and O₂ — hence the name. RuBisCO has a much greater affinity for CO₂ than for O₂.
  • Binding is competitive: the relative concentration of O₂ and CO₂ decides which one binds.

In C₃ plants

Some O₂ does bind RuBisCO, so CO₂ fixation decreases. RuBP, instead of forming 2 molecules of PGA, binds O₂ to form one molecule of phosphoglycerate and one of phosphoglycolate — this pathway is photorespiration.

Carboxylation (normal)

RuBP + CO₂→2 × 3-PGA

Leads to sugar.

Oxygenation (photorespiration)

RuBP + O₂→PGA + phosphoglycolate

No sugar.

NEET Highlight In photorespiration there is neither synthesis of sugars nor of ATP or NADPH; instead it releases CO₂ with the utilisation of ATP. Hence photorespiration is a wasteful process.

In C₄ plants

  • Photorespiration does not occur in C₄ plants because they have a mechanism that increases the concentration of CO₂ at the enzyme site.
  • The C₄ acid from the mesophyll is broken down in the bundle sheath to release CO₂ → intracellular CO₂ rises → RuBisCO works as a carboxylase, minimising its oxygenase activity.
  • This is why productivity and yields are better in C₄ plants. C₄ plants also show tolerance to higher temperatures.
Trap Photorespiration is not ordinary respiration that happens in light: it produces no ATP, it uses ATP. It is a consequence of the oxygenase activity of RuBisCO.
Memory trick O₂ in, CO₂ out, ATP gone, sugar none — that is photorespiration. C₄ plants "pump" CO₂ into the bundle sheath so RuBisCO never gets a chance to grab O₂.

11.10 Factors affecting photosynthesis

The rate of photosynthesis is important in deciding the yield of plants, including crop plants. It is affected by several factors, internal and external.

Internal (plant) factors

  • Number, size, age and orientation of leaves
  • Mesophyll cells and chloroplasts
  • Internal CO₂ concentration
  • Amount of chlorophyll

Depend on genetic predisposition and growth of the plant.

External factors

  • Sunlight
  • Temperature
  • CO₂ concentration
  • Water

Several factors interact and may affect photosynthesis simultaneously.

Blackman's Law of Limiting Factors (1905)

Definition If a chemical process is affected by more than one factor, its rate is determined by the factor nearest to its minimal value: it is the factor which directly affects the process if its quantity is changed.
Example A green leaf may have optimal light and CO₂ and still not photosynthesise if the temperature is very low. Given the optimal temperature, it starts photosynthesising — temperature was the limiting factor.

1. Light

  • Consider light quality, intensity and duration.
  • There is a linear relationship between incident light and CO₂ fixation rates at low light intensities.
  • At higher intensities, the rate gradually stops increasing as other factors become limiting.
  • Light saturation occurs at 10 per cent of full sunlight. Hence, except for plants in shade or dense forests, light is rarely a limiting factor in nature.
  • Increasing incident light beyond a point causes breakdown of chlorophyll and a decrease in photosynthesis.

2. Carbon dioxide concentration

  • CO₂ is the major limiting factor for photosynthesis. Its concentration in the atmosphere is very low — between 0.03 and 0.04 per cent.
  • Raising CO₂ up to about 0.05 per cent increases CO₂ fixation; beyond this, levels become damaging over longer periods.
  • C₃ and C₄ plants respond differently: at low light, neither group responds to high CO₂; at high light, both show increased rates.
GroupResponse to rising CO₂
C₄ plantsSaturation at about 360 µL L⁻¹
C₃ plantsRespond to increased CO₂; saturation seen only beyond 450 µL L⁻¹
NEET Highlight
  • Current CO₂ availability is limiting for C₃ plants.
  • C₃ plants respond to higher CO₂ with increased photosynthesis and higher productivity — used in greenhouse crops such as tomatoes and bell pepper, grown in a CO₂-enriched atmosphere.

3. Temperature

  • The dark (biosynthetic) reactions are enzymatic and hence temperature-controlled. Light reactions are also temperature-sensitive, but to a much lesser extent.
  • C₄ plants respond to higher temperatures and have a higher temperature optimum than C₃ plants.
  • The temperature optimum depends on the plant's habitat: tropical plants have a higher optimum than plants adapted to temperate climates.

4. Water

  • Water is a reactant in the light reaction, but its effect on photosynthesis is more through its effect on the plant than directly.
  • Water stress closes the stomata, reducing CO₂ availability.
  • It also makes leaves wilt, reducing their surface area and metabolic activity.
Trap Water rarely limits photosynthesis as a raw material — only a tiny fraction of absorbed water is used. It acts mainly indirectly, via stomatal closure and wilting.
