1.1 What is “living”?

We instinctively call a dog living and a stone non-living, but listing the features that define life is harder. The usual candidates are growth, reproduction, metabolism, cellular organisation and response to the environment (consciousness). The test for each: is it shown by all living things and by no non-living thing? Only then is it a defining feature.

Read onceThe section “What is Living?” was dropped from the rationalised NCERT text (2023-24), but the NMC NEET syllabus still lists “What is living?”. Learn the logic below; it is short and conceptual.

Growth — a feature, but not defining

  • Increase in mass and increase in number of individuals are the twin characteristics of growth.
  • Multicellular organisms grow by cell division. Plants grow by cell division throughout life; animals grow only up to a certain age, after which division continues only in some tissues to replace lost cells.
  • Unicellular organisms also grow by cell division (increase in number).
  • In most higher animals and plants, growth and reproduction are separate events.
TrapNon-living things also “grow”: mountains, boulders and sand mounds increase in mass by accumulation of material on the surface. Living organisms grow from inside. Since growth is not unique to life, growth cannot be taken as a defining property of living organisms.

Reproduction — a feature, but not defining

  • Production of progeny that resemble the parents, sexually or asexually.
  • Asexual examples: spores in fungi; budding in yeast and Hydra; fragmentation in filamentous algae, fungi and the protonema of mosses; true regeneration in Planaria (a fragment re-forms the whole organism).
  • In unicellular organisms (bacteria, unicellular algae, Amoeba), reproduction is synonymous with growth — both mean an increase in number of cells.
ExceptionMany organisms do not reproduce — mules, sterile worker bees, infertile human couples — yet they are clearly living. So reproduction is not an all-inclusive defining characteristic.

1.2 The defining features of life

Metabolism

  • The sum total of all chemical reactions occurring in the body (making and breaking of molecules).
  • All living organisms — from microbes to humans — metabolise; no non-living object shows metabolism.
  • Isolated metabolic reactions can be run in vitro (in a test tube). Such reactions are not living, though they are living reactions.
Key ideaMetabolism is a defining feature of all living organisms without exception.

Cellular organisation

The body of every living organism is built of cells; the properties of life emerge from how these cells and their parts are organised. Cellular organisation of the body is a defining feature of life forms.

Consciousness / response to stimuli

  • All organisms sense their surroundings (physical, chemical or biological stimuli) and respond — from prokaryotes and unicells to plants and animals.
  • Plants respond to light, water, temperature, pollutants and other organisms.
  • Photoperiod affects reproduction in seasonal breeders, both plants and animals.
  • Human beings are the only organisms aware of themselves — self-consciousness.
DefinitionLiving organisms are self-replicating, evolving and self-regulating interactive systems capable of responding to external stimuli.
RememberNCERT poses the case of patients lying in coma, supported by machines that keep the heart and lungs working: the patient has no self-consciousness — are they living or non-living? The question shows why defining life is not straightforward.

1.3 Biodiversity and the need for names

DefinitionBiodiversity — the number and types of organisms present on earth.
  • The number of species that are known and described ranges between 1.7 and 1.8 million.
  • This number keeps rising: new organisms are continuously identified as we explore new areas — and even old ones.
  • Each organism has a local name, but local names vary from place to place, even within one country. This causes confusion in communication.
Key ideaScience needs one standard name per organism, known the same way all over the world. Providing such names is nomenclature.

Identification comes before naming

A name can be given only when the organism is described correctly and we know exactly which organism the name belongs to. Correctly recognising the organism is identification.

Characterisation→Identification→Nomenclature

NEET HighlightScientific names are based on agreed principles and criteria laid down in ICBN (plants) and ICZN (animals). Mixing up which code applies to which group is a common trap.

1.4 Binomial nomenclature

Proposed by Carolus Linnaeus. Every scientific name has two components: the generic name and the specific epithet. A name with two components = binomial nomenclature.

Rules (universally accepted)

  1. Biological names are generally in Latin and written in italics. They are Latinised or derived from Latin, irrespective of their origin.
  2. The first word is the genus; the second is the specific epithet.
  3. Both words are printed in italics; when handwritten, they are underlined separately — showing their Latin origin.
  4. The genus starts with a capital letter; the specific epithet starts with a small letter.
  5. The author’s name appears after the specific epithet, at the end, in an abbreviated form — e.g. Mangifera indica Linn. means Linnaeus first described it.
TrapIn Mangifera indica, indica alone is not the species — it is the specific epithet. The species name is the full binomial. Also, the author abbreviation is not italicised.
Memory trick“Big G, small s” — Genus gets the capital, specific epithet stays small; both slanted (italic) or underlined.

1.5 Classification, taxonomy and systematics

DefinitionClassification — the process by which anything is grouped into convenient categories based on some easily observable characters.
  • Studying every one of ~1.7–1.8 million organisms individually is impossible, so we classify them into groups — the need for classification.
  • A scientific term for such a category is a taxon (plural taxa). Taxa can be at very different levels: plants, animals, mammals, dogs, cats, wheat, rice are all taxa.
  • Humans have classified organisms since early times, mainly by their uses (food, clothing, shelter). The earliest classifications were based on the “uses” of organisms.

Taxonomy

Classifying organisms into categories based on characteristics, then naming them, is taxonomy.

Characterisation→Identification→Classification→Nomenclature

Characterisation, identification, classification and nomenclature are the processes basic to taxonomy.

  • Earlier taxonomy: mainly external structure.
  • Modern taxonomy also uses internal structure, cell structure, development process and ecological information.

Systematics

  • From Latin systema = systematic arrangement of organisms.
  • Linnaeus titled his publication Systema Naturae.
  • Scope: identification, nomenclature and classification — and it also takes into account the evolutionary relationships between organisms.

Taxonomy

  • Characterise, identify, classify, name
  • Based on observable characters

Systematics

  • All of taxonomy
  • + evolutionary relationships
  • Wider in scope

1.7 Taxonomic categories: species and genus

  • Classification is not a single step; it is a hierarchy of steps. Each step is a rank or category.
  • Each category is a taxonomic category; all of them together form the taxonomic hierarchy.
  • Each category, as a unit of classification, represents a rank and is commonly called a taxon.
NEET HighlightTaxonomic categories are distinct biological entities, not merely morphological aggregates.

Species — the lowest category

1.8 Family, order, class, phylum, kingdom

Family

A group of related genera, with still fewer similarities than genus and species. Plant families are characterised on both vegetative and reproductive features.

  • Solanum, Petunia, Datura → family Solanaceae.
  • Panthera (lion, tiger, leopard) + Felis (cats) → family Felidae.
  • Cat and dog differ enough to be in separate families: Felidae and Canidae.

Order

An assemblage of families showing a few similar characters; the similar characters are fewer than among genera of a family.

  • Plant families Convolvulaceae and Solanaceae → order Polymoniales, mainly on floral characters.
  • Animal order Carnivora includes Felidae and Canidae.

Class

Includes related orders. Order Primata (monkey, gorilla, gibbon) and order Carnivora (tiger, cat, dog) are both placed in class Mammalia, which has other orders too.

Phylum / Division

  • Classes of fishes, amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals share a notochord and a dorsal hollow neural system → phylum Chordata.
  • In plants, classes with a few similar characters form a higher category called Division (equivalent of phylum).

Kingdom

All animals of the various phyla → Kingdom Animalia; all plants of the various divisions → Kingdom Plantae. Kingdom is the highest category.

Memory trickAscending order: Species → Genus → Family → Order → Class → Phylum → Kingdom — “Some Good Fruits Often Cause Painful Kidneys.”

1.9 Taxonomic hierarchy at a glance

Species→Genus→Family→Order→Class→Phylum / Division→Kingdom
NEET Highlight
  • As we go higher from species to kingdom, the number of common characteristics goes on decreasing.
  • The lower the taxon, the more characteristics its members share.
  • The higher the category, the greater the difficulty of working out its relationship to other taxa at the same level.

Taxonomists have also created sub-categories within this hierarchy for a more sound and scientific placement of taxa.

1.11 Taxonomical aids

Read onceThe “Taxonomical Aids” section (herbarium, botanical gardens, museum, zoological parks, key) was removed from the rationalised NCERT text and is not named in the NMC syllabus. A quick look is enough.
  • Herbarium — a store-house of plant specimens that are dried, pressed and preserved on sheets, arranged by an accepted classification system; sheets carry a label with name, date and place of collection, collector’s name, etc.
  • Botanical gardens — collections of living plants for reference; each plant labelled with its botanical name and family. Examples: Royal Botanical Garden, Kew (England); Indian Botanical Garden, Howrah; National Botanical Research Institute, Lucknow.
  • Museum — preserved plant and animal specimens (in jars/containers with preservative), dried specimens, insect boxes, stuffed animals and skeletons.
  • Zoological parks — wild animals kept in protected environments, close to their natural habitats, to study food habits and behaviour.
  • Key — a tool for identifying plants and animals on the basis of similarities and dissimilarities, using couplets of contrasting characters (each statement is a lead); separate keys are needed for each taxonomic category.
